# Gross Profit Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The gross profit ratio measures how much of each sales dollar is left after the direct cost of making the product. That direct cost, called cost of goods sold, covers the materials and labor that go straight into the product. What remains is gross profit, the money available to cover everything else and still leave a profit. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes the basic pricing power of the whole area of the economy.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a whole part of the economy sells at prices well above its direct costs, which is an early sign of how strong the sector's businesses are.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A sector of differentiated products with real pricing power. |
| Around the market average | A balanced sector with a mix of strong and weak pricing. |
| Persistently low | A commodity or fiercely competitive sector with little pricing power. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Gross Profit Ratio = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue`

Revenue is total sales. Cost of goods sold is the direct cost of making what was sold. The result is shown as a percentage. The sector figure combines gross profit and revenue across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's sales dollars actually keep their margin, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

A sector's gross profit ratio blends the margins of the industries inside it. A sector full of differentiated, high-margin industries will show a high figure. A sector full of commodity industries that compete on price will show a low one. The sector number is therefore a weighted average of very different cost structures.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Gross margin is the first read on a sector's pricing power. A sector that sells far above its direct costs has products people value and rivals cannot easily copy, which is the foundation of lasting profit. A sector that sells barely above its direct costs competes on price alone, which keeps profits thin no matter how well the businesses are run.

The sector figure also sets the ceiling for every profit measure below it. Operating profit and net profit are what survive after more costs are subtracted from gross profit, so a sector with a thin gross margin has little room to absorb other costs. Reading the sector's gross margin first tells the reader how much cushion the sector has before any other expense is counted. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix and the pricing power inside it. A sector weighted toward differentiated products will earn a high gross margin, while a sector weighted toward commodity goods will earn a low one. Because pricing power changes slowly, a sector's gross margin tends to be persistent.

Input costs move the figure year to year. When the prices of raw materials and labor rise, a sector that cannot pass the cost on to customers will see its gross margin shrink. A sector that can raise its own prices in step will hold the margin steady, which is itself a sign of pricing power. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors. A sector well above the others marks an area built on differentiated products and pricing power. A sector well below the others marks a commodity area where competition keeps margins thin.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High and steady | A sector of differentiated products with durable pricing power. |
| High but falling | A sector losing pricing power as competition or input costs rise. |
| Persistently low | A commodity sector competing on price with little cushion. |

Adjust for cost structure before judging. Some sectors carry heavy costs outside cost of goods sold, such as research or marketing, so a high gross margin does not always survive to the bottom line. A sector should be judged on gross margin alongside the margins further down.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate gross profit ratio over several years. A high and steady line marks a sector with durable pricing power. A line that drifts down marks a sector losing its pricing power, often to rising competition or input costs, which is one of the earliest warnings a sector is weakening.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip during a spike in raw material prices that recovers when costs ease is a cyclical move, not a real loss of pricing power. A slow decline that does not recover points to a permanent change, such as new rivals or substitutes eroding the sector's pricing. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The gross profit ratio shows the margin after direct costs, and the operating income ratio shows the margin after the costs of running the business are also subtracted. The gap between them is the weight of the sector's overhead, research, and selling costs. Reading the two together shows how much of the sector's pricing power survives the cost of operating.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a high gross margin and a high operating margin keeps its pricing power all the way down, the mark of a strong sector. A high gross margin with a thin operating margin warns that heavy overhead is consuming the advantage. The size of the gap is a fast read on how lean the sector's businesses are.

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** The gross profit ratio shows the quality of each sale, and revenue growth shows how fast the sector is making more sales. A sector can grow fast while its margin erodes, or hold a high margin while it barely grows. Looking at both shows whether the sector is expanding on healthy terms.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector growing its sales while holding a high gross margin is expanding without sacrificing pricing power, the strongest mix. Rapid growth with a falling margin warns that the sector is buying growth by cutting prices. A high margin with little growth describes a strong but mature sector.

## Part 8: Limitations

The gross profit ratio ignores every cost below the direct cost of the product. A sector with a high gross margin can still earn little once overhead and interest are counted. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single margin number hides wide differences between the strong and weak industries inside it.

Companies inside a sector may divide costs between cost of goods sold and other lines differently, which makes raw cross-sector comparison imperfect. The figure also looks backward. A sector can hold a high gross margin right up to a new rival or substitute that breaks its pricing power, so a strong margin shows where pricing power has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's gross profit ratio above or below the other sectors, and how durable does the pricing power look?
2. Is the high margin spread across the sector's industries, or driven by one or two dominant industries or companies?
3. How much of the gross margin survives to the operating and net margins, and how heavy is the sector's overhead?
4. Has the margin held steady through swings in raw material and labor costs, or does it fall when input costs rise?
5. Is any decline a cyclical move from costs, or a structural loss of pricing power to competition or substitutes?
6. Is the sector growing its sales while holding its margin, or buying growth by cutting prices?
7. Which industries inside the sector are pulling the margin up, and which are dragging it down?
8. Does the sector's pricing power justify its current standing with investors?
