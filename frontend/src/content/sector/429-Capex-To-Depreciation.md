# Capex To Depreciation

## Part 1: Definition

Capex to depreciation compares how much a business spends on long-lived assets against how fast those assets wear out. Capital spending, the capex in the name, is the money laid out on plant, equipment, and other long-lived assets. Depreciation is the yearly accounting charge that records the wearing out of assets the business already owns. The ratio divides the first by the second, so it shows whether a business is spending more than it needs just to replace what is aging, or letting its asset base run down. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as industrials or technology, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes the investing pace of the whole area of the economy.

A reading above one means the sector is spending more than its assets are wearing out, which signals investment beyond mere maintenance and a growing asset base. A reading below one means the sector is spending less than its assets are aging, which signals an asset base that is shrinking or being harvested for cash. The figure is a fast read on whether a sector is building for the future or living off the past.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Well above one | The sector is investing for growth, expanding its asset base. |
| Around one | The sector is spending roughly enough to maintain its assets. |
| Below one | The sector is under-investing, letting its asset base run down. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Capex To Depreciation = Capital Expenditures ÷ Depreciation`

Capital expenditures are the cash spent on long-lived assets during the year. Depreciation is the charge that spreads the cost of existing assets over their useful lives. A reading of one and a half means the sector spends one and a half dollars on assets for every dollar of wear recorded. The sector figure combines capital spending and depreciation across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's investing actually sits.

A sector's capex to depreciation blends the investing habits of the industries inside it. A sector full of expanding, capital-heavy industries that are building new capacity shows a high reading. A sector of mature industries that spend only enough to replace aging assets shows a reading near one, and a sector harvesting cash from a shrinking asset base shows a reading below one. The sector number is a weighted average across very different stages of investment.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Capex to depreciation reveals where a sector sits in its investment life. A sector spending well above its depreciation is building capacity, betting on future demand, and growing its asset base. A sector spending near its depreciation is in steady state, replacing what wears out. A sector spending below its depreciation is shrinking, choosing to harvest cash rather than reinvest. This life-stage read shapes how a top-down investor thinks about the sector's growth and its cash.

The figure also flags a tension between growth and cash. Heavy investment today builds future earning power, but it consumes cash now, so a high-spending sector grows at the cost of near-term cash. A sector spending below depreciation frees up cash now, but it risks an aging asset base that will eventually need a catch-up spend. Reading the investing pace helps the reader judge whether a sector is sowing for growth or quietly running down its base, which sets the context for the wider decision on where to commit capital.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by where its industries stand in their investment cycle. A sector weighted toward young, growing industries building out capacity shows a high reading, while a sector of mature industries shows a reading near one. A sector in decline, harvesting its assets, shows a reading below one. Because investment cycles move slowly, the reading tends to be persistent for a given stage.

The economic cycle moves the figure year to year, because sectors cut capital spending in downturns to conserve cash and ramp it up when confidence returns. A wave of new technology can lift the reading across a sector as companies rush to build capacity for a new demand. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the reading can really describe a handful of giants building or pausing. Depreciation reflects past spending and asset lives, so a sector that invested heavily years ago carries high depreciation that lowers the ratio even at a normal spending pace.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors to separate the builders from the harvesters. A sector spending well above depreciation is investing for growth, while a sector spending below it is winding down or returning cash.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Well above one | A growth sector building capacity and betting on future demand. |
| Around one | A mature sector spending just enough to maintain its assets. |
| Below one | A declining or cash-returning sector letting its asset base age. |

Adjust for life stage before judging. A high reading in a young sector is healthy investment, while the same reading in a mature sector may signal overbuilding into weak demand. A reading below one is normal for an asset-light sector that needs little physical investment, so it carries a very different meaning there than in a heavy industry that must keep rebuilding its plant.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate capex to depreciation over several years. A reading that sits well above one for years marks a sector in a sustained build-out. A reading hovering near one marks a mature sector in steady state. A reading drifting below one marks a sector winding down its investment.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A reading that dips in a downturn and recovers as confidence returns is a cyclical pause in spending, not a lasting change. A reading that climbs and stays high points to a structural investment wave, often around new technology or demand. A reading that falls below one and stays there points to a sector in decline or one shifting toward an asset-light model. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to tell a temporary spending cut from a permanent change in the sector's appetite to invest.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Capital Intensity Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Capital intensity shows how asset-heavy a sector is, and capex to depreciation shows whether that asset base is growing, holding, or shrinking. A capital-heavy sector must keep spending just to stand still, so its investing pace matters far more than in an asset-light sector. Reading the two together shows both the weight of the sector's asset base and the direction it is heading.

**What the combination tells you.** A capital-heavy sector spending well above depreciation is building a large and growing asset base, which consumes cash for years before it pays off. A capital-heavy sector spending below depreciation is letting a large base age, which frees cash now but stores up a future catch-up spend. The pairing shows whether a heavy sector is investing responsibly in its base or quietly running it down.

### FCF To Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Capital spending is the main thing standing between a sector's sales and its free cash flow, and capex to depreciation shows whether that spending is high or low against the asset base. A sector spending well above depreciation will show weaker free cash flow while it builds. Reading the two together explains why a sector's cash conversion is strong or strained.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with high capex to depreciation and thin free cash flow is investing for growth, so its weak cash is a choice rather than a flaw. A sector with low capex to depreciation and strong free cash flow is harvesting cash from a base it is no longer growing. The pairing shows whether thin cash generation reflects investment in the future or a sector being milked for cash today.

## Part 8: Limitations

Capital spending is lumpy, so a single year can show a high or low reading for reasons of timing rather than strategy, as one large project can swing the figure. Depreciation is an accounting charge based on past spending and assumed asset lives, so it can drift far from the real wear on assets, which distorts the ratio. A sector can prop up the reading with spending that is wasteful, since the ratio measures how much is spent, not how well.

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single reading hides wide differences between the building and harvesting industries inside it. The figure is least useful for asset-light sectors, where physical investment is small and the ratio swings on tiny numbers. It also looks backward, so a high reading shows where investment has been, not whether that investment will pay off.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector spending above or below its depreciation, and what does that say about its investment life stage?
2. Is a high reading healthy growth investment, or overbuilding into weak demand?
3. Is a reading below one a sign of decline, or normal for an asset-light sector that needs little physical investment?
4. Is a change in the reading a cyclical pause in spending, or a structural shift in the sector's appetite to invest?
5. How does the sector's investing pace square with its capital intensity, and can a heavy base afford the spend?
6. Does heavy spending explain weak free cash flow, so the thin cash is a growth choice rather than a flaw?
7. Which industries inside the sector are building capacity, and which are harvesting their asset base?
8. Is the sector's high spending likely to earn a return, or is it spending for the sake of spending?
