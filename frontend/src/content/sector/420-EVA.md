# EVA

## Part 1: Definition

Economic value added measures the dollar profit a sector earns above the cost of the capital it uses. Capital is the money put into the businesses, counting both owner money and borrowed money. Every dollar of that capital has a cost, because lenders charge interest and owners expect a return. Economic value added, often shortened to EVA, is the profit left after that full cost of capital is subtracted. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes whether the whole area of the economy creates value or destroys it.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers a simple question: after paying for every dollar of capital it uses, does the sector still earn a surplus? A positive figure means the sector builds wealth. A negative figure means the sector destroys wealth even when it reports an accounting profit.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Strongly positive | A sector that earns far above its cost of capital, often asset-light with durable advantages. |
| Near zero | A sector that roughly covers its capital cost and creates little extra value. |
| Negative | A capital-heavy or fiercely competitive sector that earns a profit but still falls short of its capital cost. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`EVA = NOPAT − (Invested Capital × WACC)`

Net operating profit after tax, called NOPAT, is the profit from operations after tax but before interest, so it counts the return to both owners and lenders. Invested capital is the money in the business, the book value of debt plus equity. The weighted average cost of capital, called WACC, is the blended yearly cost of that capital. Multiplying invested capital by WACC gives the capital charge, which is the dollar return the sector must earn just to break even. The sector figure combines profit, capital, and the capital charge across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most.

Economic value added is a dollar amount, not a ratio. A large sector can post a large figure simply because it is large, so the raw number is most useful when read against the sector's size or alongside the percentage version. The sector number is a weighted blend of value-creating and value-destroying industries, so it reflects the mix inside the sector.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Economic value added is the clearest test of whether a sector creates wealth. Profit alone can mislead, because a sector can report billions in earnings and still fall short of what its capital cost. By subtracting the full capital charge, the figure shows the true surplus. A sector with a positive figure for years rewards the capital invested in it, which is the surest sign of a structurally strong area of the economy.

The figure also frames the highest-level investment decision. Capital flows toward sectors that create value and away from sectors that destroy it, so a sector earning above its cost of capital tends to attract investment and support higher stock prices. A sector that earns a profit but destroys value on this measure is quietly weaker than its earnings suggest. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector figure is driven first by its industry mix. A sector weighted toward capital-light industries with pricing power earns far above its capital cost and posts a strong figure. A sector weighted toward capital-heavy industries that need constant spending on plant and equipment carries a large capital charge, which pulls the figure down. Because the mix changes slowly, a sector's economic value added tends to be persistent.

Two moving parts swing the figure year to year. The first is operating profit, which rises and falls with the economic cycle. The second is the cost of capital, which climbs when interest rates rise. A jump in rates lifts the capital charge across the whole sector and can turn a positive figure negative without any change in the underlying businesses. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors, scaled for size. A sector strongly positive for years marks a value-creating area of the economy. A sector near zero or negative marks one where capital struggles to earn its keep, even if it reports healthy profits.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Strongly positive, steady | A high-quality sector with durable advantages that builds wealth through the cycle. |
| Positive but swinging | A cyclical sector that creates value at the peak and destroys it in downturns. |
| Negative for years | A capital-heavy or competitive sector that earns a profit but cannot cover its capital cost. |

Scale for size before judging, because a large sector can post a large figure for size reasons alone. The percentage version, economic value added divided by revenue, makes the cross-sector comparison fair. Adjust for structure too, since sectors built on lending measure capital differently and belong in a separate comparison.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate economic value added over several years. A figure that stays positive and grows marks a durably value-creating sector. A figure that drifts toward zero marks a sector losing its edge, often as competition catches up or as the cost of capital rises against it.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip into negative territory during a downturn that recovers as the economy recovers is a cyclical low, often a chance to favor the sector before the rebound. A slow slide that does not recover points to a permanent change, such as a sector growing more capital-heavy or losing ground to a newer part of the economy. A rate-driven dip, where rising interest costs lift the capital charge, is a third pattern worth separating, because it reflects financing conditions rather than the businesses themselves. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** Economic value added is the surplus profit left after the capital charge, and the weighted average cost of capital sets that charge. A rise in the cost of capital lifts the charge across the whole sector and shrinks the surplus, even when operating profit holds steady. Reading the two together shows how much of a change in the sector's value creation comes from the businesses and how much comes from the cost of money.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector that holds a positive figure even as its cost of capital rises has businesses strong enough to absorb a higher hurdle, which marks real quality. A sector whose figure turns negative the moment rates climb was only creating value on cheap financing. The pairing shows how durable the sector's value creation is when money grows expensive.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is the percentage return the sector earns on all its capital, and economic value added is the dollar surplus that same return produces above the capital cost. The percentage shows the rate of value creation, while the dollar figure shows the scale of it. Reading the two together separates how well the sector earns from how much it earns.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a high return on invested capital and a strongly positive economic value added creates value at both a high rate and a large scale, the best mix. A sector with a positive return on invested capital but a thin or negative economic value added earns less than its capital cost, which the percentage alone can hide. The gap between the two is a fast read on whether the sector clears its hurdle.

## Part 8: Limitations

Economic value added is a dollar figure, so a large sector can post a large number simply because it is large, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without scaling for size. The figure leans on the cost of capital, which is an estimate, so a small change in that estimate can flip the sign of the figure. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole.

The figure rests on the book value of capital, which can sit far from current value, especially in older capital-heavy sectors where assets were bought long ago. A sector blends many different industries, so a single number hides wide differences between the value-creating and value-destroying industries inside it. The figure also looks backward. A sector can create value right up to a shift in technology, competition, or interest rates that changes its economics, so a strong number shows where value has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's economic value added positive, and how does it rank against the other sectors once scaled for size?
2. Does the surplus come from the businesses, or would it vanish if the cost of capital rose a little?
3. Is the value creation spread across the sector's industries, or driven by one or two dominant industries or companies?
4. How much does the figure swing with the economic cycle, and where in the cycle is it now?
5. Is any decline a cyclical dip that recovers, a rate-driven squeeze from rising capital costs, or a structural slide as the sector loses its edge?
6. Does the sector clear its hurdle on return on invested capital as well as on the dollar figure?
7. Which industries inside the sector are creating value, and which are destroying it?
8. Does the sector's value creation justify its current standing with investors?
