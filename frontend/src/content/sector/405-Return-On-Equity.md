# Return On Equity

## Part 1: Definition

Return on equity measures how much yearly profit is earned for each dollar of shareholder money. Shareholder money, called equity, is what owners keep after debts are subtracted from what the companies own. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how profitably the entire sector uses the money its owners have invested.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a whole area of the economy rewards owners well, which is the question an investor asks when deciding where to put money before narrowing down to an industry or a company.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A sector full of profitable industries, often light on hard assets and strong in pricing power. |
| Around the market average | A balanced sector with a mix of strong and weak industries. |
| Persistently low | A capital-heavy or fiercely competitive sector where returns stay thin. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity`

Net income is profit after all costs. Shareholders' equity is the owners' stake. The sector figure combines profit and equity across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's capital actually earns its return, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

A sector's return on equity reflects the mix of industries inside it. A sector dominated by capital-light, high-margin industries will show a high figure. A sector dominated by capital-heavy industries that require constant spending on plant and equipment will show a lower one. The sector number is therefore a weighted blend of very different business models.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Sector profitability guides the highest-level investment decision: which broad part of the economy to favor. Capital flows toward sectors that reward owners and away from sectors that do not, so a sector that earns a high return on equity for years tends to attract investment and support higher stock prices. A sector stuck at low returns tends to stay out of favor.

The sector figure also frames the choice between offense and defense. Some sectors earn steady returns through good times and bad, while others swing hard with the economy. Knowing a sector's typical profitability and how much it moves with the cycle helps the reader decide whether the sector fits a search for stability or a search for recovery upside. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix. A sector weighted toward profitable, asset-light industries will earn a high return, while a sector weighted toward heavy, cyclical industries will earn less. Because the mix changes slowly, a sector's profitability tends to be persistent.

The economic cycle moves the figure year to year, and the size of the swing depends on how sensitive the sector is to the economy. Sectors tied to consumer spending and raw materials swing hard, while sectors tied to necessities stay steady. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants. Broad waves of borrowing or buybacks across the sector can also lift the figure without any real improvement in the underlying businesses.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors and the market. A sector well above the market for years marks a structurally profitable area of the economy. A sector below the market for years marks one where capital struggles to earn its keep.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Above the market, steady | A high-quality sector, usually asset-light with strong pricing power. |
| Above the market but swinging hard | A cyclical sector that earns well at the peak and poorly in downturns. |
| Below the market for years | A capital-heavy or commodity sector where competition keeps returns thin. |

Adjust for structure before judging. Sectors built on lending, such as financials, run on borrowing and will show high returns for structural reasons, so they belong in a separate comparison from asset-light sectors.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate return on equity over several years. A high and steady line marks a durably profitable sector. A line that swings widely with the economy marks a cyclical sector whose profitability depends on the phase of the cycle, which matters for timing the move in and out of the sector.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A deep dip that recovers as the economy recovers is a cyclical low, often a chance to favor the sector before the rebound. A slow decline that does not recover points to a permanent change, such as a sector losing ground to a newer part of the economy. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** A sector's return on equity can be lifted by heavy borrowing across its companies, especially in sectors that lean on debt. Return on invested capital measures profit against all the capital in the sector, so borrowing cannot inflate it. Reading the two together separates a sector's real profitability from a leverage effect.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector strong on both earns its return through genuine business quality and is more dependable. A sector strong on return on equity but weaker on return on invested capital depends on debt, which makes the sector more vulnerable when financing conditions tighten. The gap between the two is a fast read on how much of the sector's strength is real versus borrowed.

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity shows how profitable the sector is, and revenue growth shows how fast it is expanding. A sector can be highly profitable yet mature and slow, or fast-growing yet unprofitable. Looking at both locates the sector on the map of long-term attractiveness.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector that is both profitable and growing is the most attractive place to commit capital for the long run. High profitability with little growth describes a mature sector that returns cash rather than reinvesting it. Rapid growth with weak profitability warns that competition inside the sector is consuming the rewards of expansion.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single sector number hides wide differences between the strong and weak industries inside it. Sectors built on borrowing will always show high returns for structural reasons, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment.

The figure looks backward. A sector can earn high returns right up to a shift in technology, regulation, or consumer behavior that changes its economics. A strong sector number shows where profits have been, not where they are going, so it must be paired with a view on how the sector is changing.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's return on equity above or below the market, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Does the sector's strength hold up under return on invested capital, or does it rely on heavy borrowing?
3. Is the high figure spread across the sector's industries, or driven by one or two dominant industries or companies?
4. How much does the sector's return swing with the economic cycle, and where in the cycle is it now?
5. Is the trend a cyclical swing that recovers, or a structural decline as the sector loses ground to newer parts of the economy?
6. Is the sector both profitable and growing, or profitable but mature?
7. Which industries inside the sector are pulling the figure up, and which are dragging it down?
8. Is the sector currently in or out of favor with investors, and does the profitability justify that standing?
