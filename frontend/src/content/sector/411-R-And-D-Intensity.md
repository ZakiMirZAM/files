# R&D Intensity

## Part 1: Definition

R&D intensity measures how much of a company's sales it spends on research and development. Research and development, called R&D, is the money spent inventing new products and improving existing ones. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or healthcare, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how much of the whole sector's revenue goes into innovation.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a broad area of the economy competes on invention and new products, or on price, scale, and steady operations. This is a core part of judging the long-run growth character of a sector before narrowing to an industry or a company.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High | A sector that competes on innovation, pouring a large share of sales into new products. |
| Moderate | A sector that innovates steadily but also competes on cost and scale. |
| Low | A sector that competes on price, scale, or stable operations rather than invention. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`R&D Intensity = Research and Development Expense ÷ Revenue`

Research and development expense is the yearly spending on inventing and improving products, reported on the income statement. Revenue is total sales. The sector figure adds R&D spending and revenue across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's real research effort sits, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

A sector's R&D intensity reflects the mix of industries inside it. A sector dominated by software, pharmaceuticals, or advanced hardware will show a high figure because invention is the basis of competition. A sector dominated by utilities, retail, or basic materials will show a low one because its products change slowly. The sector number is therefore a weighted blend of very different competitive models.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

R&D intensity reveals how a sector competes and where its future growth will come from. A high-intensity sector grows by inventing the next product and tends to enjoy stronger long-run expansion, while a low-intensity sector grows mainly with the economy and competes on price or scale. This shapes the highest-level decision of which broad part of the economy is positioned to lead over the coming years.

The figure also signals how much of a sector's reported profit is being plowed back into the future. R&D is an expense that lowers today's profit but builds tomorrow's products, so a sector that spends heavily is sacrificing near-term margin for long-term position. Cutting R&D can lift profits briefly while quietly weakening a sector's competitive standing. Knowing a sector's research intensity helps the reader judge whether its profits are sustainable or borrowed from the future. This read sets context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix. A sector weighted toward technology and healthcare will spend heavily on research, while a sector weighted toward utilities or consumer staples will spend little. Because the mix changes slowly, a sector's research intensity tends to be persistent and is one of its most stable traits.

Spending also moves with the cycle and with competition. In good times sectors fund more research, and in downturns some trim it to protect profit, which can dent future products. When competition heats up, an entire sector may raise spending to keep pace, lifting the figure without any one company pulling ahead. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe the research budgets of a few giants. Accounting choices matter too, because some research is expensed at once while related spending may sit elsewhere, which can understate the true effort.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors, because it sorts the economy into innovation-led and operations-led groups. A sector with high intensity competes on new products and tends to grow faster. A sector with low intensity competes on price and scale and tends to grow with the broad economy.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High and stable | An innovation-led sector built on invention, usually with strong long-run growth. |
| Moderate | A sector that blends steady innovation with cost and scale competition. |
| Low | An operations-led sector that competes on price and grows with the economy. |

Adjust for context before judging. A high figure is a strength only if the spending produces products that sell, because research that fails to translate into revenue is wasted, so intensity must be read alongside how fast the sector actually grows.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate R&D intensity over several years. A high and steady line marks a durably innovation-led sector. A line that climbs over time marks a sector raising its bet on invention, often as competition intensifies or new technology opens fresh ground.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip during a downturn that recovers afterward is a cyclical pullback, where companies trimmed research to protect short-term profit. A slow, steady decline that does not recover can be an early warning that a sector is underinvesting and ceding ground to more aggressive rivals, a pattern that shows up in falling sales and lost share years later. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** R&D intensity shows how much a sector invests in invention, and revenue growth shows whether that investment is paying off. Research is only worthwhile if it produces products that sell, so reading the two together at the sector level tests whether a sector's spending is translating into expansion or being wasted.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with high research intensity and strong revenue growth is converting invention into sales, which is the most attractive long-run profile. A sector that spends heavily but grows slowly is struggling to turn research into products people want, a warning that the spending is not earning its keep. The pairing separates productive innovation from expensive innovation that fails to land.

### Gross Profit Ratio

**Why read the two together.** R&D intensity shows how much a sector spends to invent, and the gross profit ratio shows how much it earns on each sale before that spending. Heavy research usually pays off in pricing power, because successful new products command higher margins. Reading the two together at the sector level shows whether a sector's invention is producing the premium it should.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with high research intensity and high gross margins is inventing products that customers pay a premium for, which is the hallmark of a strong innovation-led sector. A sector that spends heavily on research yet earns thin margins is not getting pricing power for its effort, which questions whether the spending creates real advantage. The pairing links a sector's research effort to the profitability it is meant to produce.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe the research budgets of those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many industries with very different innovation needs, so a single number hides wide differences between research-heavy and operations-led industries inside it. Spending also says nothing about quality, because a sector can spend heavily on research that never produces a successful product.

The figure depends on accounting choices. Some research is expensed at once while related effort may be recorded elsewhere or omitted, so the reported number can understate or overstate the true commitment, and the treatment differs across industries. A high figure shows how much a sector spends on invention, not how well that spending will convert into future products and profit.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's R&D intensity above or below the market, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Does the sector compete on invention and new products, or on price, scale, and steady operations?
3. Is the heavy spending translating into revenue growth, or is it being wasted?
4. Is the figure spread across the sector's industries, or driven by a few research-heavy giants?
5. Is a rising trend a sign of fresh opportunity, or of a costly arms race that lifts spending without advantage?
6. Is a falling trend a cyclical pullback, or a structural retreat that will cost the sector ground later?
7. Which industries inside the sector are carrying the research effort, and which spend little?
8. Is the sector positioned to lead future growth through innovation, or is it living off products that may soon age?
