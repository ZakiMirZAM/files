# Excess Return (ROE − Ke)

## Part 1: Definition

Excess return measures how far a sector's return on owner money beats the return its owners require. It takes the return on equity, the yearly profit earned for each dollar of shareholder money, and subtracts the cost of equity, the return owners demand for the risk of holding the shares. The result is the surplus left for owners after they are paid for their risk. A positive figure means the sector rewards its owners beyond what they require. A negative figure means it underpays them. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes whether the whole area of the economy creates value for its owners.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It is the equity value-creation test: it answers, in one number, whether a part of the economy earns more on owner money than owners demand. A sector that clears the bar builds wealth for its owners. A sector that falls short erodes it, even while reporting a profit.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Strongly positive | A sector that rewards owners well beyond their required return, often asset-light with durable advantages. |
| Near zero | A sector that roughly meets the return owners require and creates little extra for them. |
| Negative | A capital-heavy or competitive sector that earns a profit but underpays its owners for the risk. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Excess Return = ROE − Ke`

Return on equity is net income divided by shareholders' equity, the profit earned on owner money. The cost of equity, written as Ke, is the return owners require, usually estimated from the capital asset pricing model, which adds a risk premium to a safe base rate. Subtracting the second from the first gives the spread between what the sector earns for owners and what owners demand. The sector figure combines the returns and the hurdles across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most.

Excess return is the clearest single read on equity value creation because it puts the return and its hurdle in the same number. A sector can post a high return on equity yet still show a thin or negative excess return if its owners demand even more because of risk. The sector number is a weighted blend of value-creating and value-destroying industries, so it reflects the mix inside the sector.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Excess return answers the question a raw return on equity cannot: is the return actually enough? A high return on equity looks impressive, but if owners demand even more because the sector is risky, the sector still underpays them. By subtracting the hurdle, excess return turns the return on equity into a verdict on whether the sector builds wealth for its owners. A sector with a positive figure for years is a genuine value creator on the equity its owners provide.

The figure also frames a top-level investment decision. Capital flows toward sectors that reward owners beyond their required return and away from those that do not, so a sector with a wide positive spread tends to attract investment and support higher valuations. A sector with a negative spread is quietly weaker than its profits suggest, because it earns money while still falling short of what its owners need. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector figure is driven by both sides of the spread. On the return side, the industry mix and competitive strength set how much the sector earns on owner money, with asset-light, advantaged industries earning the most. On the hurdle side, risk sets how much owners demand, with volatile sectors facing a higher bar. A sector can lift its excess return by earning more or by being less risky, and the figure tends to be persistent because both traits change slowly.

The economic cycle moves the return side year to year, since profits rise and fall with the economy. Interest rates and market fear move the hurdle side, since both lift the cost of equity for every sector at once. A rise in rates can turn a positive spread negative without any change in the businesses, simply because owners now require more. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants. Heavy borrowing across the sector can inflate the return on equity, which widens the spread without genuine improvement.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors, because it already accounts for risk on both sides. A sector with a wide positive spread for years rewards its owners well above their required return. A sector with a negative spread underpays them, even if its raw return on equity looks healthy.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Wide and positive, steady | A high-quality sector that rewards owners well above their required return through the cycle. |
| Positive but swinging | A cyclical sector whose spread is wide at the peak and negative in downturns. |
| Negative for years | A capital-heavy or competitive sector that earns a profit but consistently underpays its owners. |

Excess return is fairer than raw return on equity for cross-sector comparison, because a risky sector with a high return on equity may show a smaller spread than a steady sector with a lower one. Adjust for structure too, since sectors built on lending earn high returns on equity for structural reasons and belong in a separate comparison.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate excess return over several years. A spread that stays wide and positive marks a durably value-creating sector. A spread that narrows toward zero marks a sector losing its edge, either because returns are falling or because the required hurdle is rising against it.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A spread that dips negative in a downturn and recovers as the economy recovers is a cyclical low, often a chance to favor the sector before the rebound. A slow narrowing that does not recover points to a permanent change, such as competition competing the return away. A rate-driven squeeze, where a rising cost of equity narrows the spread even as returns hold, is a third pattern worth separating, because it reflects financing conditions rather than the businesses. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is the raw profit a sector earns on owner money, and excess return is that same figure after subtracting what owners require. Return on equity shows the level of profitability, while excess return shows whether that level clears the bar. Reading the two together separates a high return that genuinely rewards owners from a high return that risk has already spoken for.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a high return on equity and a wide excess return rewards its owners both generously and beyond their required return, the best mix. A sector with a high return on equity but a thin excess return is risky enough that owners demand most of the return back, which the raw figure hides. The gap between the two is the cost of equity, and it shows how much of the return is true reward.

### Cost Of Equity

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity is the hurdle owners require, and excess return is the return on equity minus exactly that hurdle. Reading them together shows whether a wide or narrow spread comes from the return side or the hurdle side. A spread can narrow because returns fell or because the required hurdle rose, and only the pairing tells which.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a positive excess return despite a high cost of equity earns enough to clear a demanding bar, which marks real strength. A sector with a negative excess return despite a low cost of equity underpays its owners even on an easy bar, a clear warning. The pairing locates the source of value creation or its absence.

## Part 8: Limitations

Excess return leans on the cost of equity, which is an estimate built on a model of risk that markets do not always follow. A small change in that hurdle can flip the spread from positive to negative, so the verdict is only as reliable as the hurdle behind it. Return on equity, the other half, can be inflated by heavy borrowing, so a wide spread can rest on leverage rather than business quality.

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single spread hides wide differences between the value-creating and value-destroying industries inside it. Sectors built on lending earn high returns on equity for structural reasons, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment. The figure also looks backward, so a wide spread shows where value has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's excess return positive, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Does a wide spread come from a high return on equity, a low required hurdle, or both?
3. Is the return on equity behind the spread genuine, or inflated by heavy borrowing across the sector?
4. Would the spread survive a rise in the cost of equity from higher interest rates or greater market fear?
5. Is the value creation spread across the sector's industries, or driven by one or two dominant industries or companies?
6. Is any narrowing a cyclical dip that recovers, a rate-driven squeeze, or a structural slide as competition erodes returns?
7. Which industries inside the sector are widening the spread, and which are dragging it negative?
8. Does the sector's equity value creation justify its current standing with investors?
