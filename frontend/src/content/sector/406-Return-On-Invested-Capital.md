# Return On Invested Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Return on invested capital measures how much operating profit a sector earns for each dollar of capital put into its businesses. Invested capital is all the money the businesses use to operate, counting both what owners put in and what lenders provided. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how well the whole area of the economy turns all of its capital into profit.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a whole part of the economy earns a strong return on every dollar invested in it, counting owner money and borrowed money alike.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A sector that earns far above its cost of capital, often asset-light. |
| Around the cost of capital | A sector that roughly covers what its capital costs, creating little extra value. |
| Persistently low | A capital-heavy or competitive sector that struggles to cover its capital cost. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`ROIC = Net Operating Profit After Tax ÷ Invested Capital`

Net operating profit after tax is the profit from operations after tax but before interest, so it counts the return to both owners and lenders. Invested capital is the book value of debt plus equity used in the business, often with spare cash removed. The sector figure combines operating profit and invested capital across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's capital actually earns its return, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

Return on invested capital differs from return on equity because it counts all the capital, not just the owners' share. Because borrowing cannot inflate it, the figure shows the true profitability of a sector's businesses. The sector number is a weighted blend of capital-light and capital-heavy industries, so it reflects the mix inside the sector.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Return on invested capital is the cleanest read on a sector's business quality. It measures profit against every dollar of capital, so a sector cannot lift the figure simply by borrowing more. A sector that earns a high return on invested capital for years has businesses with real advantages, which is the surest sign of lasting strength.

The sector figure matters most when set against the cost of that capital. Capital is not free, since lenders and owners both expect a return, so a sector only creates value when its return on invested capital beats its cost of capital. A sector earning above its cost of capital builds wealth, while a sector earning below it destroys wealth even when it reports a profit. This comparison is the heart of judging whether a sector is worth favoring. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix and the advantages inside it. A sector weighted toward capital-light industries with pricing power will earn a high return, while a sector weighted toward capital-heavy industries that need constant spending on plant and equipment will earn a lower one. Because these traits change slowly, a sector's return on invested capital tends to be persistent.

The strength of competition also shapes the figure. A sector protected by strong advantages can hold a high return for years, while a sector open to easy entry sees rivals compete the return down toward the cost of capital. The economic cycle moves the figure year to year, with capital-heavy and cyclical sectors swinging hardest. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors and against the cost of capital. A sector well above its cost of capital for years marks a structurally strong area of the economy. A sector stuck near or below its cost of capital marks one where capital struggles to earn its keep.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Well above cost of capital, steady | A high-quality sector with durable advantages that builds value. |
| Above cost of capital but swinging | A cyclical sector that earns well at the peak and poorly in downturns. |
| Near or below cost of capital | A capital-heavy or competitive sector that creates little or no value. |

Adjust for capital intensity before judging. Capital-heavy sectors tie up far more invested capital per dollar of profit than asset-light sectors, so a capital-heavy sector belongs in a separate comparison rather than a single ranking against asset-light ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate return on invested capital over several years. A high and steady line well above the cost of capital marks a durably strong sector. A line drifting down toward the cost of capital marks a sector losing its advantages, often as competition catches up, which is one of the clearest warnings a sector is weakening.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip during a downturn that recovers as the economy recovers is a cyclical low, often a chance to favor the sector before the rebound. A slow decline that does not recover points to a permanent change, such as new rivals competing the return away or a sector growing more capital-heavy. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity measures profit against owner money alone, while return on invested capital measures profit against all the capital, including debt. The gap between them shows how much of the sector's return on equity comes from borrowing. Reading the two together separates real business quality from a leverage effect.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector strong on both earns its return through genuine quality and is more dependable. A sector with a high return on equity but a much lower return on invested capital depends on debt, which makes it more vulnerable when financing tightens. The gap between the two is a fast read on how much of the sector's strength is real versus borrowed.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital shows the true profitability of a sector's businesses, and debt to equity shows how much borrowing those businesses carry. A high return earned with little debt is far stronger than the same return propped up by heavy borrowing. Reading the two together shows the quality and the risk behind the return.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a high return on invested capital and low debt is both profitable and safe, the best mix. A high return paired with heavy debt warns that the strength rests on cheap financing staying cheap. The pairing shows whether the sector's returns would survive a rise in interest rates.

## Part 8: Limitations

Return on invested capital depends on the book value of capital, which can be far from current value, especially in older capital-heavy sectors where assets were bought long ago. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single number hides wide differences between the strong and weak industries inside it.

Companies inside a sector may define invested capital differently, which makes raw cross-sector comparison imperfect. The figure also looks backward. A sector can earn high returns right up to a shift in technology or competition that erodes its advantages. A strong number shows where returns have been, not where they are going. The figure means little without the cost of capital beside it, since a high return below a high cost of capital still destroys value.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's return on invested capital above or below its cost of capital, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. How wide is the gap between return on equity and return on invested capital, and how much of the strength is borrowed?
3. Is the high return spread across the sector's industries, or driven by one or two dominant industries or companies?
4. Do strong competitive advantages protect the return, or is competition slowly competing it away?
5. Is any decline a cyclical low that recovers, or a structural slide toward the cost of capital?
6. How capital-heavy is the sector, and does the book value of its capital reflect current value?
7. Which industries inside the sector are pulling the return up, and which are dragging it down?
8. Does the sector's return on capital justify its current standing with investors?
