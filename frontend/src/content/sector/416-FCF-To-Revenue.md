# FCF To Revenue

## Part 1: Definition

FCF to revenue measures how much of a sector's sales turn into spare cash for its owners. Free cash flow, the FCF in the name, is the cash a business has left after paying its running costs and after the spending needed to maintain and grow its assets. Revenue is total sales. The ratio shows what share of each sales dollar survives as cash the owners can actually use. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how efficiently the whole area of the economy converts sales into owner cash.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers how cash-generative a whole part of the economy is, which is a stronger test than accounting profit because cash is harder to dress up.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A cash-rich sector, often asset-light with light reinvestment needs. |
| Moderate | A sector that converts a fair share of sales into spare cash. |
| Persistently low or negative | A capital-heavy sector that plows most of its sales back into assets. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`FCF To Revenue = Free Cash Flow ÷ Revenue`

Free cash flow is the cash left after running costs and after the spending on assets needed to keep the business going and growing. Revenue is total sales. A figure of fifteen percent means fifteen cents of every sales dollar becomes spare cash. The sector figure combines free cash flow and revenue across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's cash generation actually sits, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

A sector's FCF to revenue blends the cash habits of the industries inside it. A sector full of asset-light industries that need little reinvestment keeps a large share of sales as cash and shows a high figure. A sector full of capital-heavy industries that spend constantly on plant and equipment shows a low or even negative figure. The sector number is therefore a weighted average across very different reinvestment needs.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

FCF to revenue is a clean read on a sector's true earning power. Accounting profit can be shaped by choices that do not move cash, while free cash flow is what is left after the bills and the reinvestment are paid. A sector that turns a large slice of sales into cash can fund dividends, buybacks, and growth from its own operations, which makes it self-reliant and resilient. A sector that generates little cash depends on outside financing to keep going.

The sector figure also frames how a sector behaves through the cycle. Cash-generative sectors can keep paying owners and investing in a downturn, while cash-thin sectors must cut back or borrow when sales slip. Knowing how much cash a sector throws off helps the reader judge its quality and staying power before any industry or company is chosen. This top-down read sets the context for the wider decision about where to commit capital.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix and how much reinvestment each industry needs. A sector weighted toward asset-light industries with light spending needs keeps more sales as cash and shows a high figure. A sector weighted toward capital-heavy industries that fund large amounts of plant and equipment shows a low one. Because these needs change slowly, a sector's cash conversion tends to be persistent.

The cycle moves the figure year to year. In a downturn, sales fall while fixed costs and some spending remain, so the figure dips, and it recovers as sales rebound. A sector building a new wave of capacity will show a depressed figure during the build, even if the businesses are healthy, because the spending counts against current cash. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of cash-rich giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors. A sector well above the others is a cash machine that funds itself and rewards owners. A sector well below the others swallows its sales in reinvestment and leans on outside money.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A cash-rich, asset-light sector that funds dividends and growth itself. |
| Moderate | A sector that converts a fair share of sales into spare cash. |
| Low or negative | A capital-heavy sector reinvesting most of its sales into assets. |

Adjust for structure before judging. A capital-heavy sector in a building phase will show a low figure that reflects investment rather than weakness, so compare a sector against others with similar reinvestment needs. A young, fast-growing sector may also show a low figure while it spends to expand, which is different from a mature sector that cannot generate cash.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate FCF to revenue over several years. A high and steady line marks a durably cash-generative sector. A line that swings with the economy marks a cyclical sector whose cash generation depends on the phase of the cycle, which matters for timing the move in and out of the sector.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip during a downturn or a capacity build that recovers afterward is a cyclical or investment low, often temporary. A slow decline that does not recover points to a structural change, such as rising reinvestment needs or fading pricing power that leaves less cash from each sale. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The net income ratio shows the share of sales that becomes accounting profit, and FCF to revenue shows the share that becomes spare cash. Reading the two together shows how much of a sector's reported profit is actually backed by cash. A wide gap, where profit is high but cash is thin, points to heavy reinvestment or profit that does not convert to cash.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector strong on both is genuinely profitable and cash-generative, which is the highest-quality combination. A sector with healthy profit but weak free cash flow is either reinvesting heavily or reporting profit that does not turn into cash. The pairing tells the reader whether a sector's profitability is real and spendable or only an accounting figure.

### Capital Intensity Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Capital intensity shows how much a sector must spend on assets to run its business, and that spending is the main thing standing between sales and free cash flow. A capital-heavy sector loses a large share of its sales to asset spending, which holds down FCF to revenue. Reading the two together explains why a sector's cash conversion is high or low.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with low capital intensity and high FCF to revenue is asset-light and cash-rich, a strong place to commit capital. A sector with high capital intensity and low cash conversion must keep feeding its asset base to stay in business. The pairing shows whether thin cash generation reflects a structural need to reinvest or a deeper weakness.

## Part 8: Limitations

Free cash flow swings with the timing of asset spending, so a single year can look strong or weak for reasons that do not reflect the underlying business. A sector cutting investment can boost the figure in the short run while quietly weakening its future. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those cash-rich giants rather than the sector as a whole.

A sector blends many different industries, so a single number hides wide differences between the cash-rich and capital-hungry industries inside it. Capital-heavy and fast-growing sectors will show low figures for structural reasons, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment. The figure also looks backward. A sector can generate strong cash right up to a shift in technology or competition that raises its spending needs, so a high figure shows where cash has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's FCF to revenue above or below the other sectors, and is the level structural or cyclical?
2. How wide is the gap between the sector's profit margin and its cash conversion, and what explains it?
3. Is a low figure caused by genuine weakness, or by heavy reinvestment in a capital-heavy or fast-growing sector?
4. Can the sector fund its dividends, buybacks, and growth from its own cash, or does it rely on outside financing?
5. Is any decline a cyclical dip or a capacity build that recovers, or a structural rise in reinvestment needs?
6. Does high capital intensity explain weak cash conversion, or is pricing power fading across the sector?
7. Which industries inside the sector are pulling the figure up, and which capital-heavy industries are dragging it down?
8. Does the sector's cash generation justify the quality investors currently assign to it?
