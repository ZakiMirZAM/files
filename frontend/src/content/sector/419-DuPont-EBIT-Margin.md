# DuPont EBIT Margin

## Part 1: Definition

The DuPont EBIT margin measures how much operating profit a sector earns from each dollar of sales. It is one of five levers that together build a sector's return on equity. Operating profit, the EBIT in the name, is what a business earns from running its operations, before interest and tax. The margin is operating profit as a share of sales, so a higher figure means the core business keeps more of each sale before financing and tax. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how profitable the operations of the whole area of the economy are.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers how much operating profit a whole part of the economy squeezes from its sales, which is the operating-profitability lever inside the return-on-equity chain.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A sector with strong pricing power and efficient operations. |
| Moderate | A sector earning a fair operating profit on its sales. |
| Persistently low | A competitive or commodity sector where operating profit stays thin. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`EBIT Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue`

Operating income is profit from running the business, before interest and tax. Revenue is total sales. A figure of twenty percent means a sector earns twenty cents of operating profit on every sales dollar. The sector figure combines operating income and revenue across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's operating profit actually sits, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

This ratio is one lever in the five-factor return-on-equity chain. That chain multiplies five factors to build return on equity: the tax burden, the interest burden, this operating margin, asset turnover, and financial leverage. In plain words, the chain starts with how much operating profit each sale earns, then strips out interest and tax, then scales the result by how hard the assets work and how far owner money is stretched. The EBIT margin is the lever that captures the core profitability of the business itself, before financing and tax cloud the picture. A higher margin feeds a higher return on equity through the rest of the chain. The sector number is a weighted blend across industries with very different cost structures.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

The EBIT margin is the cleanest read on a sector's operating quality. By stopping before interest and tax, it strips out financing choices and tax outcomes and shows how profitable the underlying business is on its own. A sector with a high operating margin keeps a large share of each sale, which usually points to pricing power, scale, or efficiency. A sector with a thin margin earns little from each sale, which usually points to heavy competition or commodity pricing.

The sector figure also frames how a sector behaves through the cycle. Sectors with high, steady margins tend to have durable advantages, while sectors with thin or swinging margins are more exposed to competition and to changes in input costs. Because this lever sits at the top of the return-on-equity chain, it is often the strongest signal of genuine business quality. Knowing a sector's operating margin helps the reader judge its underlying strength before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by the cost structures and pricing power of the industries inside it. A sector weighted toward industries with strong brands, scale, or unique products keeps a high share of each sale and shows a high margin. A sector weighted toward commodity or fiercely competitive industries shows a low one. Because these advantages change slowly, a sector's operating margin tends to be persistent.

The cycle moves the figure year to year, and the size of the swing depends on the sector's cost structure. Sectors with high fixed costs see margins jump when sales rise and fall hard when sales drop, while sectors with flexible costs hold steadier. Input costs matter too, since a rise in raw materials or wages squeezes the margin unless the sector can raise prices. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe the operations of a handful of giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors. A sector well above the others runs high-quality operations with real pricing power. A sector well below the others competes on price and earns little from each sale.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High and steady | A sector with durable pricing power and efficient operations. |
| Moderate | A sector earning a fair operating profit without a strong advantage. |
| Low or swinging hard | A competitive or commodity sector with thin or cyclical margins. |

Adjust for structure before judging. A high-volume, low-margin sector can still earn a strong return on equity if its assets turn over fast, so read this lever next to asset turnover rather than alone. Compare margins among sectors with similar business models, because a thin margin in a high-turnover sector is not the same weakness as a thin margin in a slow-turnover one.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate EBIT margin over several years. A high and steady line marks a sector with a durable operating advantage. A line that swings with the economy marks a cyclical sector whose operating profit depends on the phase of the cycle, which matters for timing the move in and out of the sector.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip during a downturn that recovers as sales rebound is a cyclical low, often temporary. A slow decline that does not recover points to a structural change, such as rising competition, fading pricing power, or higher input costs that the sector cannot pass on. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge this lever, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The operating income ratio and the EBIT margin both measure operating profit against sales, so reading them together confirms the sector's core profitability from two angles. The EBIT margin frames that profitability as the top lever of the return-on-equity chain. Seeing them side by side anchors how much of each sale the sector keeps before financing and tax.

**What the combination tells you.** When both point to a high, steady level, the sector has dependable operating strength that should feed through to its return on equity. When both are thin or falling, the sector is struggling to earn from its sales no matter how it is financed. The pairing confirms whether a sector's operating quality is solid before the other levers are considered.

### Asset Turnover

**Why read the two together.** The EBIT margin shows how much profit a sector earns on each sale, and asset turnover shows how much sales the sector generates from its assets. The two combine to drive the return a sector earns on its assets, which then feeds return on equity. A high margin with low turnover and a low margin with high turnover can produce the same asset return by different routes.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a strong margin and high turnover is exceptional, earning well on each sale and using its assets hard. A sector with a thin margin offsets it only if turnover is high, common in volume-driven areas. The pairing shows whether a sector's operating profitability translates into a strong return on its assets or is undone by a slow-moving asset base.

## Part 8: Limitations

The EBIT margin captures operating profitability but ignores financing and tax, so it shows only the top of the return-on-equity chain rather than the return owners actually receive. A high margin earned on a slow-moving asset base may still produce a weak return on equity, so the margin must be read with turnover and leverage. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe the operations of those giants rather than the sector as a whole.

A sector blends many different industries, so a single number hides wide differences between the high-margin and thin-margin industries inside it. Margins also vary structurally between business models, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment. The figure looks backward as well. A sector can earn strong margins right up to a shift in competition, technology, or input costs that erodes its pricing power, so a high figure shows where operating profit has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's EBIT margin above or below the other sectors, and is the level structural or cyclical?
2. Does the high margin reflect genuine pricing power and efficiency, or a temporary up-phase of the cycle?
3. How much does the margin swing with the economy, given the sector's cost structure?
4. Read alongside asset turnover, does the strong margin translate into a strong return on the sector's assets?
5. Is any decline a cyclical dip that recovers, or a structural erosion from competition or rising input costs?
6. Can the sector pass higher input costs on to customers, or do they squeeze the margin?
7. Which industries inside the sector earn the highest operating margins, and which thin the figure out?
8. Does the sector's operating profitability justify the quality investors currently assign to it?
