# Capital Intensity Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The capital intensity ratio measures how many dollars of assets a company needs to generate one dollar of sales. Assets are everything the company uses to run, such as factories, equipment, and networks. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as utilities or technology, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how asset-heavy the whole sector is.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a broad area of the economy needs a large asset base to operate, which forces constant reinvestment, or runs light and frees up cash for owners. This is the mirror image of asset turnover and a core part of judging the economic character of a sector before narrowing to an industry or a company.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High | A capital-heavy sector that needs large plant, networks, or equipment to produce its sales. |
| Moderate | A balanced sector with a mix of heavy and light industries. |
| Low | A capital-light sector that generates large sales from a small asset base. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Capital Intensity Ratio = Total Assets ÷ Revenue`

Total assets are everything the company owns. Revenue is total sales. The sector figure adds assets and revenue across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes the capital weight of the sector's real economic mass, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

This ratio is the inverse of asset turnover, so a high capital intensity means a low turnover and the other way round. A sector's capital intensity reflects the mix of industries inside it. A sector dominated by utilities, telecom, or heavy manufacturing will show a high figure because it owns large physical assets. A sector dominated by software or services will show a low one. The sector number is therefore a weighted blend of very different asset models.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Capital intensity reveals how much a sector must keep spending just to stay in business and grow. A capital-heavy sector has to pour cash into plant and equipment year after year, which leaves less to return to owners and raises the cost of expansion. A capital-light sector can grow with little new spending, which frees up cash and makes it more flexible. This shapes the top-down decision of which broad part of the economy offers the better long-run deal for owners.

The figure also explains how a sector behaves through the cycle. Heavy assets bring heavy fixed costs, so capital-intensive sectors earn well when sales are high and fall hard when sales drop, because the costs stay even as revenue swings. Knowing a sector's capital intensity helps the reader judge how cyclical and how cash-hungry it is. Heavy reinvestment also tends to come with weaker future returns, because the spending often runs ahead of the demand it serves. This read sets context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix. A sector weighted toward utilities, telecom, and heavy industry will be capital-heavy, while a sector weighted toward software and services will be light. Because the mix changes slowly, a sector's capital intensity is one of its most persistent traits.

Investment waves move the figure over time. When a sector builds new capacity, assets land on the balance sheet before they produce full sales, pushing the figure up for a stretch. When sales fall in a downturn while the asset base stays put, the figure also rises because revenue shrinks against fixed assets. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe the asset weight of a few giants. How assets are valued matters too, because older, heavily depreciated assets carry lower book values and can understate a sector's true capital weight.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors, because it sorts the economy into capital-heavy and capital-light groups. A sector with high capital intensity must keep reinvesting and returns less cash. A sector with low capital intensity is flexible and freer to return cash to owners.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High and stable | A structurally heavy sector, common in utilities, telecom, and heavy industry. |
| Rising over time | A sector in a building phase, with new assets running ahead of sales. |
| Low and stable | A capital-light sector that grows cheaply and returns more cash to owners. |

Adjust for structure before judging. A high figure is not weakness on its own, because a capital-heavy sector can be highly profitable and stable when it earns strong margins and faces little competition, so capital intensity must be read alongside how much the sector earns.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate capital intensity over several years. A high and steady line marks a durably heavy sector. A line that climbs marks a sector in a heavy investment phase, which can either build future earnings or signal overspending that future returns will not justify.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A temporary rise during a building phase that falls back as new assets start producing sales is a healthy investment cycle. A rise driven by falling sales against a fixed asset base is a cyclical low, not a structural change. A slow, steady climb that keeps going can warn of a sector overinvesting faster than demand grows, a pattern that tends to precede weak returns. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Asset Turnover

**Why read the two together.** The capital intensity ratio and asset turnover are two views of the same trait, because one is the inverse of the other. Capital intensity measures assets needed per dollar of sales, while turnover measures sales produced per dollar of assets. Reading them together at the sector level confirms how asset-heavy a sector is from both directions.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with high capital intensity and low turnover is structurally heavy and must keep reinvesting to operate. A sector with low capital intensity and high turnover is light and flexible. The two always move opposite each other, so a sharp change in one without the other signals a data quirk worth checking rather than a real shift in the sector.

### FCF To Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Capital intensity shows how much a sector must sink into assets, and FCF to revenue shows how much sales actually become free cash for owners after that spending. Heavy capital intensity drains cash through constant reinvestment, so reading the two together at the sector level shows how much the asset burden eats into owner returns.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with high capital intensity and low free cash flow to revenue is plowing its earnings back into assets, leaving little for owners, which is the classic profile of a cash-hungry heavy sector. A sector with low capital intensity and strong free cash flow to revenue converts sales into owner cash efficiently. The pairing shows whether a sector's asset weight is starving its owners or leaving room to reward them.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many industries with very different asset needs, so a single number hides wide differences between heavy and light industries inside it. A high figure read alone looks like a drawback, when a capital-heavy sector can be stable and profitable if it earns strong margins, so the ratio must be paired with profitability.

The figure depends on how assets are valued on the books. Older assets carry lower book values after years of depreciation, which understates the capital weight of sectors with aging plant, while sectors that recently invested look heavier. The figure also rises mechanically when sales fall in a downturn, even though the asset base has not grown. A high number shows the asset burden as the books record it, not always the true economics behind it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's capital intensity above or below the market, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Is the sector structurally heavy by business model, or is a high figure a temporary building phase?
3. Does the heavy asset base come with strong enough margins to justify the constant reinvestment?
4. Is the figure spread across the sector's industries, or driven by a few asset-heavy giants?
5. How much does the asset burden eat into the cash the sector can return to owners?
6. Is a rising trend a healthy investment cycle, a cyclical drop in sales, or overinvestment ahead of demand?
7. Which industries inside the sector are capital-heavy, and which run light?
8. Does the sector's capital intensity make it more cyclical and cash-hungry, and does that argue for or against favoring it?
