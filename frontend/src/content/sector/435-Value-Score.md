# Value Score

## Part 1: Definition

Value Score is the scorecard's single rating of how much a sector's businesses create value, expressed as one number from 0 to 100. It blends the measures on the scorecard into that one figure so the reader can rank the sectors quickly. A higher score means the sector screens as a stronger value creator on the blend, and a lower score means it screens as weaker. The score is a screening rank, not a price. It does not say what the sector is worth or whether its shares are cheap. It sorts the field and points to where a closer look is warranted. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector score combines many industries into one size-weighted figure and describes how the whole area of the economy screens on the same blend.

The blend is not balanced evenly. It leans hardest on economic profit, meaning whether the sector's return on capital clears the cost of that capital, and treats margins, cash conversion, valuation, and growth as lighter supporting evidence. So the score answers a pointed question before any industry or company is chosen: does this part of the economy earn more on its capital than that capital costs, and does it do so on numbers that hold up?

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High score | The sector clears its cost of capital with room to spare, so it screens as a genuine value creator worth a closer look. |
| Middle score | The sector covers its cost of capital but adds little beyond it, or mixes strong and weak parts that average out. |
| Low score | The sector earns at or below its cost of capital, or its score rests on numbers that do not hold up, so it screens as a value destroyer or a caution. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Value Score = a weighted blend of the scorecard measures, scaled to a 0 to 100 range, with economic profit carrying the most weight`

Each measure on the scorecard is scored against an absolute scale first, so a wide spread earns a high mark on its own terms rather than only against weak peers, and then nudged by where the sector ranks among the others. The marks are weighted and scaled onto the 0 to 100 range. The heaviest weight sits on the ROIC minus WACC spread, the direct test of whether reinvested capital builds value, with EVA margin beside it to capture the same test per dollar of sales. The cost of capital itself, cash conversion, operating margin, the sector's price against the book value its returns justify, and revenue growth fill in behind them at lighter weights. For banks and property groups, where the return-on-capital test does not apply because debt is their raw material, the blend swaps to the equity-side test of return on equity against the cost of equity.

One rule overrides the blend. A sector whose core profitability is negative, whether an outright loss or a return that sits below its cost of capital, is capped near the bottom of the range, so a value destroyer can never screen as high quality no matter how it compares with equally weak peers. The sector figure is built by scoring each company, then combining across the sector weighted by company size, so the largest companies shape the number most. The score is a summary, so it hides the detail beneath it, and two sectors with the same score can earn it for different reasons.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

The score exists to make the top-down sort fast and to anchor it on the one thing that actually signals value. An investor comparing every sector cannot weigh each measure for each field at once, so a single composite gives a first cut. The reason economic profit carries the most weight is that it is the only measure that answers whether growth in the sector is worth anything. Damodaran puts the test plainly in Investment Valuation: reinvestment raises value only when the return on capital is greater than the cost of capital, and when there are no excess returns the sector's long-run value is unaffected by how fast it grows. A sector that earns above its hurdle compounds value as it reinvests. A sector that earns below it destroys value as it grows, however healthy its reported profit looks.

This is also why the score leans away from raw return on equity and headline growth. Return on equity is contaminated by borrowing: a heavily indebted sector shows a high return on equity mechanically, without earning more on its capital, so it flatters value creation that is not there. Growth without excess returns is motion without progress. By weighting the return-on-capital spread above both, the score ranks the sectors by the quality of their economics rather than by the size of a number that leverage or a boom can inflate. A high score flags a field whose businesses earn their keep and deserves the deeper work of valuation and structural analysis. A low score flags a field where capital struggles, which may not be worth the time.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The deepest driver is the sector's industry mix and the economics that mix carries. A sector weighted toward capital-light industries with pricing power earns well above its cost of capital and scores high, while a sector weighted toward capital-heavy, cyclical, or fiercely competitive industries earns near or below its hurdle and scores low. Because the mix changes slowly, the score tends to be persistent, and a sector that scores high usually keeps doing so until its structure shifts. Margins, cash conversion, and the sector's valuation against the returns it justifies move the score at the margin, and the weights themselves shape it, since a blend anchored on economic profit ranks fields differently from one anchored on cheapness.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A jump in interest rates lifts the cost of capital across the whole market, which raises the hurdle every sector must clear and pulls scores down even when the businesses have not changed. A swing in profitability over the economic cycle moves the return side. Because the score is relative in part, a sector's number can shift because other sectors changed rather than itself. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector's score can really describe a handful of giants while smaller members earn far less, and a one-time gain or a leverage-inflated return in those giants can lift the raw inputs before the numbers settle.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The score is built for ranking, so it is most useful set against the other sectors. A sector near the top of the range screens as a stronger value creator on the blend than one near the bottom, and the gap between scores is roughly the gap in the quality of their economics. The ranking points the reader toward the fields whose businesses earn above their hurdle and away from the fields where growth works against shareholders.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Score well above the other sectors | A high-return, capital-light field whose businesses clear their hurdle with room to spare. |
| Score near the middle of the pack | A field that covers its cost of capital but adds little beyond it, ordinary on the blend. |
| Score near the floor | A field earning at or below its cost of capital, or one leaning on a distorted number, where growth erodes value. |

Compare scores with care, because two sectors can reach the same number by different routes. One may clear its hurdle on genuine returns while another sits near the floor because its core profitability turned negative and the cap pinned it there. Read what drove a sector's score before trusting the rank, and check it against the integrity questions that sit beside the score, which flag when the underlying numbers lean on one-time gains, leverage, or a few dominant companies rather than durable strength.

## Part 6: Reading the score over time

The score is a present-time reading, taken from the most recent fiscal year across the sector, so it is a snapshot rather than a line on a chart. To judge where it is heading, follow the trend of the measures that feed it, each of which has its own multi-year history a click away. The ROIC minus WACC spread carries the most weight, so its direction usually sets the direction of the score: a spread widening over several years lifts the score's foundation, while a spread drifting toward zero undermines it.

Separate the cycle from the structure when reading those inputs. A score that would fall because rising interest rates lifted the cost of capital across the market reflects financing conditions, and it tends to recover when rates ease. A score that would fall because the sector's returns are eroding against a steady hurdle points to a real decline in the businesses. A score that rests on economic profit staying wide through a full cycle, in both easy and tight money, is the strongest evidence that the sector creates value by design rather than by luck. The direction of the heavy inputs matters more than any single reading of the score.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC − WACC Spread

**Why read the two together.** The ROIC minus WACC spread is the measure the score leans on hardest, so the score is largely a scaled, blended version of it. Reading the two together shows whether a high score rests on genuine economic profit or on the lighter measures in the blend. A high score built on a wide spread is anchored on the one test that signals value, while a high score sitting on a thin or negative spread is leaning on margins, growth, or a low hurdle that may not last.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with both a high score and a wide positive spread creates value through real business quality, and its reinvestment should compound. A sector with a decent score but a spread near zero screens well on the supporting measures while barely clearing its hurdle, which the single number can hide. The pairing separates durable quality from a score propped up by softer evidence.

### EVA Margin

**Why read the two together.** EVA margin is economic profit measured per dollar of sales rather than per dollar of capital, and it sits in the blend beside the spread to catch what the spread alone misses. Two sectors can share the same spread yet turn very different amounts of economic profit out of each sale, because one ties up far more capital than the other. Reading the two together shows whether the sector's value creation is thin and capital-hungry or fat and efficient.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a high score, a wide spread, and a healthy EVA margin creates value at both a high rate on its capital and a high yield on its sales, the best mix. A sector with a positive spread but a slim EVA margin earns above its hurdle only on a heavy capital base, so its value creation is more fragile to a rise in the cost of capital. The pairing shows how much room the sector's economics leave before growth stops paying.

## Part 8: Limitations

The score is a screening rank, not a valuation, so it never says what a sector is worth or whether its shares are cheap. It omits price entirely, which means a high-scoring sector can already be expensive and a low-scoring one can be a bargain the score cannot see. It blends several measures into one figure, which hides the detail beneath, so two sectors with the same score can be strong and weak for opposite reasons. The size-weighted number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may describe those leaders rather than the typical member, and it rests on the book value of capital, which can sit far from current value in older, capital-heavy sectors.

The cap that floors value destroyers also compresses the bottom of the range, so several genuinely weak sectors can share a similar low score without being equally weak. The score looks backward, so it shows where a sector's economics have been, not where a shift in technology, competition, or interest rates may take them. It cannot see a fading moat or a coming disruption that the numbers have not yet registered. And a raw input can be distorted by a one-time gain, a leverage-inflated return, or a universe that changed from one year to the next, which is why the score is paired with a set of integrity questions that flag exactly those distortions. The score tells the reader where to look first, not what to conclude, so it must be read with those questions and with a proper valuation before any decision.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's Value Score high, middling, or near the floor, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Does a high score rest on a wide ROIC minus WACC spread, or on the lighter measures in the blend such as margins or growth?
3. Is the return behind the score genuine economic profit, or a return on equity inflated by heavy borrowing across the sector?
4. Would the score survive a rise in interest rates that lifted the sector's cost of capital and narrowed its spread?
5. Is a high score shared across most of the sector's industries, or produced by a few dominant companies?
6. Do the integrity questions beside the score fire, warning that the numbers lean on one-time gains, leverage, or a changed universe?
7. Is the score near the floor because the cap pinned a value destroyer, or because the sector genuinely sits just below its hurdle?
8. Does a high score survive a proper valuation and a look at price, or is it only a screening flag that the deeper work has yet to support?
