# ROIC − WACC Spread

## Part 1: Definition

The ROIC minus WACC spread measures how far a sector's return on its capital sits above or below the cost of that capital. Return on invested capital, often shortened to ROIC, is the percentage a sector earns each year on all the money tied up in its businesses. The weighted average cost of capital, often shortened to WACC, is the return its lenders and owners require for putting that money to work. The spread is the second figure subtracted from the first. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector spread combines many industries into one size-weighted number. It is the clearest single answer to one question: does this part of the economy create wealth or consume it?

A positive spread means the sector earns more on its capital than that capital costs, so every dollar it reinvests builds value. A negative spread means the sector earns less than its hurdle, so growth actually destroys value even when the sector still reports a profit. A spread near zero means the sector runs in place, covering its cost of capital but adding nothing beyond it.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Wide and positive | A high-quality sector whose returns clear the hurdle with room to spare, so reinvestment compounds value. |
| Near zero | A sector that just covers its cost of capital, neither building nor destroying much wealth. |
| Negative | A sector earning below its hurdle, where growth and reinvestment erode value rather than create it. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Spread = ROIC − WACC`

Both figures are computed for each company first, then blended across the sector and weighted by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. The spread shown for the sector is taken from the weighted ROIC and the weighted WACC, so the three figures always reconcile: the spread equals the displayed ROIC minus the displayed WACC. This matters because each company's own capital structure and risk sets its own hurdle, and pooling them gives a fair picture of the whole sector rather than a single blanket assumption.

The spread is a difference, not a level. A sector can have a high ROIC and still show a thin spread if its hurdle is just as high, and a sector with a modest ROIC can show a wide spread if its hurdle is low. Reading the spread keeps the two halves tied together so a strong return is never mistaken for value creation until it is set against its cost.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

The spread is the direct verdict that ROIC and WACC only hint at on their own. A return on its own says nothing about quality, because a high return below a high hurdle still destroys value while a modest return above a low hurdle creates it. By subtracting the hurdle from the return, the spread turns two separate figures into one answer about whether the sector builds wealth. It is the economic profit test, the same idea that drives economic value added, expressed as a percentage so sectors of different sizes can be compared.

The spread also ranks the sectors by quality in a single glance. The sectors with the widest positive spreads are the ones whose business models earn well above the risk they carry, which is where reinvested capital compounds fastest. The sectors with negative spreads are the ones where capital would be better returned to owners than plowed back into growth. This top-down read frames every industry and company choice that follows, because a great company inside a value-destroying sector still fights the tide.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The spread widens when returns rise or the hurdle falls, and narrows when the reverse happens. Pricing power, light capital needs, and durable demand lift the return side, which is why sectors built on brands, software, or intellectual property tend to show the widest spreads. Heavy fixed assets, commodity pricing, and cyclical demand hold returns down, which is why capital-hungry sectors often run near or below zero.

Interest rates move the hurdle for every sector at once. When rates rise, the cost of both debt and equity climbs, so the hurdle lifts and spreads compress across the whole market even if returns hold steady. A sector that looked value-creating in a low-rate world can slip below its hurdle when rates climb. The size-weighted figure can also be dominated by a few giant companies, so a wide sector spread can really describe a handful of leaders while smaller members earn far less.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The spread is most useful ranked against the other sectors, because the gap between spreads is the gap in quality. A sector with a wide positive spread is one the market should reward for reinvesting, while a sector with a negative spread is one where growth works against shareholders.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Wide positive spread | A high-return, capital-light sector that compounds value when it reinvests. |
| Thin positive spread | A sector that creates value but only just, so its edge is fragile to a higher hurdle. |
| Negative spread | A capital-heavy or cyclical sector earning below its cost of capital, where growth erodes value. |

Read the spread alongside its two parts, never alone. A negative spread driven by a temporarily high hurdle is different from one driven by chronically weak returns, and only splitting the two tells the reader whether the problem is the cycle or the business model.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the spread over several years. A steady positive line marks a sector that reliably out-earns its cost of capital, the fingerprint of a durable, high-quality area of the economy. A line that drifts toward or below zero marks a sector whose edge is fading, whether because returns are slipping or the hurdle is rising.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A spread that narrows because rates climbed is a market-wide squeeze that tends to reverse when rates fall, while a spread that narrows because returns are eroding points to a structural decline in the business itself. A spread that stays wide across a full cycle, through both easy and tight money, is the strongest evidence that a sector creates value by design rather than by luck. Reading the trend over many years is the only honest way to judge whether the wealth creation is real.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is the return half of the spread, and the spread cannot be understood without it. A wide spread could come from a strong return or simply from a low hurdle, and only looking at the return itself tells the reader which. Reading the two together shows whether value creation rests on genuine earning power or on cheap capital.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a high return and a wide spread is creating value through real business quality, and its reinvestment should compound. A sector with a modest return but a positive spread is leaning on a low hurdle, which can vanish if rates rise. The pairing separates durable quality from a financing tailwind that may not last.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** The weighted average cost of capital is the hurdle the spread measures against, and a change in the spread can come entirely from the hurdle moving. Reading them together shows whether a narrowing spread reflects a weaker business or simply a higher cost of capital from rising rates.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector whose spread shrank while its hurdle climbed is feeling the rate cycle, and its underlying returns may be intact. A sector whose spread shrank while its hurdle held steady is suffering a real decline in returns. The pairing tells the reader whether to wait for the cycle to turn or to treat the sector as structurally weaker.

## Part 8: Limitations

The spread inherits every estimate baked into its two parts. The cost of capital rests on assumptions about risk and the return owners require, so two reasonable methods can produce different hurdles and therefore different spreads for the same sector. Because the spread is a difference between two large numbers, a small error in either one can flip the verdict between value creation and value destruction, so a spread close to zero should be read with caution.

The size-weighted figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so a positive sector spread may hide many smaller members that earn below their hurdle. The spread also does not apply cleanly to banks and insurers, where debt is raw material rather than financing, so for those the equity-side excess return of ROE over the cost of equity is the better value test. A single sector spread blends many industries with very different economics, so it is a starting point for the search, not the final word.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's spread positive or negative, and how wide is it against the other sectors?
2. Does a wide spread come from a genuinely high return on capital, or from an unusually low cost of capital?
3. How much of a recent narrowing is a market-wide squeeze from rising rates, and how much is a real decline in returns?
4. Would the sector still create value if interest rates lifted its hurdle further?
5. Which industries inside the sector carry the widest spreads, and which sit below zero?
6. Is a positive spread durable across a full cycle, or does it depend on cheap capital staying cheap?
7. Is the size-weighted spread describing the whole sector, or just a few dominant leaders?
8. Does the spread justify reinvesting in the sector, or would shareholders be better served by capital returned to them?
