# Sales To Invested Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Sales to invested capital measures how much revenue a business generates from each dollar of capital put to work in it. Invested capital is the total money funding the business, both the owners' equity and the debt, after setting aside spare cash. Revenue is total sales. The ratio divides sales by invested capital, so it shows how productively the business turns its funding into sales. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as retail or utilities, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes the capital productivity of the whole area of the economy.

A high reading means the sector produces a lot of sales from a small base of capital, which is the mark of a capital-light, fast-turning business. A low reading means the sector needs a large base of capital to generate its sales, which is the mark of a capital-heavy business. At the sector level this shows how efficiently a whole part of the economy puts its funding to work, which is one half of what drives the return that funding earns.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High | A capital-light sector that turns a small capital base into large sales. |
| Moderate | A balanced sector with sales in step with its capital base. |
| Low | A capital-heavy sector that needs a large base of capital per dollar of sales. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Sales To Invested Capital = Revenue ÷ Invested Capital`

Revenue is total sales for the year. Invested capital is equity plus debt, less spare cash, which is the funding actually at work in the business. A reading of two means the sector generates two dollars of sales for each dollar of invested capital. The sector figure combines revenue and invested capital across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's capital productivity actually sits.

A sector's sales to invested capital blends the capital needs of the industries inside it. A sector full of capital-light industries that need little funding to generate sales, such as services or software, shows a high reading. A sector full of capital-heavy industries that must fund large amounts of plant, networks, or inventory shows a low one. The sector number is a weighted average across very different capital intensities, so it captures how much funding the sector needs to do its business.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Sales to invested capital is one of the two engines behind a sector's return on capital. A sector's return on invested capital can be broken into the profit it earns on each sale and the sales it generates from each dollar of capital. This ratio is that second engine, the productivity of capital. A sector that turns its capital quickly into sales can earn a strong return even on modest margins, while a sector that needs heavy capital must earn fat margins to make the same return.

The figure also tells the reader how much new capital a sector must raise to grow. A capital-light sector with a high reading can expand sales without pouring in large amounts of new funding, which makes growth cheap and cash-rich. A capital-heavy sector with a low reading must invest heavily to grow, which consumes cash and ties up funding. Knowing a sector's capital productivity helps the reader judge how efficient and how self-funding its growth will be, which is central to the top-down decision on where to commit capital.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by the capital needs of its industries. A sector weighted toward capital-light industries that generate sales with little funding shows a high reading, while a sector weighted toward capital-heavy industries that must fund large asset bases shows a low one. Because these capital needs are structural, a sector's capital productivity tends to be persistent.

The economic cycle moves the figure year to year, because sales fall faster than the capital base in a downturn, which lowers the reading, and recover as sales rebound. A sector building out new capacity will show a depressed reading during the build, because the new capital is in place before the sales it will generate arrive. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the reading can really describe a handful of giants. A sector shifting toward outsourcing or asset-light models will lift its reading over time even if its sales stay the same.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors to separate the capital-light from the capital-heavy. A sector with a high reading puts its capital to work efficiently, while a sector with a low reading must carry a heavy base of funding to do its business.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High | A capital-light sector that grows sales without heavy new funding. |
| Moderate | A balanced sector with capital productivity near the market. |
| Low | A capital-heavy sector that needs large funding to generate its sales. |

Adjust for structure before judging. A low reading is normal for a capital-heavy sector and is not a sign of weakness on its own, so compare a sector against others with similar capital needs. A capital-heavy sector with a low reading can still earn a strong return on capital if its margins are fat, so this figure must be read alongside profitability rather than alone.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate sales to invested capital over several years. A high and steady reading marks a durably capital-light sector. A reading that swings with the economy marks a sector whose capital productivity depends on the phase of the cycle, which matters for judging the sector's efficiency through good times and bad.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A reading that dips in a downturn or during a capacity build and recovers afterward is a cyclical or investment low, often temporary. A reading that climbs over time may show a sector becoming more capital-efficient, perhaps by shifting to asset-light models. A reading that drifts down for years points to a structural change, such as a sector needing ever more capital to generate the same sales, which erodes its returns. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector's true capital productivity.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is the profit a sector earns on its funding, and it is built from two parts, the margin on each sale and the sales generated from each dollar of capital. Sales to invested capital is that second part. Reading the two together shows how much of a sector's return on capital comes from working its capital hard rather than from fat margins.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a strong return on invested capital and a high sales to invested capital earns its return through capital efficiency, generating large sales from a light base. A sector with a strong return but a low sales to invested capital must be earning fat margins to make up for its heavy capital. The pairing separates sectors that win on volume and turnover from sectors that win on pricing power.

### Asset Turnover

**Why read the two together.** Asset turnover shows sales against total assets, while sales to invested capital shows sales against the funding at work after spare cash. Both measure how hard a sector works its base to generate sales, but invested capital strips out idle cash and focuses on the capital actually deployed. Reading the two together confirms whether a sector's capital efficiency is real or flattered by how its base is measured.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector strong on both is genuinely capital-light and efficient at turning its base into sales. A gap between the two, where one looks efficient and the other does not, points to large cash holdings or a financing structure that changes the picture. The pairing gives a cleaner read on how productively the sector deploys the capital it actually puts to work.

## Part 8: Limitations

Invested capital is built from book values that can drift from real worth, so the ratio rests on accounting figures that may understate or overstate the true capital at work. A sector that has owned its assets for a long time carries a low book base that inflates the reading, while a sector that recently invested carries a high base that depresses it. The figure measures the volume of sales per dollar of capital and says nothing about whether those sales are profitable, so a high reading can mask thin or negative margins.

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single reading hides wide differences between the capital-light and capital-heavy industries inside it. Capital-heavy and fast-building sectors will show low readings for structural reasons, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment. The figure also looks backward, so it shows where the sector's capital productivity has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Where does the sector's sales to invested capital rank against the other sectors, from capital-light to capital-heavy?
2. Is a high reading backed by healthy margins, or does it mask thin or negative profit on each sale?
3. Is a low reading a structural feature of a capital-heavy sector, or a sign of falling capital efficiency?
4. How much of the sector's return on invested capital comes from capital productivity rather than fat margins?
5. Is a dip in the reading a cyclical low or a capacity build that recovers, or a structural rise in capital needs?
6. Does the sector's high capital productivity let it grow sales without raising large amounts of new funding?
7. Which industries inside the sector are pulling the reading up, and which capital-heavy industries are dragging it down?
8. Is the sector becoming more capital-efficient over time, or does it need ever more capital to generate the same sales?
