# How to Read a Sector

## What it is

This overview is the method behind the Define This Sector First questions. A sector is one of eleven top-level groupings that split the whole market by what companies sell: Technology, Financial Services, Healthcare, Consumer Cyclical, Consumer Defensive, Industrials, Energy, Basic Materials, Real Estate, Communication Services, and Utilities. The averages on this page are computed across every listed company in the sector, weighted by size. They are the output of the sector's businesses, not an explanation of them, and this page asks you to build the explanation first.

The test is simple. Could you describe this sector to someone who has never heard of it: what it sells, who pays for it, who the biggest companies are, and why the profitable ones are profitable? Until you can, a ROIC number for the sector is a fact without a cause. Once you can, the same number becomes evidence for or against a story you already hold, which is the only way a number ever changes a decision.

## What defining a sector requires

| Step | The question | Why it comes before the numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Product and buyer | What does the sector sell, and who are the end buyers | Decides what demand actually depends on: consumers, businesses, or governments |
| Main players | Who are the biggest companies, and what separates leaders from the pack | The weighted averages ARE the biggest companies; you cannot read the average without knowing who dominates it |
| Growth driver | Is revenue growth coming from volume, price, mix, or something structural | Volume growth and price growth fade differently; structural growth is the only kind worth a premium |
| Value chain | Who keeps the profit as a dollar moves from raw input to end customer | Explains why one layer earns a spread over its cost of capital while the next layer earns nothing |
| Business models | How do the dominant models here turn revenue into profit | A subscription sector, a spread-lending sector, and a commodity sector produce the same ratios by different machinery |
| Failure modes | What actually kills companies here | The risk that ends the investment is usually the sector's standard failure, not an exotic one |

Structure sets the ceiling on the whole exercise. The five competitive forces, meaning rivalry, entry, supplier power, buyer power, and substitutes, decide how much profit a sector's structure permits its companies to keep. A sector with high entry barriers and weak buyers can hold returns above the cost of capital for decades; a sector with low barriers and strong buyers competes its returns away even while its demand grows. The capital cycle runs on top of that: high returns attract investment, investment builds capacity, and capacity kills the returns, so a sector's current spread must always be read against how much capital is flowing in.

## Why it matters

Every number below this section has a qualitative cause, and the cause decides whether the number persists. A wide ROIC minus WACC spread is a fact; whether it is a moat or the top of a capital cycle is a judgment that requires knowing the businesses. A sector that screens cheap may be cheap because its dominant model is dying. The financial averages can rank sectors, but only the definition you build here can tell you whether the ranking means anything.

## Common traps

The first trap is treating the sector as one business. Every sector contains industries with opposite economics; the industry scorecard below exists because the sector average hides the spread between them.

The second trap is reading the average without knowing its weights. The averages are market-cap weighted, so two or three giants can set the sector's ROIC while thirty small companies drown. Know whether you are reading the sector or reading its largest member.

The third trap is explaining growth without naming its type. Price-driven growth reverses when the price cycle turns; volume growth saturates; only structural growth, a durable shift in what buyers need, deserves a lasting premium.

The fourth trap is defining the sector as it was. Sector definitions drift: companies migrate, business models change, and the classification lags. Define what the sector's companies do today, not what the label suggests they did ten years ago.
