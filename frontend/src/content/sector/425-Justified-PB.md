# Justified P/B

## Part 1: Definition

Justified price-to-book measures the price-to-book ratio a sector's fundamentals warrant. The price-to-book ratio compares a sector's market value with its book value, the owners' stake recorded on the balance sheet. The actual ratio is set by the market. The justified ratio is what the ratio should be, given how profitably the sector uses owner money and how fast it is expected to grow. It is built from the return on equity, the growth rate, and the cost of equity, so it expresses fair value rather than market mood. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes the price-to-book the whole area of the economy deserves.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It is a fair-value anchor for the sector's equity. A justified ratio above one means the sector's fundamentals deserve a price above book value, because it earns more on owner money than owners require. A justified ratio below one means the opposite.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Well above one | A sector whose return on equity beats its cost of equity by a wide margin, so its fundamentals warrant a rich price. |
| Around one | A sector that roughly earns its cost of equity, so its fundamentals warrant a price near book value. |
| Below one | A sector that earns less on owner money than owners require, so its fundamentals warrant a discount to book. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g)`

Return on equity is the profit earned on owner money. The growth rate, written as g, is how fast the sector's earnings are expected to grow for the long run. The cost of equity, written as Ke, is the return owners require. The formula compares how much the sector earns above its growth-adjusted hurdle. When return on equity equals the cost of equity, the justified ratio is exactly one, meaning fair value sits at book value. The sector figure combines these inputs across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most.

The formula rises with return on equity and growth and falls with the cost of equity. A sector that earns far more on owner money than owners require deserves a high price-to-book. A sector that earns less deserves a discount. The sector number is a weighted blend of richly valued and cheaply valued industries, so it reflects the mix inside the sector.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Justified price-to-book turns a sector's fundamentals into a fair price. It captures the central insight that a sector deserves a price above book value only when it earns more on owner money than owners require. By combining return on equity, growth, and the cost of equity into one ratio, it sets a benchmark for what the sector's equity is worth, free of the market's swings of optimism and fear.

The figure matters most when compared with the actual price-to-book the market is paying. A sector trading well above its justified ratio may be overvalued, priced for more than its fundamentals support. A sector trading well below may be overlooked, priced for less than it deserves. This comparison is a top-level read on whether a part of the economy is cheap or expensive relative to what it actually earns. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector figure is driven first by the spread between return on equity and the cost of equity. A sector that earns far above its equity hurdle warrants a high price-to-book, while a sector that earns below it warrants a discount. The industry mix sets this spread, and because it changes slowly, a sector's justified ratio tends to be persistent. Growth lifts the ratio further when the sector earns above its hurdle, since profitable growth compounds the value created.

The cost of equity moves the figure through interest rates and market risk. A rise in rates lifts the hurdle and lowers the justified ratio for every sector, even when profitability holds steady. The growth assumption also swings the figure, and small changes in expected long-run growth can move the justified ratio sharply, especially when growth sits close to the cost of equity. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors and, above all, against each sector's own actual price-to-book. A sector whose justified ratio sits well above the others is one whose fundamentals warrant a premium. A sector whose justified ratio sits below the others warrants a discount, because it earns less on owner money relative to what owners require.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Justified ratio high, actual ratio lower | A sector the market may be underpricing relative to its fundamentals. |
| Justified and actual ratios close | A sector the market is pricing roughly in line with its fundamentals. |
| Justified ratio low, actual ratio higher | A sector the market may be overpricing relative to its fundamentals. |

Compare like with like. A sector rich in high-return, asset-light industries earns a high justified ratio for genuine reasons, so it belongs beside similar sectors rather than capital-heavy ones. Sectors built on lending earn high returns on equity for structural reasons, so their justified ratios belong in a separate comparison.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate justified price-to-book over several years, most usefully against the actual ratio over the same span. A justified ratio that holds steady marks a sector whose fundamentals are stable. A justified ratio that drifts up or down marks a sector whose profitability, growth, or hurdle is changing.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip in the justified ratio during a downturn, as return on equity falls, is a cyclical move that recovers with the economy. A steady decline points to a structural change, such as competition eroding the sector's return on owner money. Watching the gap between the justified and actual ratios over time is the real prize, because a market price that drifts far above the justified ratio warns of overvaluation, while a price that drifts far below may flag a bargain. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Actual P/B

**Why read the two together.** Justified price-to-book is the ratio the sector's fundamentals warrant, and actual price-to-book is the ratio the market is paying. Neither answers the valuation question alone, because fair value means nothing without the market price beside it. Reading the two together is the core test of whether a sector is cheap or expensive relative to what it earns.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector whose actual price-to-book sits well below its justified ratio may be undervalued, priced for less than its fundamentals support. A sector whose actual ratio sits well above its justified ratio may be overvalued, priced for more than it deserves. The gap between the two is a direct read on whether the market is overpaying or underpaying for the sector's equity.

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is the engine of the justified ratio, since a sector deserves a price above book value only when it earns more on owner money than owners require. Reading the two together shows whether a high justified ratio rests on strong profitability or on a generous growth assumption. The return on equity grounds the justified ratio in something the sector actually earns.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a high return on equity and a high justified ratio deserves its premium on the strength of real profitability. A sector with a modest return on equity but a high justified ratio is leaning on optimistic growth, which is fragile. The pairing shows whether the fair value the formula produces rests on solid ground or on assumptions.

## Part 8: Limitations

Justified price-to-book is highly sensitive to its inputs. The growth rate and the cost of equity both sit in the formula in ways that can swing the result sharply, and when growth approaches the cost of equity the ratio can become unstable and unreliable. Because the cost of equity is itself an estimate built on a model of risk, the justified ratio inherits that uncertainty.

The figure rests on the book value of equity, which can sit far from current value, and book value carries little meaning for asset-light sectors whose worth lies in things the balance sheet does not record. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single justified ratio hides wide differences inside it. The formula assumes steady long-run growth, which rarely holds neatly, so the justified ratio is a guide to fair value rather than a precise target.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's justified price-to-book above or below one, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. How does the justified ratio compare with the actual price-to-book the market is paying, and which way does the gap point?
3. Does a high justified ratio rest on a strong return on equity, or on an optimistic growth assumption?
4. How close is the growth rate to the cost of equity, and how unstable does that make the figure?
5. Would the justified ratio fall sharply if the cost of equity rose with interest rates?
6. Is the book value behind the ratio meaningful, or is the sector asset-light with worth the balance sheet misses?
7. Which industries inside the sector warrant the richest ratios, and which warrant a discount?
8. Is the gap between the justified and actual ratios a genuine mispricing, or a sign the market sees something the fundamentals miss?
