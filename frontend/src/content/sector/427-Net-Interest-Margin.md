# Net Interest Margin

## Part 1: Definition

Net interest margin measures the spread a lender earns between the interest it collects and the interest it pays. A lender, such as a bank, takes in money through deposits and borrowing, then lends it out at a higher rate. Net interest income is the difference between the interest earned on loans and the interest paid on funding. The margin divides that income by the assets that earn it, so it shows how much spread the lender keeps for each dollar of lending. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as financials or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes the lending spread across the whole area of the economy.

Net interest margin only carries meaning for sectors heavy in lenders, above all financials. For a sector of manufacturers or software firms the margin is close to zero and tells the reader nothing. For a bank-heavy sector it is the core driver of profitability, because lending is how these businesses make most of their money.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Wide margin | Lenders earn a large spread, often in a high-rate or low-competition setting. |
| Moderate margin | A normal spread between lending rates and funding costs. |
| Thin margin | A squeezed spread, often from low rates or fierce competition for loans. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Net Interest Margin = Net Interest Income ÷ Average Earning Assets`

Net interest income is interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowing. Average earning assets are the loans and securities that produce that interest, measured across the year. A margin of three percent means the lender keeps three cents of spread for each dollar of earning assets. The sector figure combines net interest income and earning assets across every lender in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest banks shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's lending spread actually sits.

A bank-heavy sector's net interest margin blends the spreads of the lenders inside it. Lenders that fund themselves cheaply with deposits and lend at higher rates show a wide margin. Lenders that rely on costly borrowing or compete hard on loan rates show a thin one. The sector number is a weighted average across very different lending models, and it is meaningless for the parts of the sector that do not lend.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

For a bank-heavy sector, net interest margin is the engine of profit. Most of a lender's earnings come from the spread between what it earns on loans and what it pays for funding, so the margin sets the ceiling on how profitable the sector can be. A sector of lenders with wide margins can earn strong returns, while a sector with squeezed margins struggles no matter how well it controls costs. This makes the margin the first thing to read when judging a financial sector.

The figure also reveals how the sector responds to interest rates, which is the main macro force on lenders. Rising rates often widen the spread because loan rates reprice faster than deposit rates, while falling rates can compress it. Knowing a sector's typical margin and how it moves with rates helps the reader judge whether the sector is positioned to benefit from the rate cycle or to suffer from it. This is a top-down read that sets the context before any bank is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The level of interest rates is the strongest force on the sector margin. When rates rise, lenders often earn more on new and floating loans before they pass higher rates to depositors, which widens the spread. When rates fall, the spread can narrow. The shape of the yield curve matters too, because lenders borrow short and lend long, so a steep curve helps the margin and a flat curve hurts it.

Competition for loans and deposits sets the baseline. A sector with many lenders fighting for the same borrowers will price loans tightly and earn a thinner margin. The funding mix matters, because lenders rich in cheap deposits earn more than those leaning on costly market borrowing. A small number of very large banks can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the sector margin can really describe a handful of giants. Loan losses do not enter the margin directly, but a sector pushing for higher spreads by lending to riskier borrowers may show a wide margin that later turns into losses.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

Net interest margin is not a fair cross-sector metric, because only lending-heavy sectors produce a meaningful number. Comparing a bank-heavy sector against a manufacturing sector on this figure is meaningless. The honest comparison is between the financial sector and itself across time, or against financial sectors in other markets.

| Cross-period pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Margin widening as rates rise | A rate-driven tailwind lifting the sector's lending profit. |
| Margin steady through the cycle | A sector with stable funding and disciplined loan pricing. |
| Margin shrinking despite normal rates | Rising competition or a worsening funding mix squeezing the spread. |

Adjust for structure before judging. A wide margin can reflect either cheap deposit funding or riskier lending, and the two carry very different risk. A sector earning a wide margin by lending to weaker borrowers may be storing up losses that the margin alone never shows.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate net interest margin over several years. A steady line marks a financial sector with stable funding and disciplined pricing. A line that tracks the interest-rate cycle marks a sector whose lending profit rises and falls with rates, which matters for timing the move in and out of the sector.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A margin that widens with rising rates and narrows with falling rates is a normal cyclical swing tied to the rate environment. A margin that grinds lower for years across different rate settings points to a structural change, such as rising competition, the spread of low-cost online lenders, or a lasting shift toward costly funding. Reading the trend against the rate cycle is the only honest way to tell a temporary squeeze from a permanent erosion of the sector's lending economics.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Assets

**Why read the two together.** Net interest margin shows the spread a lending sector earns on its earning assets, and return on assets shows the total profit the sector earns on all its assets. For a bank-heavy sector the margin is the largest input into return on assets, so reading the two together shows how much of the sector's asset profitability comes from the lending spread. A wide margin should feed a healthy return on assets.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a wide margin but a weak return on assets is leaking profit elsewhere, through high costs or loan losses that eat the spread. A sector with a thin margin but a solid return on assets earns its keep from fees and other income rather than lending. The pairing shows whether the sector's profit really rests on its lending spread or on something else.

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Net interest margin sets the lending profit a financial sector earns, and return on equity shows the profit it earns on owner money after leverage. Lenders run on heavy borrowing, so a modest margin can still produce a high return on equity once leverage is applied. Reading the two together shows how the sector turns a thin spread into a return for owners.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a healthy margin and a strong return on equity earns its return from genuine lending profit. A sector with a thin margin but a high return on equity leans hard on leverage, which lifts returns in good times and magnifies losses in bad. The pairing reveals how much of the sector's profitability is real lending strength and how much is borrowed.

## Part 8: Limitations

Net interest margin only means something for lending-heavy sectors, so it is useless for most of the economy and even misleading when applied to a mixed sector. A wide margin can reflect either cheap funding or riskier lending, and the figure cannot tell the two apart, so a strong margin can mask loans that later default. The margin ignores fees and trading income, which can be a large part of a modern financial sector's profit.

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-banks, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. The margin also looks backward and moves with rates, so a wide figure set in a high-rate year says little about the spread the sector will earn once rates change. A strong margin shows where lending profit has been, not where it is going, so it must be paired with a view on the rate cycle and on credit quality.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is this sector lending-heavy enough for net interest margin to carry real meaning?
2. Is the sector's margin wide because of cheap deposit funding, or because it is lending to riskier borrowers?
3. How does the margin move with the interest-rate cycle, and where in that cycle is the sector now?
4. Is a widening margin a rate-driven tailwind, or is a narrowing margin a structural squeeze from competition?
5. Does the wide margin feed through to a healthy return on assets, or is it eaten by costs and loan losses?
6. How much of the sector's profit comes from the lending spread, and how much from fees and other income?
7. Which lenders inside the sector are pulling the margin up, and which are dragging it down?
8. Does a strong current margin rest on a rate setting that is about to change?
