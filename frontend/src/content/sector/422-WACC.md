# WACC

## Part 1: Definition

The weighted average cost of capital measures the blended yearly cost of all the money a sector uses. A sector funds its businesses with two kinds of capital. Debt is money borrowed from lenders, who charge interest. Equity is money from owners, who expect a return for the risk they take. The weighted average cost of capital, often shortened to WACC, blends these two costs in proportion to how much of each the sector uses. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes the hurdle rate the whole area of the economy must clear to create value.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers a single question: what return must this part of the economy earn just to satisfy its lenders and owners? Every other return is judged against it. A sector earns value only when it beats this hurdle, and destroys value when it falls below it.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Low | A steady, lightly borrowed sector with calm cash flows and a modest hurdle. |
| Around the market average | A balanced sector with a normal mix of risk and borrowing. |
| High | A risky or volatile sector where owners and lenders demand more, lifting the hurdle. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`WACC = (E ÷ V × Ke) + (D ÷ V × Kd × (1 − Tax Rate))`

Here E is the value of equity, D is the value of debt, and V is the two added together. Ke is the cost of equity, the return owners require. Kd is the cost of debt, the interest rate on borrowing, and it is reduced by the tax rate because interest is tax-deductible. The formula weights each cost by its share of total capital. The sector figure combines the capital and the costs across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most.

The weighted average cost of capital is a hurdle, not a profit. It says nothing about how well a sector performs, only what it must clear. The sector number is a weighted blend of low-risk and high-risk industries, so it reflects the mix inside the sector and the financing habits within it.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

The weighted average cost of capital is the yardstick against which every return is measured. A sector's profit means little on its own, because a high return below a high hurdle still destroys value, while a modest return above a low hurdle creates it. By setting the hurdle, this figure turns raw returns into a verdict on whether a sector builds wealth. It is the reason a sector's return on invested capital is judged against its cost of capital rather than in isolation.

The figure also reads the risk the market sees in a sector. A high cost of capital means lenders and owners demand more, which signals a riskier or more volatile area of the economy. A low cost of capital signals a steady, dependable one. Knowing the hurdle helps the reader judge whether a sector's returns are genuinely strong or merely keeping pace with the risk. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector figure is driven first by risk. Owners demand a higher return from sectors whose profits swing hard with the economy, which lifts the cost of equity and the overall hurdle. Steady, defensive sectors face a lower hurdle. The industry mix sets this baseline, and because risk traits change slowly, a sector's cost of capital tends to be persistent relative to the others.

Interest rates move the figure for every sector at once. When central banks raise rates, both the cost of debt and the cost of equity climb, lifting the hurdle across the whole market. The sector's financing mix also matters, because debt is cheaper than equity after the tax break, so a more heavily borrowed sector can show a lower blended cost, though it carries more risk in return. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors, because the gap between hurdles reflects the gap in risk. A sector with a low cost of capital is one the market sees as steady and dependable. A sector with a high cost of capital is one the market sees as risky, so it must earn more just to break even.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Low hurdle | A defensive, steady sector with calm cash flows that owners and lenders trust. |
| Moderate hurdle | A balanced sector with ordinary risk and financing. |
| High hurdle | A cyclical or volatile sector where capital providers demand a large premium. |

Read the hurdle alongside the return, never alone. A high cost of capital is not a flaw if the sector earns even more, and a low cost of capital is no comfort if the sector still falls short. The comparison that matters is the return a sector earns set against the hurdle it faces.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate cost of capital over several years. A steady line marks a sector whose risk and financing habits hold constant. A line that rises marks a sector facing a higher hurdle, often because interest rates have climbed or because the market sees the sector as riskier than before.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A rise driven by climbing interest rates is a market-wide move that lifts every sector's hurdle together, and it tends to reverse when rates fall. A rise specific to one sector points to a structural change, such as the market judging the sector more volatile or more heavily indebted. The hurdle matters most when read against the sector's returns over the same span, because a rising hurdle squeezes value creation even when profits hold steady. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is the percentage return a sector earns on all its capital, and the weighted average cost of capital is the hurdle that return must clear. Neither figure means much alone, because a return only creates value when it beats the cost of the capital behind it. Reading the two together is the core test of whether a sector builds or destroys wealth.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector whose return on invested capital sits well above its cost of capital creates value and rewards the capital invested in it. A sector whose return falls below its cost of capital destroys value even while reporting a profit. The gap between the two, often called the spread, is the single clearest measure of a sector's quality.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity shows how much a sector borrows, and the weighted average cost of capital blends the cost of that debt with the cost of equity. Because debt is cheaper than equity after the tax break, more borrowing can lower the blended hurdle, but it also raises risk, which can push the cost of equity up. Reading the two together shows how a sector's financing choices shape its hurdle.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with heavy debt and a low cost of capital is leaning on cheap borrowing to hold its hurdle down, which works until rates rise or lenders pull back. A sector with light debt and a moderate hurdle is financing itself conservatively. The pairing shows whether a low hurdle is durable or rests on financing conditions staying favorable.

## Part 8: Limitations

The weighted average cost of capital is an estimate, not a measured figure. It depends on assumptions about risk and the return owners require, so two reasonable methods can produce different hurdles for the same sector. Because so much rests on it, a small error in the hurdle can change the verdict on whether a sector creates value. The cost of equity in particular leans on a model of risk that markets do not always follow.

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries with different risks, so a single hurdle hides wide differences inside it. The figure also says nothing about performance on its own. A low hurdle is meaningless if the sector cannot even clear it, so the cost of capital is only useful set beside the return the sector actually earns.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's cost of capital high or low against the other sectors, and what does that say about the risk the market sees?
2. Does the sector's return on invested capital clear its cost of capital, and how wide is the spread?
3. Is a high hurdle driven by genuine business risk, or by heavy borrowing that could turn against the sector?
4. How much of a recent rise in the hurdle is market-wide from rising rates, and how much is specific to the sector?
5. Would the sector still create value if interest rates lifted its hurdle further?
6. Is a low hurdle durable, or does it rest on cheap borrowing that depends on favorable financing conditions?
7. Which industries inside the sector face the highest hurdles, and do their returns justify the risk?
8. Does the gap between the sector's return and its hurdle justify its current standing with investors?
