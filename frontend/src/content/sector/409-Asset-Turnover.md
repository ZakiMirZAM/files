# Asset Turnover

## Part 1: Definition

Asset turnover measures how much yearly sales a company generates for each dollar of assets it owns. Assets are everything the company uses to run, such as cash, factories, equipment, and inventory. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or industrials, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how hard the whole sector's asset base works to produce sales.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a broad area of the economy is asset-light, turning a small asset base into large sales, or asset-heavy, needing a large asset base to support its sales. This is a core part of judging the economic character of a sector before narrowing to an industry or a company.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High | An asset-light sector that produces large sales from a small asset base, often services or distribution. |
| Moderate | A balanced sector with a mix of asset-light and asset-heavy industries. |
| Low | An asset-heavy sector that needs large plant, equipment, or networks to generate its sales. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Total Assets`

Revenue is total sales for the year. Total assets are everything the company owns. The sector figure adds revenue and assets across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes how productively the sector's real economic weight uses its assets, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

A sector's asset turnover reflects the mix of industries inside it. A sector dominated by capital-light industries that sell services or move goods will show a high figure. A sector dominated by capital-heavy industries that own large factories, pipelines, or networks will show a low one. The sector number is therefore a weighted blend of very different business models.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Asset turnover reveals the economic engine of a sector, which is whether it grows by selling more through a light asset base or by building ever more plant and equipment. This shapes how a sector responds to the economy and how much capital it must keep sinking into itself. Capital-light sectors can expand sales without heavy spending, while capital-heavy sectors must invest large sums just to grow, which weighs on the cash they can return to owners.

The figure also explains a sector's profit math. A sector can earn a strong return on assets either by charging a high margin on each sale or by turning its assets over many times at a thin margin. Knowing which lever a sector pulls helps the reader understand where its profitability comes from and how durable it is. This top-down read sets context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix. A sector weighted toward asset-light services will turn its assets quickly, while a sector weighted toward heavy industry, real estate, or utilities will turn them slowly. Because the mix changes slowly, a sector's typical turnover tends to be persistent.

The economic cycle moves the figure year to year. When sales rise in a boom, turnover rises because the same asset base produces more revenue, and when sales fall, turnover drops as assets sit idle. A wave of heavy investment across a sector pushes the figure down for a time, because new assets land on the balance sheet before they produce full sales. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe the asset productivity of a few giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors, because it sorts the economy into asset-light and asset-heavy groups. A sector with high turnover earns its return through speed and volume. A sector with low turnover earns its return through margin instead, charging more on each sale to justify its large asset base.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High turnover | An asset-light sector that leans on volume and usually returns more cash to owners. |
| Low turnover, high margin | An asset-heavy sector that earns through pricing, common in utilities and heavy industry. |
| Falling over time | A sector pouring capital into new assets faster than sales are catching up. |

Adjust for structure before judging. A low figure is not weakness, because an asset-heavy sector can be highly profitable through margin, so turnover must always be read together with how much the sector earns on each sale.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate asset turnover over several years. A high and steady line marks a durably asset-light sector. A line that rises and falls with the economy marks a sector whose turnover tracks the business cycle, climbing in booms as assets get fully used and falling in downturns as they sit idle.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip that recovers as sales rebound is a cyclical move, common in sectors tied to consumer demand or raw materials. A slow, steady decline that does not recover points to a structural change, such as a sector building heavier assets or facing falling prices that shrink sales against a fixed asset base. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Asset turnover shows how fast a sector turns assets into sales, and the net income ratio shows how much profit it keeps from each dollar of sales. A sector earns its return on assets through some blend of the two, so reading them together at the sector level reveals which lever drives the sector's profitability. One sector wins on speed, another on margin.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with high turnover and a thin margin earns its return through volume, which suits efficient, competitive parts of the economy. A sector with low turnover and a fat margin earns through pricing power on a heavy asset base. A sector strong on both is unusually attractive, while a sector weak on both struggles to earn its keep no matter how the economy turns.

### Capital Intensity Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Asset turnover and the capital intensity ratio are two views of the same trait, which is how asset-heavy a sector is. Turnover measures sales against assets, while capital intensity measures the assets needed to support sales. Reading them together at the sector level confirms how much capital a sector must commit to operate and grow.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with low turnover and high capital intensity is structurally heavy, must keep reinvesting, and returns less cash to owners. A sector with high turnover and low capital intensity is light, flexible, and freer to return cash. The pairing pins down where a sector sits on the spectrum from capital-light to capital-heavy, which shapes its long-term attractiveness.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many industries with very different asset needs, so a single number hides wide differences between asset-light and asset-heavy industries inside it. A low figure read alone looks like inefficiency, when it often just reflects a capital-heavy business model that earns through margin instead.

The figure depends on how assets are valued on the balance sheet. Older assets carry lower book values after years of depreciation, which can flatter turnover in sectors with aging plant, while sectors that recently built new assets look worse. A high figure shows sales productivity as the books record it, not the true age or quality of the asset base behind it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's asset turnover above or below the market, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Does the sector earn its return through high turnover and volume, or through fat margins on a heavy asset base?
3. Is the figure spread across the sector's industries, or driven by a few dominant companies?
4. How much does the sector's turnover swing with the economic cycle, and where in the cycle is it now?
5. Is a falling trend a cyclical dip in sales, or a structural shift toward a heavier asset base?
6. How much capital must the sector keep sinking into assets to grow, and how does that affect cash returned to owners?
7. Which industries inside the sector are asset-light, and which are weighing the figure down?
8. Is the sector's asset productivity an advantage that supports favoring it, or a structural drag that argues against it?
