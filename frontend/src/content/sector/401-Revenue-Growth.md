# Revenue Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Revenue growth measures how fast sales rise from one year to the next. Revenue, also called sales, is the money a company takes in from selling its products and services before any costs are subtracted. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how fast the whole area of the economy is expanding.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a whole part of the economy is growing or shrinking, which is the question an investor asks when deciding where to put money before narrowing down to an industry or a company.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A young or expanding sector tied to a long-running shift in demand. |
| Around the economy's growth | A mature sector that grows roughly with the overall economy. |
| Persistently low or negative | A shrinking sector losing ground to newer parts of the economy. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Revenue Growth = (This Year's Revenue − Last Year's Revenue) ÷ Last Year's Revenue`

This year's revenue and last year's revenue are the total sales in each period. The result is shown as a percentage. The sector figure combines revenue across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's sales are actually growing, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

A sector's revenue growth blends the growth of the industries inside it. A sector full of expanding industries will show a high figure. A sector full of mature or declining industries will show a low one. The sector number is therefore a weighted average of very different growth stories.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Revenue growth guides the highest-level investment decision: which broad part of the economy to favor. Capital flows toward sectors that are expanding and away from sectors that are stalling, so a sector growing its sales for years tends to attract investment and support higher stock prices. A sector with flat or falling sales tends to stay out of favor.

The sector figure also frames the choice between offense and defense. Some sectors grow steadily through good times and bad, while others surge in booms and stall in downturns. Knowing a sector's typical growth and how much it swings with the economy helps the reader decide whether the sector fits a search for durable expansion or a search for recovery upside. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by the long-run demand for what the sector sells. A sector tied to a lasting shift, such as a move toward new technology or new energy sources, will grow for years. A sector tied to a shrinking need will grow slowly or decline. Because these demand trends change slowly, a sector's growth tends to be persistent.

The economic cycle moves the figure year to year, and the size of the swing depends on how sensitive the sector is to the economy. Sectors tied to consumer spending and raw materials swing hard, while sectors tied to necessities stay steady. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants. Price increases also lift revenue without any rise in the amount sold, so high inflation can flatter a sector's growth.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors and the overall economy. A sector growing well above the economy for years marks an expanding area that is taking a larger share of total spending. A sector growing below the economy for years marks one that is slowly losing ground.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Above the economy, steady | A structurally growing sector riding a lasting demand trend. |
| Above the economy but swinging hard | A cyclical sector that surges in booms and stalls in downturns. |
| Below the economy for years | A mature or declining sector losing share to newer parts of the economy. |

Adjust for inflation before judging. A sector can show high revenue growth mostly from rising prices rather than rising volume, so a sector with heavy price increases belongs in a separate comparison from one growing on real demand.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate revenue growth over several years. A high and steady line marks a durably expanding sector. A line that swings widely with the economy marks a cyclical sector whose growth depends on the phase of the cycle, which matters for timing the move in and out of the sector.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A deep dip that recovers as the economy recovers is a cyclical low, often a chance to favor the sector before the rebound. A slow decline that does not recover points to a permanent change, such as a sector losing demand to a newer part of the economy. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Revenue growth shows how fast the sector is expanding, and return on equity shows how profitably it turns owner money into earnings. A sector can grow fast yet earn little, or earn well yet barely grow. Looking at both locates the sector on the map of long-term attractiveness.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector that is both growing and profitable is the most attractive place to commit capital for the long run. Rapid growth with weak profitability warns that competition inside the sector is consuming the rewards of expansion. Strong profitability with little growth describes a mature sector that returns cash rather than reinvesting it.

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Revenue growth shows the top line expanding, and the operating income ratio shows how much of each sales dollar survives as operating profit. A sector with high fixed costs turns extra sales into much more profit, while a sector with thin margins keeps little of the growth. Reading the two together shows whether growth is reaching the bottom line.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with rising sales and a widening operating margin is converting growth into real profit, the strongest signal of all. Rising sales with a flat or falling margin warns that the cost of growth is eating the gains. Falling sales with a shrinking margin marks a sector under genuine pressure.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole. A sector blends many different industries, so a single growth number hides wide differences between the fast and slow industries inside it. Revenue growth says nothing about profit, so a sector can grow its sales for years while losing money on every sale.

The figure can be inflated by rising prices rather than rising volume, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading during high inflation. Revenue growth also looks backward. A sector can grow strongly right up to a shift in technology or consumer behavior that ends the trend. A strong growth number shows where demand has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's revenue growth above or below the overall economy, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Is the growth real volume growth, or is it mostly rising prices during a period of high inflation?
3. Is the growth spread across the sector's industries, or driven by one or two dominant industries or companies?
4. How much does the sector's growth swing with the economic cycle, and where in the cycle is it now?
5. Is the trend a cyclical swing that recovers, or a structural decline as the sector loses demand to newer parts of the economy?
6. Is the sector growing and profitable, or growing while earning thin returns?
7. Which industries inside the sector are pulling the growth up, and which are dragging it down?
8. Is the sector currently in or out of favor with investors, and does the growth justify that standing?
