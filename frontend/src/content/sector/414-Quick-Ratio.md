# Quick Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The quick ratio measures whether a business can cover its short-term bills using only its most liquid assets. Liquid assets are things that turn into cash fast, such as cash itself, money owed by customers, and short-term investments. Inventory is left out, because goods on the shelf can be slow to sell and may have to be discounted to clear. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how easily the whole area of the economy could meet its near-term obligations without leaning on selling stock.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers how much short-term financial cushion a whole part of the economy carries, which is a quick read on resilience when conditions tighten.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A sector with ample liquid assets, common in cash-rich, asset-light areas. |
| Around one | A sector that can meet its short-term bills with its quick assets. |
| Persistently low | A sector that relies on inventory or fresh financing to cover near-term obligations. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities`

Current assets are what the businesses expect to turn into cash within a year. Inventory is the stock of unsold goods, removed here because it is the slowest current asset to convert. Current liabilities are the bills due within a year. The sector figure combines these items across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's short-term cushion actually sits, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

A sector's quick ratio blends the liquidity habits of the industries inside it. A sector full of inventory-light, cash-rich industries will show a high figure. A sector full of inventory-heavy industries, such as retail or manufacturing, will show a lower one once that inventory is stripped out. The sector number is therefore a weighted average across very different business models.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

The quick ratio is a strict read on a sector's short-term financial safety. It strips out inventory and asks whether the most dependable assets alone can cover the near-term bills. A sector that clears this bar comfortably can ride out a slow patch or a credit squeeze without forced selling or emergency borrowing. A sector that falls short depends on selling inventory or raising fresh money at the worst possible time.

The sector figure also frames how a sector would hold up if financing dried up. When lenders pull back, sectors with thin quick liquidity face the most strain, because they cannot lean on inventory that no one is buying in a downturn. Knowing a sector's quick liquidity helps the reader judge its resilience before any industry or company is chosen. This top-down read sets the context for the wider decision about where to commit capital.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by its industry mix and how much each industry relies on inventory. A sector weighted toward inventory-light industries that hold cash will show a high figure. A sector weighted toward retailers and manufacturers that carry large stocks will show a low one, because that inventory is removed. Because these habits change slowly, a sector's quick ratio tends to be persistent.

Cash management across the sector moves the figure over time. When companies build cash buffers, the figure rises, and when they spend down cash on buybacks or expansion, it falls. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants sitting on big cash piles. Broad shifts in payment terms with customers and suppliers can also nudge the figure without any real change in safety.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors. A sector well above the others marks a liquid, resilient area that can absorb a shock. A sector well below the others marks one that depends on inventory or fresh financing to meet its near-term bills.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Well above one | A cash-rich, inventory-light sector with a strong short-term cushion. |
| Around one | A sector that can cover its near-term bills with its quick assets. |
| Persistently below one | An inventory-heavy sector that leans on stock sales or financing. |

Adjust for structure before judging. An inventory-heavy sector will always look weaker on the quick ratio than on the current ratio, so the gap between the two is structural rather than a sign of trouble. Compare a sector against others with similar inventory needs rather than across very different business models.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate quick ratio over several years. A high and steady line marks a sector that keeps a firm short-term cushion. A line that drifts lower marks a sector spending down its liquid assets, which can leave it exposed if conditions turn.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip during an expansion, when companies put cash to work, often reverses as the cycle matures. A steady decline that does not reverse points to a structural change, such as a sector taking on more short-term obligations or holding less cash by design. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio counts all current assets, while the quick ratio removes inventory. Reading the two together shows how much of a sector's short-term cushion depends on selling its stock. A wide gap between them means a sector leans heavily on inventory to look liquid. A narrow gap means the cushion rests on cash and receivables that convert quickly.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector strong on both ratios is genuinely liquid and resilient. A sector that looks fine on the current ratio but weak on the quick ratio is depending on inventory it may struggle to sell in a downturn. The pairing tells the reader how dependable a sector's short-term safety really is once the slow assets are set aside.

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** The quick ratio is a snapshot of liquid assets against near-term bills, and the cash conversion cycle measures how long a sector ties up cash in operations before collecting it. A short cycle frees cash and tends to support a healthy quick ratio. A long cycle traps cash in receivables and inventory, which strains short-term liquidity.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with strong quick liquidity and a short cash cycle runs efficiently and rarely faces cash strain. A sector with a weak quick ratio and a long cash cycle is vulnerable, because cash is locked up just when it may be needed. The pairing shows whether a sector's liquidity is a steady state or a fragile balance.

## Part 8: Limitations

The quick ratio is a single snapshot, so it shows liquidity on one date rather than across a full year of cash swings. It treats receivables as dependable, yet in a downturn customers pay more slowly, so the real cushion can be thinner than the figure suggests. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies holding large cash piles, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole.

A sector blends many different industries, so a single number hides wide differences between the cash-rich and inventory-heavy industries inside it. Inventory-heavy sectors will always look weak on this measure for structural reasons, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment. The figure also looks backward. A sector can show comfortable liquidity right up to a shock that freezes its receivables or its financing, so a safe-looking figure shows where liquidity has been, not how it will hold up next.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's quick ratio above or below the other sectors, and is the level structural or a recent change?
2. How wide is the gap between the quick ratio and the current ratio, and how much does the sector lean on inventory to look liquid?
3. Could the sector meet its near-term bills without selling stock or raising fresh money?
4. How exposed is the sector to a credit squeeze that would make short-term financing harder to get?
5. Is any decline a cyclical move as companies put cash to work, or a structural drop toward thinner liquidity?
6. Does a long cash conversion cycle explain weak quick liquidity, or does the sector simply hold little cash?
7. Which industries inside the sector are pulling the figure up, and which inventory-heavy industries are dragging it down?
8. Does the sector's liquidity justify the resilience investors currently assign to it?
