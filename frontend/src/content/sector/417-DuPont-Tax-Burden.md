# DuPont Tax Burden

## Part 1: Definition

The DuPont tax burden measures how much of a sector's pre-tax profit survives after tax. It is one of five levers that together build a sector's return on equity. Pre-tax profit is what a business earns before income tax is taken out. The tax burden is the slice of that profit the business keeps once tax is paid, so a higher figure means more profit is retained for owners. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how much of the whole area of the economy's pre-tax profit makes it past the tax authorities.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers how favorable the tax outcome is for a whole part of the economy, which is the tax-retention lever inside the return-on-equity chain.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High | A sector that keeps most of its pre-tax profit, often with light or well-managed tax. |
| Moderate | A sector taxed at around the typical corporate rate. |
| Low | A sector facing heavy tax, common where rates are high or breaks are few. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Tax Burden = Net Income ÷ Pre-tax Income`

Net income is profit after tax. Pre-tax income is profit before tax. A figure of seventy-five percent means a sector keeps seventy-five cents of every pre-tax dollar after tax. The sector figure combines net income and pre-tax income across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's tax outcome actually sits, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

This ratio is one lever in the five-factor return-on-equity chain. That chain multiplies five factors to build return on equity: the tax burden, the interest burden, the operating margin, asset turnover, and financial leverage. In plain words, the chain starts from how much profit each sale earns, adjusts for interest and tax, then scales the result by how hard the assets work and how far owner money is stretched. The tax burden is the final lever, the share of profit that survives tax to reach owners. A higher tax burden lifts return on equity, and a heavier tax bite lowers it. The sector number is a weighted blend across industries with different tax positions.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

The tax burden isolates the role of tax in a sector's bottom line, separate from how well the businesses actually operate. Two sectors can run equally good operations yet end with different returns to owners because one faces a heavier tax bite. Pulling out this lever shows how much of a sector's profitability is shaped by tax rather than by the business itself. A favorable tax position is a real advantage, but it is a different kind of strength from strong operations.

The sector figure also flags exposure to tax policy. A sector that keeps a high share of pre-tax profit may be relying on tax breaks or favorable rates that could change. Knowing how much a sector leans on the tax lever helps the reader judge how durable its returns are if tax law shifts. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen, and it explains part of why two sectors with similar operations can reward owners differently.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by the tax positions of the industries inside it. A sector weighted toward industries that earn income in low-tax places or qualify for tax breaks keeps more of its pre-tax profit. A sector weighted toward industries taxed at the full domestic rate keeps less. Because tax structures change slowly, a sector's tax burden tends to be fairly steady from year to year.

Tax policy moves the figure over time. A cut in the corporate tax rate lifts the figure across all sectors at once, while the removal of an industry tax break lowers it for the sectors that relied on it. One-time items, such as a write-down or a settled tax dispute, can swing the figure in a single year without reflecting the normal tax position. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe the tax outcome of a handful of giants.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors. A sector well above the others keeps more of its pre-tax profit, which lifts its return on equity for tax reasons rather than operating ones. A sector well below the others gives up more to tax, which holds down its return.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High | A sector keeping most of its pre-tax profit, often through tax breaks or low-tax income. |
| Around the average | A sector taxed near the typical corporate rate. |
| Low | A sector facing a heavy tax bite that drags on its return to owners. |

Adjust for one-time items before judging. A single year's figure can be thrown off by a tax dispute or a write-down, so read the lever across several years. Compare the lever in the context of the full return-on-equity chain, because a strong tax burden cannot make up for weak operating margins or poor asset use.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate tax burden over several years. A high and steady line marks a sector with a durable, favorable tax position. A line that steps up or down sharply usually reflects a change in tax law rather than anything about the underlying businesses.

Separating policy from one-time noise is the key skill. A single-year spike or dip often comes from a one-time tax item and reverses the next year. A lasting shift, such as a new corporate tax rate or the loss of an industry break, moves the line to a new level and stays there. Reading the trend across several years is the only honest way to judge this lever, because a single year reflects the moment more than the sector's normal tax position.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** The tax burden is one of the five levers that build return on equity, and reading the two together shows how much of a sector's return comes from a favorable tax outcome. A strong return on equity backed by a high tax burden is partly a tax story. The pairing locates how much of the headline return is operating quality versus a tax advantage that could change.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a strong return on equity and an ordinary tax burden earns its return mostly from operations, which is more durable. A strong return that leans on an unusually high tax burden is exposed to a change in tax policy. The pairing tells the reader how much of a sector's profitability would survive if its tax advantage went away.

### DuPont Interest Burden

**Why read the two together.** The tax burden and the interest burden are the two levers that sit between operating profit and the bottom line in the return-on-equity chain. The interest burden shows how much operating profit survives interest, and the tax burden shows how much of what remains survives tax. Reading them together shows the full path from operating profit to owner profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector that keeps a high share through both levers carries light interest and light tax, so its operating profit flows cleanly to owners. A sector squeezed on both is losing profit to lenders and to tax before owners see it. The pairing shows how much of a sector's operating strength actually reaches its return on equity.

## Part 8: Limitations

The tax burden says nothing about how well a sector operates, since it sits below operating profit in the chain. A sector can post a flattering tax burden in a year shaped by one-time items that will not repeat, so a single figure can mislead. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe the tax outcome of those giants rather than the sector as a whole.

A sector blends many different industries, so a single number hides wide differences between the lightly and heavily taxed industries inside it. The lever is sensitive to tax policy that can change with little warning, which makes a favorable figure less dependable than an operating strength. The figure also looks backward. A sector can enjoy a light tax position right up to a change in tax law, so a high figure shows where the tax outcome has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's tax burden above or below the other sectors, and is the level structural or shaped by one-time items?
2. How much of the sector's return on equity comes from this tax lever rather than from operations?
3. Does the sector lean on tax breaks or low-tax income that a change in policy could remove?
4. Is a recent move a one-time tax item, or a lasting shift from a new tax rate or rule?
5. How would the sector's return on equity hold up if its tax advantage disappeared?
6. Read alongside the interest burden, how much of the sector's operating profit reaches owners after both levers?
7. Which industries inside the sector keep the most of their pre-tax profit, and which give up the most?
8. Does the sector's tax position make its return on equity look stronger than its operations alone would justify?
