# EVA Margin

## Part 1: Definition

Economic value added margin measures the surplus profit a sector earns above its capital cost for each dollar of sales. Economic value added, often shortened to EVA, is the profit left after the full cost of capital is subtracted, counting both the interest lenders charge and the return owners expect. Dividing that surplus by revenue turns the dollar figure into a percentage, so large and small sectors can be compared on the same scale. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how much true value each dollar of sales creates across the whole area of the economy.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers whether a whole part of the economy turns its sales into real surplus, not just accounting profit. A positive margin means each dollar of sales builds wealth. A negative margin means sales generate profit but still fall short of the capital cost.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Strongly positive | A sector that creates real surplus on every dollar of sales, often asset-light with pricing power. |
| Near zero | A sector that roughly covers its capital cost and adds little value per dollar of sales. |
| Negative | A capital-heavy or competitive sector whose sales earn a profit but fail to cover the capital cost. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`EVA Margin = EVA ÷ Revenue`

Economic value added is net operating profit after tax minus a capital charge, where the capital charge is invested capital multiplied by the weighted average cost of capital. Revenue is total sales. The sector figure combines the surplus and the sales across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Because the result is a percentage, it lets the reader compare a giant sector and a small one on equal terms.

Economic value added margin fixes the main weakness of the raw dollar figure, which is that a large sector looks impressive simply because it is large. The margin strips out size and shows the rate of value creation per dollar of sales. The sector number is a weighted blend of high-margin and low-margin industries, so it reflects the mix inside the sector.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

Economic value added margin is the fairest single read on a sector's value creation. A plain profit margin can look healthy while the sector still falls short of its capital cost, because a margin says nothing about the capital behind the sales. This figure subtracts the full capital charge first, so a positive margin proves the sector creates real surplus on its sales rather than just reporting earnings.

The figure also makes sectors of very different sizes directly comparable, which the raw dollar version cannot. A small sector with a high margin creates more value per dollar of sales than a giant sector with a thin one. Capital flows toward sectors that create value efficiently, so a high and steady margin marks an attractive place to commit capital. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector figure is driven first by its industry mix and how asset-light it is. A sector weighted toward capital-light industries with strong margins earns a high surplus per dollar of sales, while a sector weighted toward capital-heavy industries carries a large capital charge that thins the margin. Because the mix changes slowly, a sector's economic value added margin tends to be persistent.

Operating profitability moves the figure with the economic cycle, since the surplus rises and falls with margins. The cost of capital also matters, because a rise in interest rates lifts the capital charge against every dollar of sales and shrinks the margin. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe a handful of giants. Heavy borrowing or buybacks across the sector can shift invested capital and move the margin without any real change in the businesses.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors, and because it is already a percentage the ranking is fair across sectors of any size. A sector with a high margin for years marks an area that creates real value efficiently. A sector near zero or negative marks one where sales earn a profit but cannot cover the capital cost.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High and steady | A high-quality sector that creates real surplus on every dollar of sales. |
| Positive but swinging | A cyclical sector whose margin is strong at the peak and thin or negative in downturns. |
| Negative for years | A capital-heavy or competitive sector that reports profit but destroys value per dollar of sales. |

Adjust for structure before judging. Sectors built on lending measure capital and revenue differently, so they belong in a separate comparison from asset-light sectors rather than a single ranking.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate economic value added margin over several years. A margin that stays high marks a durably value-creating sector. A margin that drifts toward zero marks a sector losing its edge, often as competition compresses its profitability or as the cost of capital rises against it.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip into negative territory during a downturn that recovers as the economy recovers is a cyclical low, often a chance to favor the sector before the rebound. A slow slide that does not recover points to a permanent change, such as competition competing the surplus away or the sector growing more capital-heavy. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge a sector, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** Economic value added is the dollar surplus the sector creates, and economic value added margin is that surplus per dollar of sales. The dollar figure shows the scale of value creation, while the margin shows the rate. Reading the two together separates a sector that creates a lot of value because it is large from one that creates value efficiently on each sale.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector strong on both creates value at a large scale and a high rate, the best mix. A sector with a large dollar figure but a thin margin creates value mainly because it is big, which is less impressive per dollar of sales. The pairing shows whether the sector's value creation is broad and large or genuinely efficient.

### Net Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Net income ratio is the sector's accounting profit per dollar of sales, while economic value added margin is the surplus per dollar of sales after the capital charge. The gap between them is the cost of the capital the sector uses. Reading the two together shows how much of the reported margin survives once the capital cost is paid.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a healthy net income ratio and a positive economic value added margin earns a profit that genuinely beats its capital cost. A sector with a healthy net income ratio but a negative economic value added margin reports profit while still destroying value, a warning the plain margin hides. The gap is a fast read on how capital-hungry the sector's profits really are.

## Part 8: Limitations

Economic value added margin leans on the cost of capital, which is an estimate, so a small change in that estimate can flip the margin from positive to negative. The figure rests on the book value of capital, which can sit far from current value, especially in older capital-heavy sectors. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe those giants rather than the sector as a whole.

A sector blends many different industries, so a single margin hides wide differences between the value-creating and value-destroying industries inside it. Sectors built on lending measure capital and sales differently, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment. The figure also looks backward. A sector can hold a strong margin right up to a shift in competition, technology, or interest rates that changes its economics, so a high margin shows where value has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's economic value added margin positive, and how does it rank against the other sectors?
2. Does the margin come from the businesses, or would it turn negative if the cost of capital rose a little?
3. How wide is the gap between the net income ratio and the economic value added margin, and how capital-hungry are the sector's profits?
4. Is the surplus spread across the sector's industries, or driven by one or two dominant industries or companies?
5. How much does the margin swing with the economic cycle, and where in the cycle is it now?
6. Is any decline a cyclical dip that recovers, or a structural slide as competition compresses the surplus?
7. Which industries inside the sector are lifting the margin, and which are dragging it down?
8. Does the sector's value creation per dollar of sales justify its current standing with investors?
