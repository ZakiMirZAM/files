# DuPont Interest Burden

## Part 1: Definition

The DuPont interest burden measures how much of a sector's operating profit survives after interest is paid. It is one of five levers that together build a sector's return on equity. Operating profit is what a business earns from running its operations, before interest and tax. The interest burden is the slice of that profit left once lenders are paid, so a higher figure means interest takes a smaller bite. A sector is a broad grouping of related industries, such as technology or energy, so the sector figure combines many industries into one. The sector view weights the figure by company size and describes how much of the whole area of the economy's operating profit makes it past its lenders.

The sector figure sits one level above the industry figure. It answers how heavily interest costs drag on a whole part of the economy, which is the interest-drag lever inside the return-on-equity chain.

| Sector-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near one | A sector with little debt, where interest barely dents operating profit. |
| Moderate | A sector carrying a normal amount of debt and interest. |
| Low | A heavily borrowed sector where interest eats a large share of operating profit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Interest Burden = Pre-tax Income ÷ Operating Income`

Operating income is profit from running the business, before interest and tax. Pre-tax income is what is left after interest but before tax. A figure of ninety percent means interest takes ten percent of operating profit, leaving ninety for owners and the tax authorities. The sector figure combines pre-tax income and operating income across every company in the sector and weights by company size, so the largest companies shape the sector number most. Size weighting describes where the sector's interest drag actually sits, rather than letting small companies distort the picture.

This ratio is one lever in the five-factor return-on-equity chain. That chain multiplies five factors to build return on equity: the tax burden, the interest burden, the operating margin, asset turnover, and financial leverage. In plain words, the chain starts from how much profit each sale earns, then strips out interest and tax, then scales the result by how hard the assets work and how far owner money is stretched. The interest burden is the lever that captures the cost of debt, the share of operating profit that survives the interest bill. A figure near one means interest barely dents profit, while a lower figure means lenders take a large share before owners do. The sector number is a weighted blend across industries with different debt loads.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge a sector

The interest burden isolates the cost of a sector's borrowing, separate from how well the businesses operate. Two sectors can run equally strong operations yet end with different returns to owners because one pays far more interest. Pulling out this lever shows how much of a sector's profit is lost to lenders before it can reach owners. A light interest burden means more operating profit flows through, while a heavy one means debt is quietly draining the return.

The sector figure also flags sensitivity to interest rates. A sector with a low interest burden already loses a large share of profit to debt, and rising rates would deepen that drag. Knowing how heavily interest weighs on a sector helps the reader judge how its returns would hold up if borrowing costs climbed or credit tightened. This top-down read sets the context before any industry or company is chosen, and it links a sector's financing choices to its profitability.

## Part 4: What influences the sector aggregate

The sector number is driven first by how much debt the industries inside it carry. A sector weighted toward asset-light industries with little debt shows a figure near one, because interest barely touches operating profit. A sector weighted toward capital-heavy or finance-driven industries that borrow heavily shows a lower figure. Because debt loads change slowly, a sector's interest burden tends to be persistent.

Interest rates and credit conditions move the figure over time. When rates rise, the interest bill grows and the figure falls across borrowed sectors, even if operating profit holds steady. When rates fall or debt is paid down, the figure rises. A small number of very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so a sector number can really describe the debt position of a handful of giants. A sharp drop in operating profit during a downturn can also lower the figure, because the same interest bill takes a bigger share of a smaller profit.

## Part 5: How this sector compares with other sectors

The sector figure is most useful ranked against the other sectors. A sector near one carries little debt and loses almost nothing to interest. A sector well below the others is heavily borrowed and gives up a large share of its operating profit to lenders.

| Cross-sector pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Near one | A lightly borrowed sector where interest barely dents profit. |
| Moderate | A sector carrying a normal debt load and a manageable interest bill. |
| Low | A heavily borrowed sector exposed to rising rates and tight credit. |

Adjust for structure before judging. Finance-driven sectors that borrow as part of their business model show a different pattern, so they belong in a separate comparison from asset-light sectors. Read this lever next to the operating margin, because a sector with strong operating profit can absorb a heavier interest bill than one with thin margins.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The sector chart shows the aggregate interest burden over several years. A line near one and steady marks a sector that finances itself conservatively and loses little to interest. A line that drifts lower marks a sector taking on more debt or facing higher rates, which leaves less operating profit for owners.

Separating the cycle from the structure is the key skill. A dip when rates rise that reverses as rates ease is a cyclical move tied to financing conditions. A steady decline that does not reverse points to a structural shift, such as a sector borrowing more by design or funding buybacks with debt. A dip driven by falling operating profit in a downturn recovers as profit rebounds. Reading the trend across a full cycle is the only honest way to judge this lever, because a single year reflects the moment more than the structure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity shows how much a sector borrows, and the interest burden shows what that borrowing costs in lost operating profit. Reading the two together connects the size of a sector's debt to the drag it puts on the bottom line. A high debt load paired with a heavy interest burden confirms that borrowing is genuinely weighing on returns.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with heavy debt but a still-healthy interest burden is borrowing at low cost or earning enough operating profit to absorb it. A sector with heavy debt and a low interest burden is being squeezed, and rising rates would hurt it badly. The pairing shows whether a sector's borrowing is comfortable or already straining its profitability.

### DuPont EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** The EBIT margin shows how much operating profit a sector earns from each sale, and the interest burden shows how much of that profit survives the interest bill. Reading the two together traces operating strength through to what reaches owners. A strong margin can carry a heavy interest burden, while a thin margin leaves little room for debt.

**What the combination tells you.** A sector with a strong operating margin and a light interest burden converts its sales into owner profit cleanly and safely. A sector with a thin margin and a heavy interest burden has little cushion, so a small setback in sales or a rise in rates can wipe out the return. The pairing shows how much of a sector's operating profit actually survives to lift return on equity.

## Part 8: Limitations

The interest burden says nothing about how well a sector operates, since it sits below operating profit in the chain. A sharp swing in operating profit can move the figure even when the interest bill is unchanged, so a single year can mislead. The size-weighted sector figure can be dominated by a few mega-companies, so it may describe the debt position of those giants rather than the sector as a whole.

A sector blends many different industries, so a single number hides wide differences between the lightly and heavily borrowed industries inside it. Finance-driven sectors carry interest as part of their business model, which makes raw cross-sector comparison misleading without adjustment. The figure also looks backward. A sector can show a light interest burden right up to a jump in rates or a refinancing at higher cost, so a high figure shows where the interest drag has been, not where it is going.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the sector's interest burden near one or well below it, and is the level structural or driven by recent rate moves?
2. How much operating profit does the sector lose to interest before it can reach owners?
3. How exposed is the sector to rising rates and tighter credit given its current interest drag?
4. Is a recent dip a cyclical move tied to rates, a downturn in operating profit, or a structural rise in borrowing?
5. Read alongside debt to equity, is the sector's borrowing comfortable or already straining its profit?
6. Read alongside the operating margin, does the sector earn enough operating profit to absorb its interest bill?
7. Which industries inside the sector lose the most operating profit to interest, and which lose the least?
8. Does the sector's interest drag threaten the return on equity investors currently reward it for?
