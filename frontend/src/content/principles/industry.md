# Principles: Judging an Industry

This page teaches you how to judge an industry, which is a group of companies that sell similar products and face similar economics. It sits in the middle of the app's flow. The economy pages tell you how the whole system behaves. The single company pages tell you about one business. This page is the layer in between. It explains why some industries are far more profitable than others, and how to compare companies fairly once you know which industry they belong to. Read it before you trust any number on a sector page or a comparison table, because a company can only be understood against the industry it lives in.

## What an industry actually is

An industry is a group of companies whose products are close substitutes and whose profits move for similar reasons. The simplest way to know whether two companies are in the same industry is to ask whether the choices of one directly affect the other. If Mercedes cuts the price of a coupe, BMW has to think about a price response, so the two are direct competitors (David Besanko, Economics of Strategy). Companies that compete for the same customers, buy from the same kinds of suppliers, and rise and fall with the same forces belong together.

This matters because almost everything else on this page depends on drawing the boundary correctly. Group companies too broadly and the comparison is meaningless. Group them too narrowly and you miss real rivals. Besanko describes a practical test: two firms compete if a price increase by one sends many of its customers to the other (David Besanko, Economics of Strategy).

Get the boundary right first. Then the rest of the analysis can stand on it.

## Industry structure sets the profit ceiling

How much money an industry can earn is decided largely by the intensity of competition inside it. The standard way to think about that intensity is the five competitive forces: rivalry between existing companies, the threat of new companies entering, the threat of substitute products, the bargaining power of suppliers, and the bargaining power of buyers. When all five are mild, the industry can earn high profits for years. When they are fierce, profits get competed away (David Besanko, Economics of Strategy).

Besanko shows this with the airline industry, where carriers repeatedly slashed prices to fill empty seats and turned good years into industry-wide losses. The economics of competition, he writes, cannot be undone. An industry with low barriers and easy price wars will struggle no matter how hard any single company tries (David Besanko, Economics of Strategy).

| Force | The question it answers |
|-------|--------------------------|
| Rivalry | How hard do existing companies fight each other on price? |
| New entrants | How easily can a new company show up and take share? |
| Substitutes | Can buyers meet the same need a different way? |
| Supplier power | Can suppliers raise input prices and keep the gains? |
| Buyer power | Can customers force prices down? |

Read the structure before you read any company in it. A great company in a brutal industry is still swimming against the current.

## A moat is a durable competitive advantage

A moat is a structural advantage that protects a company's high returns from competition for a long time. The word comes from Warren Buffett, who pictured a great business as a castle protected by a moat that keeps attackers out (Pat Dorsey, The Little Book That Builds Wealth). The key word is durable. High returns always attract competitors, and for most companies the returns get competed away quickly. A moat is what slows that process down.

Dorsey explains why this raises the value of a company. Compare two firms growing at the same rate and using the same capital. The one with a moat can keep reinvesting at high returns for a decade or more, because rivals are held at bay. The one without a moat sees its returns drop as soon as competitors move in. The moated company is worth more today because it generates profits protected from competition for far longer (Pat Dorsey, The Little Book That Builds Wealth).

Hamilton Helmer describes specific sources of this kind of power, such as scale economies, where a larger company spreads fixed costs over more output and a new rival cannot match its costs (Hamilton Helmer, 7 Powers). When you judge an industry, ask which companies in it can defend their advantage and which are running on luck.

## The value-creation test

An industry creates value only when the return it earns on the money invested in it beats the cost of that money. Return on invested capital is the profit a business earns for every dollar tied up in it. The cost of capital is the return investors could earn elsewhere on an equally risky investment, so it is what the company must beat just to break even in economic terms (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA). The cost of capital is not a bill you can see. It is an opportunity cost, the return lenders and shareholders give up by leaving their money in this business instead of another (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA).

Stewart calls the surplus economic value added, or EVA, which is sales minus operating costs minus the full cost of the capital used. If a business only earns its cost of capital, it creates no value. It is worth just the money put into it. Real value appears only when the return clears the cost of capital (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA).

Damodaran frames the same idea as a hurdle rate, the minimum return an investment must clear, and the rule that a company should only invest when the expected return beats that hurdle (Aswath Damodaran, Applied Corporate Finance). When you look at an industry, find out whether its typical returns sit above or below this line. Below the line, the industry is destroying value even while it looks busy.

## Compare like with like

A multiple is a price expressed as a number of times some figure such as earnings, sales, or book value, for example a price that is fifteen times earnings. Multiples and ratios only mean something when you compare them against true peers in the same industry. A company that trades at twenty times earnings is not automatically expensive. It depends on what comparable companies trade at and why (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation).

Damodaran warns that this convenience is also the danger. It is easy to pull together a multiple and a group of so-called comparable companies, but if those companies differ in risk, growth, or cash flow, the comparison quietly breaks. He notes that firms in the same business are routinely called comparable even when they are not truly alike (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). A fair comparison controls for those differences. An unfair one just lines up numbers that look similar.

So before you read a comparison table, check that the companies on it really share the same economics. If they do not, the table is decoration, not analysis.

## Growth is not automatically good

Growth helps only when each new dollar invested earns more than it costs. Growth that earns exactly the cost of capital is neutral. It adds size but not value. Growth that earns less than the cost of capital actually destroys value, because the company is pouring money into projects that return less than investors could get elsewhere (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA).

Stewart puts it bluntly. You can expand sales, earnings, and margins all you want, but if you are not growing economic value added, you are not creating value. You are at best preserving it (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA). Damodaran reaches the same conclusion through the hurdle rate: growth from investments that beat the hurdle adds value, and growth from investments below it subtracts value (Aswath Damodaran, Applied Corporate Finance).

This is one of the most useful habits you can build. When an industry or a company brags about fast growth, ask the next question. Is that growth earning more than it costs, or is it just getting bigger?

## Capital allocation separates good operators from bad ones

Capital allocation is how a company decides to use the cash it generates. It can reinvest in the business, buy back its own shares, pay dividends, or acquire other companies. Over time, these choices drive returns more than almost anything else, especially in slower-growing industries where there is excess cash to redeploy each year (William Thorndike, The Outsiders).

Thorndike describes the disciplined approach used by the best operators. They set a hurdle rate, the minimum acceptable return for any project, then compare every option against it and against each other, choosing the one with the highest risk-adjusted return (William Thorndike, The Outsiders). Giroux shows the same skill at industry scale. The average company, he writes, deploys capital poorly, buying back stock when prices are high and cutting back when they are cheap, and overspending late in expansions just before demand falls (Giroux, Capital Allocation). He points to the collapse of General Electric, which destroyed enormous shareholder wealth by deploying capital at low or negative returns, repeating the same mistakes without learning from them (Giroux, Capital Allocation).

When you study an industry, watch how its companies spend. Two firms with identical products can deliver very different returns purely because one allocates capital well and the other does not.

## Aggregated numbers can mislead

The numbers a company reports are not a neutral measurement. Financial statements often conceal more than they reveal, and a sound judgment depends on factors that a standard set of ratios will not show (Martin Fridson, Financial Statement Analysis). Fridson calls this the adversarial nature of financial reporting. Companies have an interest in how their results look, so the analyst's job is the relentless pursuit of an accurate picture, not the rote filling-in of boxes (Martin Fridson, Financial Statement Analysis).

He also flags a trap built into the system. Comparability is supposed to be a cornerstone of accounting rules, which tempts people to assume that any two companies reporting under the same rules can be lined up fairly. Fridson argues that this assumption is often wrong, and that good analysis begins after the usual questions have been answered (Martin Fridson, Financial Statement Analysis).

So treat industry averages and reported margins as a starting point, not a verdict. Ask what an accounting choice might be hiding before you trust the headline figure.

## Favor industries with durable demand

Some industries are simply built on steadier ground than others. Darrin Erickson organizes investing around the world's major sectors and the industries inside them, looking at what drives each industry's sales growth and profitability and which factors an investor should weigh before buying in (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing). The point of that survey is to steer money toward businesses whose demand is likely to last.

Erickson's whole approach starts from owning good businesses and weathering rough markets by thinking like a business owner, which only works if the underlying demand holds up over time (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing). An industry serving a need that grows reliably gives every company in it a tailwind. An industry whose demand is fading hands every company a headwind no amount of effort can fully overcome.

When you choose where to spend your attention, start with industries that have durable demand and real room to grow. It tilts the odds in your favor before you pick a single company.
