# Principles: Judging a Sector

This page teaches you how to judge a sector, which is a broad family of related industries that share a general line of business. It sits one level above the industry page in the app's flow. The economy pages tell you how the whole system is moving. The industry page tells you how one tight group of competitors earns its money. This page is the layer in between, where you decide which broad parts of the market to favor before you ever look inside an industry. The app groups the whole market into about eleven sectors, so a sector is wide on purpose, and you judge it by different things than you judge an industry.

## What a sector is, and how it differs from an industry

A sector is a broad group of related industries that share a general line of business. An industry is much narrower, a set of direct competitors selling close substitutes. The Technology sector, for example, holds the semiconductor industry, the software industry, and the hardware industry, which are related but not the same business. Darrin Erickson organizes the whole investing world this way, reviewing eleven sectors and the ninety-one global industries inside them, and he groups companies into sectors and industries based on common drivers of profitability and sales growth (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing).

So the difference is one of altitude. An industry is companies that take each other's customers. A sector is several such industries gathered under one umbrella because their fortunes rise and fall for broadly similar reasons. The app shows the same eleven broad sectors Erickson uses, such as Technology, Financial Services, Healthcare, Industrials, Consumer Cyclical, Consumer Defensive, Energy, Basic Materials, Utilities, Real Estate, and Communication Services.

Keep the two levels straight. When this page talks about a sector, it means the wide family. When you want the tight competitive detail, you drop down to the industry page.

## Sectors are the level of top-down allocation

Top-down analysis means you start with the whole economy, then choose sectors, then industries, then companies, in that order. The sector is the rung where you first commit money to one broad area over another. Reilly and his coauthors lay this out as a three-step process: study the overall market and economy, then the industry or sector, then the individual company (Frank Reilly, Investment Analysis). The economy points to which sectors to favor, the sectors point to industries, and the industries point to companies.

The reason to work in this order is simple. Most stocks struggle to do well when the broad area around them is declining, so it pays to decide where the wind is at your back before you pick a single name (Frank Reilly, Investment Analysis). Studies cited in that book found that a large part of any one firm's earnings change traces back to changes in aggregate corporate earnings and in its own sector, with the economy-wide effect being the largest (Frank Reilly, Investment Analysis).

This is the cleanest way the sector layer differs from the industry layer. The industry page is where you compare rivals. The sector layer is where you decide how to spread your money across the broad map first.

## Sectors take turns leading as the economy moves through its cycle

The business cycle is the repeated swing of the whole economy between expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. Economists call these short-run swings in output and employment the business cycle, and a stretch of falling output and rising unemployment is a recession (N. Gregory Mankiw, Macroeconomics). These swings are real but irregular, so they recur without being neatly predictable (N. Gregory Mankiw, Macroeconomics).

As the economy moves through that cycle, different sectors lead at different times. This taking of turns is called sector rotation, and Reilly treats it as a core tool of active management, where investors shift their weighting toward the sectors expected to do well next (Frank Reilly, Investment Analysis). Cyclical sectors, meaning sectors whose sales swing hard with the economy, such as consumer cyclical, industrials, materials, financials, and technology, tend to lead during expansions. Defensive sectors, meaning sectors whose sales hold up no matter the economy, such as consumer staples, utilities, and healthcare, hold up better during downturns.

So the same sector can look strong or weak depending only on where the economy stands in its cycle. Reading the cycle first tells you which broad areas are likely to lead next.

## Cyclical versus defensive is a sector-level trait

Whether a part of the market is cyclical or defensive is mostly decided at the sector level, not by any one company inside it. Howard Marks explains why. The economic cycle has a profound effect on some companies' sales and far less on others, and the difference tracks what they sell (Howard Marks, Mastering the Market Cycle). Sales of industrial raw materials, components, and big-ticket durable goods like cars and equipment respond directly to the cycle, because buyers can delay those purchases in hard times. Everyday necessities like food, beverages, and medicine barely move, because people buy them regardless of the economy (Howard Marks, Mastering the Market Cycle).

That is the heart of the cyclical and defensive split, and it lives at the sector level. A single well-run company cannot escape it. A great cement maker is still in a cyclical business, and a mediocre food company is still in a defensive one.

| Trait | What it means | Example sectors |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|
| Cyclical | Sales swing hard with the economy | Consumer Cyclical, Industrials, Basic Materials, Financials, Technology |
| Defensive | Sales hold up in downturns | Consumer Defensive (staples), Utilities, Healthcare |

For timing, a sector's sensitivity to the economy matters more than the quality of any one business inside it. That sensitivity is the first thing to read.

## Each sector has its own dominant macro lever

A macro lever is the one or two big forces that move a whole sector at once. Different sectors answer to different levers, so the question to ask about a sector is not just how the economy is doing but which specific force drives it. Interest rates and the credit cycle, meaning the swing between easy and tight lending, move the rate-sensitive sectors hardest. Credit is the giving of buying power in exchange for a promise to repay, which is debt, and whole stretches of the economy expand and contract with how freely that credit flows (Ray Dalio, Big Debt Crises). When credit is easy, financials lend more, real estate buyers can borrow, and utilities can finance their heavy assets cheaply. When credit tightens, those same sectors feel it first.

Other sectors answer to other levers. Commodity prices drive energy and materials, since their revenue is the price of what they dig up or refine. A strong dollar pressures large multinationals, whose foreign sales convert back into fewer dollars. Marks adds that the demand for expensive, financed goods rises and falls with the credit cycle on top of the ordinary economic cycle (Howard Marks, Mastering the Market Cycle).

| Sector area | Dominant macro lever |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Financials, Real Estate, Utilities | Interest rates and the credit cycle |
| Energy, Basic Materials | Commodity prices |
| Large multinationals across sectors | The exchange rate of the dollar |

Learn the one or two forces that move each sector. That is faster and more reliable than tracking every company in it.

## A sector is too broad to have a single competitive structure

This is the sharpest line between the sector page and the industry page. A sector spans several industries with very different economics, so it does not have one competitive structure or one moat. The semiconductor industry and the software industry both sit in Technology, yet they compete, earn, and defend themselves in completely different ways. Structure and moats are judged one level down, at the industry, because that is the only level narrow enough to share a single competitive shape.

So judge a sector by its macro drivers and its aggregate profitability, not by the five competitive forces. Aggregate profitability means whether the sector as a whole earns more on its capital than that capital costs. The cost of capital is the return investors give up by leaving their money in this business instead of an equally risky one elsewhere, an opportunity cost that no accountant ever deducts (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA). A sector that earns above that line is creating value across its broad base. One that earns below it is destroying value even while it looks busy (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA).

When you want the competitive detail, go to the industry page. The sector page answers a wider question: is this broad family worth your attention at all.

## A sector average can hide wide dispersion

Dispersion is the spread between the strongest and weakest parts inside a group. A sector aggregate is an average, and an average can hide a wide spread. A strong sector headline can mask weak industries buried inside it, and a dull headline can hide a few excellent ones.

Damodaran's work on relative valuation shows why the spread is so easy to miss. Multiples such as price to earnings or price to book vary widely across industries even within the same sector, depending on each industry's growth and risk (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). Pulling together one sector multiple and treating every industry in it as comparable is exactly the trap he warns against, because firms in the same broad business can still differ sharply in risk, growth, and cash flow (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation).

So read the spread across a sector's industries, not just the one headline number. The app's industries-within-a-sector matrix exists for this. A high sector average is a question, not an answer.

## Sectors are how you diversify and how money rotates between risk-on and risk-off

Sectors are the main blocks you diversify across, and they are also how money moves between risk-on, when investors favor cyclicals, and risk-off, when they retreat to defensives. Erickson advises owning a variety of businesses that earn across a range of economic sectors, because that spread smooths the combined earnings stream and the price swings that follow it (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing). He shows a diversified group of companies from different sectors producing far steadier earnings than a portfolio concentrated in a single sector such as financials (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing).

A deliberate sector tilt, meaning owning more of one sector than its share of the market, is an active bet, so size it on purpose. Bogle's honest counterpoint is that the simplest way to capture the market is to own the whole thing, weighted by size, and hold it, because owning the market is a winner's game while trying to beat it is a loser's game (John C. Bogle, The Little Book of Common Sense Investing). Every step away from owning everything is an active choice that can help or hurt. Over-concentration in one sector is a real risk, so any tilt should be chosen, not stumbled into.

Diversify across sectors first, and treat any overweight as a decision you can defend.

## A favored sector is a starting filter, not a buy

Even the best sector is not a buy. It is a filter that narrows the field. A favored sector still contains weak industries and overpriced companies, so the sector call only sets up the work that follows on the industry and company pages.

History makes the warning concrete. Whole sectors can become bubbles and then mean-revert, meaning prices stretched far above earnings eventually fall back toward normal. Shiller documents how the broad market reached extreme overpricing around 2000, led by technology, and warns that such stretched valuations are followed by long, painful corrections (Robert Shiller, Irrational Exuberance). Buying a sector just because it leads, near the top of its run, is how investors get hurt. Reilly makes the matching point at the company level: even within the best industry, the analyst may find that the best companies are not the best investments because they are already overvalued (Frank Reilly, Investment Analysis).

So treat a favored sector as the first cut, not the verdict. It tells you where to look, and then the industry page and the company work decide what, if anything, to actually buy.
