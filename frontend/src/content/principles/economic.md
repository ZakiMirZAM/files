# Principles: Reading the Economy

This page explains why the app studies the whole economy before it looks at any single industry or company. The economy is the environment that every business lives inside. A company sells into it, borrows from it, and competes within it, so its results are shaped by forces that are larger than the company itself. We start wide, with the economy, then narrow to industries, and only then to individual companies. The ideas below are the foundations you need first, and each one is drawn from a standard textbook so you can trust where it comes from.

## Start with the economy, then narrow down

An economy is not a mystery. It is just a large group of people dealing with one another as they go about their lives (N. Gregory Mankiw, Principles of Economics). Because the behavior of the whole economy reflects the behavior of all the individuals and firms inside it, the economy sets the conditions in which every company operates.

This is why the app works from the top down. We look first at the economy as a whole, then at the industry, then at the company. A business cannot escape the weather around it. If incomes are rising and credit is easy, most companies find selling easier. If the economy is shrinking, even well run companies struggle. Reading the economy first tells you which way the wind is blowing before you pick a specific sail.

## Supply and demand set prices and quantities

Supply and demand are the two forces that make markets work. They determine the quantity of each good produced and the price at which it is sold (N. Gregory Mankiw, Principles of Economics). A market is simply a group of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service. Buyers as a group set the demand, and sellers as a group set the supply.

The price you see in a market is the price that brings the plans of buyers and sellers into balance. This balance is called the equilibrium price, the price at which the amount people want to buy equals the amount sellers want to sell (Hal R. Varian, Intermediate Microeconomics). When demand rises or supply falls, prices climb. When demand falls or supply grows, prices drop. Every price in the economy, from a gallon of gasoline to an hour of labor, is set by this same tug of war. If you want to know how any event will affect the economy, think first about how it changes supply and demand.

## Every choice has a cost

Resources are scarce. Scarcity means society has limited resources and cannot produce everything people want (N. Gregory Mankiw, Principles of Economics). Because resources are limited, every choice means giving something up.

The cost of something is what you give up to get it. This is called the opportunity cost, the value of the best alternative you passed up (N. Gregory Mankiw, Principles of Economics). The clearest example for an investor is the use of capital. Money put into one company cannot also be put into another company, or into a safe bond, or kept in cash. The return you could have earned elsewhere is the true cost of any investment. This idea sits underneath everything the app measures, because a company only creates value when it earns more than the cost of the capital it ties up.

## Productivity drives long-run growth

When you travel around the world, you see large differences in living standards. The average income in a rich country can be roughly ten times the average income in a poor country (N. Gregory Mankiw, Principles of Economics). Over a century, average income in the United States, measured by real gross domestic product per person, has grown by about 2 percent per year, which is enough to roughly double living standards every 35 years. Here, gross domestic product, or GDP, means the total value of all the goods and services an economy produces.

What explains the difference between fast growing and slow growing countries is productivity, the amount of goods and services produced for each hour of work (N. Gregory Mankiw, Principles of Economics). A nation gets richer over time when its workers produce more per hour. The same logic holds for long-run investment returns. Real growth in output is what ultimately funds rising profits and rising wages across the whole market. The app pays attention to where in the world economic wealth is most likely to be created in the future, because that is where durable growth tends to live (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing).

## The business cycle moves in stages

Output does not grow smoothly. On average, real GDP grows over time, but this long-run average hides the fact that growth speeds up in some years and slows or turns negative in others (N. Gregory Mankiw, Macroeconomics). When output falls and unemployment rises, the economy is in a recession. Economists call these short-run swings in output and employment the business cycle.

The cycle is real, but it is not regular or easy to predict. Recessions are as irregular as they are common (N. Gregory Mankiw, Macroeconomics). A simple way to picture the cycle is in four stages.

| Stage | What is happening |
|---|---|
| Expansion | Output and employment are rising. |
| Peak | Growth reaches its high point and starts to flatten. |
| Contraction | Output falls and unemployment rises (a recession). |
| Trough | The low point, after which growth resumes. |

Different parts of the economy lead or lag at each stage. Some activities, such as home buying, respond strongly to the economic cycle, while others, such as buying food, barely respond at all (Howard Marks, Mastering the Market Cycle). This is the reason sectors take turns leading the market, and it is the engine behind the sector rotation the app tracks.

## Inflation eats purchasing power

Inflation is an overall increase in prices (N. Gregory Mankiw, Macroeconomics). When the New York Times rose from 15 cents to 3 dollars and a dozen eggs rose from 60 cents to 1.60 dollars over several decades, that was inflation at work. The rate of inflation is the percentage change in the overall level of prices.

Because a price is the rate at which money is exchanged for a good, inflation is closely tied to money. When the quantity of money grows quickly, the price level rises (N. Gregory Mankiw, Macroeconomics). The cost to you is lost purchasing power, since each dollar buys less than it did before. Inflation also pushes up interest rates. Lenders want to be paid back in dollars that still hold their value, so the stated, or nominal, interest rate tends to rise by about the expected inflation rate on top of the real return. This link is known as the Fisher effect (N. Gregory Mankiw, Macroeconomics), and it is the bridge from rising prices to the next principle.

## Interest rates are the discount rate behind every valuation

An interest rate is the price of money, the amount a borrower pays to use someone else's funds for a period of time. That price runs through every valuation in the app. To value a company, you estimate its future cash and then translate those future amounts into today's money. The rate used to do that translation is the discount rate, and the standard one is the weighted average cost of capital, the blended return that a firm's lenders and shareholders require (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation).

The discount rate has a floor called the risk-free rate, the return on a safe government bond, and good practice keeps a company's assumed long-run growth at or below the growth rate of the economy itself (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). This is exactly how the macro economy reaches into a single company's value. When rates rise, the present value of future cash falls, because each future dollar is discounted more heavily. The cost of capital is not an accounting trick. It is a real cost the business must cover, just like wages or materials, and a company only adds value when it earns more than that cost (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA).

## Debt fuels booms and forces busts

Credit is the giving of buying power, granted in exchange for a promise to pay it back, which is debt (Ray Dalio, Big Debt Crises). Credit is useful, because money borrowed and used productively can raise output and incomes. The trouble begins when borrowing outruns the ability to repay.

This creates the credit cycle. In the good times, lending is easy, asset prices rise, and confidence grows. The borrowing itself adds spending power, which lifts the economy further, which encourages still more borrowing. Then the process reverses. Losses make lenders cautious, credit tightens, and the same heavy borrowing that fueled the boom now forces a painful unwind (Ray Dalio, Big Debt Crises). The credit cycle is both highly responsive to the economy and highly influential on it, and its swings are powerful and extreme (Howard Marks, Mastering the Market Cycle). Watching how freely credit is flowing is one of the clearest reads on where the economy stands.

## Markets overshoot and then mean-revert

Prices are not always tied tightly to fundamentals. People often do not know with any accuracy what the right level of the market is, and few spend much time deciding whether it is over or underpriced (Robert J. Shiller, Irrational Exuberance). Into that gap steps emotion. When optimism feeds on itself, a rise in prices encourages more buying, which pushes prices higher still, in a feedback loop that can carry valuations far above what the underlying earnings justify. That is a speculative bubble.

Bubbles form when emotion outruns fundamentals, and they do not last. Over long stretches, prices and valuations tend to mean-revert, which means they drift back toward levels that fundamentals can support (Robert J. Shiller, Irrational Exuberance). For the app, the lesson is steadying. A high price today is not proof of value, and a low price after a panic is not proof of danger. Reading the economy helps you tell the difference between a price moved by real conditions and a price moved by mood.

## The economy is the soil

A great company planted in a shrinking economy still struggles, the way a strong seed planted in poor soil still fails to thrive. The most effective way to build wealth is through owning good businesses, but those businesses do not operate in a vacuum (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing). Market cycles are driven by the economic cycle, so the health of the economy sets the limit on what even the best company can achieve.

This is the final reason the app starts at the top. Long-run wealth comes from where economic growth is actually being created (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing). Within a healthy economy, the structure of an industry then shapes how much of that growth a company can keep, since firms in the same industry can position themselves very differently against their rivals (David Besanko, Economics of Strategy). That handoff from economy to industry is where this page ends and the industry page begins.

## How this connects to the app

These principles are the soil the rest of the app grows from. The economic page tracks the real signals behind them: growth and productivity, the business cycle, inflation, interest rates, and the flow of credit. Because different sectors lead and lag at different stages of the cycle, those readings feed directly into the sector-rotation matrix, which maps each phase of the economy to the sectors most likely to lead. Read the economy first, and the industry and company pages that follow will make far more sense.
