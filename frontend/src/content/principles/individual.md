# Principles: Analyzing a Single Company

This page teaches you how to look at one company and form a view of what it is really worth. It is the last and most detailed layer of the app's top-down flow. You started broad, with the economy, then sectors, then an industry, and now you arrive at a single business and the price of its shares. The job here is to separate two different things, the value of the business and the price the market is asking, and to do that with real numbers and a few durable ideas. The sections below are the foundation under the app's individual-stock tools.

## A share is a small piece of a real business

When you buy a share, you are not buying a ticker symbol or a number that wiggles on a screen. You are buying a fractional ownership stake, which means a small slice of an actual company, its factories, its brands, its customers, and its future profits. Owning shares in a company can be almost as good as starting the business yourself, but with far less effort (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing).

This is the starting point for everything else. Warren Buffett describes his own attitude as a partnership, where the people who hold shares are owners, not renters, and are entitled to think and be treated like owners of the whole business (Warren Buffett, The Essays of Warren Buffett). If you keep this in mind, you will judge a stock the way you would judge buying the entire company, by asking what the business earns and what it is likely to earn in the years ahead.

## Value is the cash a business will produce, brought back to today

Intrinsic value is the true worth of a business based on what it can actually do, not on its current market price. The cleanest definition comes from Buffett. Intrinsic value is the discounted present value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life (Warren Buffett, The Essays of Warren Buffett).

Aswath Damodaran builds the same idea more formally. The value of any asset is the present value of the expected future cash flows it generates, where present value means today's worth of money you will only receive later (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). Free cash flow is the cash a business has left over after it pays its costs, its taxes, and the spending needed to maintain and grow itself. That leftover cash, added up over the whole life of the business and pulled back to today, is what the business is worth.

Think of a company the way you would think of an apartment building. The building is worth the rent it will collect over the years, less the cost of keeping it up. A company is worth the cash it will throw off over its lifetime, less what it must spend to keep running and growing (Pat Dorsey, The Little Book That Builds Wealth).

## Three inputs drive value: cash flows, growth, and risk

If value is the present value of future cash, then only three things can change it. How much cash the business produces now, how fast that cash grows, and how risky the future cash is. Change any one of these and the value changes (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation).

Cash flows come first. Damodaran starts from a company's earnings, then adjusts for taxes and for reinvestment, the money put back into the business to fund future growth (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). Growth comes second, and it is the most critical and hardest input to estimate, because so much of a company's value sits in years that have not happened yet. Risk comes third, and it raises or lowers the rate you use to discount those future cash flows.

The practical lesson for a beginner is humility about your own estimates. A small change in the growth rate or the risk you assume can move the value a lot. Because of that, it pays to test a few reasonable cases rather than trusting a single confident number.

## A dollar later is worth less than a dollar now

Money has a time value. A dollar you will receive in five years is worth less than a dollar in your hand today, for two reasons. You could have invested today's dollar in the meantime, and the future dollar might never arrive. Present value is the tool that handles this. It is intuitive, simple to compute, and useful for everything from saving for a house to valuing a company (Aswath Damodaran, Applied Corporate Finance).

The discount rate is the percentage used to shrink a future dollar down to its value today. A cash flow that arrives right now needs no adjustment. A cash flow further out gets discounted more heavily. The discount rate reflects the riskiness of the estimated cash flows, with higher rates for riskier cash and lower rates for safer cash (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). A safe government bond is discounted gently. A young company with an uncertain future is discounted hard.

This is why two companies with the same expected profits can be worth very different amounts. The one whose cash is more certain, and arrives sooner, is worth more.

## Earning power matters more than one year's reported profit

A single year of reported earnings can mislead you. A good year might include a one-time gain, a bad year a one-time write-off, and most businesses move through cycles of strong and weak demand. What you want is earning power, the average level of profit a company can sustain as it exists today (Graham and Dodd, Security Analysis).

In the Graham and Dodd tradition, you estimate earnings power value by taking sustainable current earnings, normalized to strip out the unusual items and to smooth across the cycle, and treating that as a level the business can hold over time (Graham and Dodd lineage, Value Investing from Graham to Buffett and Beyond). This deliberately leans on the observable past, the real history of the company and its industry, rather than on optimistic forecasts of new products, because those forecasts have a poor track record.

For a beginner, the takeaway is simple. Before you judge a company by its latest profit, ask whether that profit is normal. Look at several years, remove the one-offs, and form a view of what the business earns in a typical year.

## Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact

Reported profit, also called net income, depends on accounting choices. Companies decide when to record revenue and how to spread out costs, and those choices are flexible. Financial statement analysis is partly an adversarial exercise, because financial statements often conceal more than they reveal, and a good analyst reads between the lines rather than just filling in ratios (Martin Fridson, Financial Statement Analysis: A Practitioner's Guide).

This is why you read all three financial statements together. The income statement shows reported profit. The balance sheet shows what the company owns and owes. The cash flow statement shows the actual cash that moved. Watch the gap between net income and cash flow from operations. Accruals are the accounting entries that record revenue or expenses before the cash actually changes hands. When profits keep rising but the cash behind them does not, the accruals are doing the heavy lifting, and that is a warning sign about earnings quality.

A beginner rule of thumb. Trust a profit more when it is backed by real cash. If a company reports steady earnings but its operating cash flow tells a weaker story, slow down and find out why.

## A moat protects future cash flows

A moat, or durable competitive advantage, is anything that lets a company keep earning high returns while fending off competitors, the way a moat protects a castle. It matters because it directly affects value. A company with a moat can reinvest its cash at high rates of return for a decade or more, while a company without one sees its returns collapse as soon as rivals move in (Pat Dorsey, The Little Book That Builds Wealth).

This is the link back to cash flows. When you buy shares of a company with a moat, you are buying a stream of cash that is shielded from competition for many years, which is worth more today than the same cash exposed to attack. Without a moat, high returns invite competition and fade fast. Hamilton Helmer frames the same idea as Power, the set of conditions that create persistent differential returns, and warns that a business without at least one such advantage is vulnerable (Hamilton Helmer, 7 Powers). The economics behind why advantages erode, and why a few firms hold them, is the subject of competitive analysis (David Besanko, Economics of Strategy).

For a beginner, ask Dorsey's deceptively simple question about any company you like. What stops a smart, well-funded competitor from taking these profits away?

## Management decides what happens to the cash

A business earns cash. Someone has to decide what to do with it. That decision is capital allocation, and it is one of the most important and most overlooked drivers of long-term returns (William Thorndike, The Outsiders). Management has roughly five choices. Reinvest in the existing business, make acquisitions, pay down debt, pay dividends, or buy back shares.

Done well, this compounds value. Tom Murphy's company grew from a fraction of CBS's size to three times its value over thirty years, largely through disciplined capital allocation rather than flashy expansion (William Thorndike, The Outsiders). Done poorly, it destroys value, often through overpriced acquisitions or buying back stock at the wrong price. Yet capital allocation gets far less attention than it deserves, and most investors judge it crudely, by whether a deal adds to reported earnings rather than by the actual cash returns it produces (Giroux, Capital Allocation). A useful test from Buffett. Has management created at least a dollar of value for every dollar of profit it kept rather than paid out (William Thorndike, The Outsiders)?

So when you study a company, study the people running it. Look at how they have spent the company's cash over the years, and whether those choices made each remaining share more valuable.

## Price versus value, and the margin of safety

Price is what the market asks today. Value is your estimate of what the business is worth. They are almost never the same. The price of a stock is practically always different from its intrinsic value, because markets swing between fear and greed and often fixate on the latest news (Modern Value Investing).

The margin of safety is the gap between the two, and it is your protection. The principle is to refuse to buy unless the price is clearly and substantially below your estimate of value (Warren Buffett, The Essays of Warren Buffett). If you think a business is worth ten dollars a share, you do not pay nine, you wait to pay six or seven. The bigger the discount, the smaller the chance you lose money even if your estimate turns out to be somewhat wrong (Modern Value Investing). The function of the margin of safety, in Graham's phrase, is to make an exact forecast of the future unnecessary.

This reframes the market itself. Treat its mood swings as offers you can take or ignore, not as a verdict on what your business is worth. Buy when fear pushes the price well under value, and let the difference work in your favor.

## Stay in your circle of competence, and define risk as losing money

Your circle of competence is the set of businesses you can actually understand well enough to judge. Intelligent investors draw a thick boundary and stick with companies inside it (Warren Buffett, The Essays of Warren Buffett). This is not a limit to apologize for. If you cannot explain how a company makes money and what protects it, you cannot estimate its value, and you have no business owning it. There is no penalty for the companies you skip.

Risk is the other idea most beginners get backwards. Most of the financial industry defines risk as volatility, how much a price bounces around, measured by statistics built from past prices (Modern Value Investing). But a falling price is not the same as a loss. Real risk is the permanent loss of capital, money you do not get back, and a temporary price drop on a sound business can even be an opportunity to buy more. Howard Marks puts understanding risk at the center of investing, because investing is entirely about dealing with an uncertain future, and the priced-in numbers say nothing about the real chance of loss (Howard Marks, The Most Important Thing).

Put together, these two ideas keep you safe. Own only what you understand, and judge each holding by the odds of a permanent loss, not by how nervous the daily price makes you feel.

## From principles to the app's tools

Each tool on the individual-stock page is one of these ideas made concrete. The discounted cash flow model is intrinsic value built directly, by projecting free cash flow and discounting it at a rate that reflects risk. Economic value added checks whether the company earns more than its cost of capital, which is the moat showing up in the numbers. The ratios and the statement trends are where you test earning power and earnings quality, watching the gap between profit and cash. The Graham number is a quick, conservative sanity check rooted in earning power and asset value. And the margin of safety ties it all together, by comparing the price the market is asking against the value you estimated, so you only buy when the gap is clearly in your favor.
