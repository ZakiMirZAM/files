# Principles: Comparing Companies Head to Head

This page teaches you how to rank the large companies inside one industry against each other. The industry page already judged the group as a whole, its structure and its demand. Here that work is done, so the industry and the economy are held still. Everything you are about to compare is the company itself, not the weather around it. When two rivals sell similar products into the same market and face the same forces, any gap between them comes from how each company is run and how each is priced. This page is about that act of comparison: lining up true rivals, reading their multiples, ranking them on quality, and knowing where relative comparison stops being enough.

## Comparison only works between true rivals

Comparative analysis means ranking companies that genuinely compete, so that the differences you find are about the businesses and not their surroundings. When you hold the industry and the economy constant, a company that scores better than its peers is better for reasons inside its own walls. That is the whole point of comparing within one industry rather than across the market.

This only works if the companies really belong together. A comparable company is one with cash flows, growth, and risk similar to the company you are studying (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). Damodaran points out that analysts routinely call firms in the same business comparable even when they are not truly alike. So the first job is not to compute anything. It is to make sure the names on your list are real rivals competing for the same customers under the same conditions.

Get that right and the ranking carries meaning. Get it wrong and every number that follows is comparing things that were never the same.

## Compare like with like

The companies on a comparison must share the same economics, or the ranking is just decoration. Two businesses can sit in the same broad sector and still differ so much in risk, growth, and cash flow that putting their numbers side by side tells you nothing. Damodaran warns that the ease of relative valuation is also its danger: you can pull together a multiple and a group of so-called comparable firms in minutes, and if those firms differ on the things that matter, the comparison quietly breaks (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation).

The fix is to insist on genuine likeness before you trust a single ratio. If one company carries heavy debt and another carries none, a price-based comparison can make the indebted one look cheap when it is not. If one is growing twice as fast as the rest, a low price tag may be perfectly fair rather than a bargain.

So before you read a leaderboard, ask whether the companies on it truly share the same business. If they do not, you are not ranking rivals. You are lining up numbers that happen to look similar.

## Relative valuation in one line

Relative valuation means estimating what a company is worth by looking at how similar companies are priced right now (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). The tool it uses is the multiple. A multiple is a price expressed as a number of times some figure such as earnings, sales, or book value. A company priced at twelve times its earnings has a price-earnings multiple of twelve.

Because raw stock prices cannot be compared across companies, you standardize them into multiples first, then compare the multiples across rivals (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). The simple idea behind the whole exercise is that comparable businesses should trade at comparable multiples. So a company priced far below its peers may be cheap, and one priced far above them may be expensive.

The word that matters is *may*. A low multiple is a question, not an answer. It tells you a company is priced lower than its rivals. It does not yet tell you whether that is a bargain or a warning.

## A multiple is shorthand for fundamentals

A multiple is not a verdict on its own. It is shorthand for three things underneath the price: how fast the company can grow its cash, how risky those cash flows are, and how much cash the business actually generates (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). Companies with higher growth, lower risk, and stronger cash generation deserve higher multiples than companies with lower growth, higher risk, and weaker cash. The differences in multiples across rivals are usually justified by these differences in fundamentals.

This is why a cheaper-looking multiple is not automatically a cheaper company. Damodaran gives the plain warning: if you ignore these variables, you might conclude that a stock trading at eight times earnings is cheaper than one trading at twelve times earnings, when the real reason for the gap is that the second company is growing faster. The same trap appears with price-to-book multiples, where the higher one often reflects a higher return on capital rather than overpricing (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation).

So before you call a peer cheap, control for the differences. Ask whether the low multiple is explained by slower growth, more risk, or weaker cash flow. If it is, the company is priced correctly, not on sale.

| What deserves a higher multiple | Why |
|---------------------------------|-----|
| Higher expected growth | More future cash to pay for today |
| Lower risk | Investors accept a smaller reward for safer cash |
| Higher return on capital | Each dollar reinvested produces more |
| Stronger, steadier cash generation | More of the earnings turn into real, distributable cash |

## Rank on quality, not only price

The cheapest company is not the best company if it is the worse business. Price tells you what you pay. Quality tells you what you are getting. To rank rivals on quality, the cleanest test is whether each one earns a return on invested capital above its cost of capital. Return on invested capital is the profit a company earns for every dollar tied up in the business. Cost of capital is the return investors could earn elsewhere on an equally risky bet, so it is what the company must beat just to break even in real terms (Aswath Damodaran, Applied Corporate Finance).

Damodaran frames this as a hurdle rate, the minimum return an investment must clear, and the rule that a business creates value only when its return beats that hurdle (Aswath Damodaran, Applied Corporate Finance). Bennett Stewart calls the surplus economic profit, or economic value added, which is the profit left after charging the business for the full cost of the capital it uses. A company that only earns its cost of capital creates nothing. It is worth just the money put into it (Bennett Stewart, Best-Practice EVA). The better company in a peer group is the one earning a wider spread over its cost of capital, not the one with the lowest price tag.

So put two columns next to each other. One ranks the rivals by how cheap they look. The other ranks them by how far their returns clear their cost of capital. The winner of a comparison should look good on the second column, not only the first.

## The moat is what separates rivals over time

A moat is a structural advantage that protects a company's high returns from competition for a long time. Among peers that look alike today, the one with a moat is the one that stays ahead. High returns always attract rivals, and for most companies those returns get competed away quickly. A moat is what slows that down (Pat Dorsey, The Little Book That Builds Wealth).

Dorsey makes the comparison directly. Take two companies growing at the same rate and using the same capital. The one with a moat can keep reinvesting at high returns for a decade or more, because competitors are held off. The one without a moat watches its returns fall as soon as rivals move in. The moated company is worth more today and deserves a higher multiple, because its stream of profits is protected for far longer (Pat Dorsey, The Little Book That Builds Wealth). Hamilton Helmer describes concrete sources of this advantage, such as scale economies, where the larger company spreads its fixed costs over more output and a smaller rival simply cannot match its costs (Hamilton Helmer, 7 Powers).

So when two rivals show the same returns right now, the deciding question is who can defend those returns. The one whose advantage will last is the better business, even if the scoreboard looks even today.

## Capital allocation explains why peers diverge

Capital allocation is how a company chooses to use the cash it produces. It can reinvest in the business, buy back its own shares, pay dividends, or buy other companies. Over a decade these choices can pull two near-identical rivals far apart, even when they sell the same products into the same market.

William Thorndike tells the clearest version of this. When Tom Murphy took over Capital Cities, the rival broadcaster CBS was sixteen times its size. Thirty years later Capital Cities was three times more valuable than CBS. Same industry, opposite outcome. CBS chased size, diversifying into unrelated businesses and overpaying for trophy assets, while Murphy stayed focused, ran his stations well, and bought more only when the returns were high (William Thorndike, The Outsiders). Giroux shows the same skill at scale: the average company allocates capital poorly, and he points to General Electric, which destroyed enormous shareholder wealth by deploying capital at low or negative returns and repeating the same mistakes (Giroux, Capital Allocation).

So when you compare rivals, watch how each one spends. Two companies with matching products can end up worlds apart purely because one allocates its cash with discipline and the other does not.

## Normalize before you rank

Normalizing means adjusting reported numbers so that companies are measured on the same basis before you compare them. Accounting choices differ from one company to the next, so the raw figures on a scorecard are not automatically comparable. Martin Fridson calls this the adversarial nature of financial reporting: statements often conceal more than they reveal, and a company has its own interest in how its results look (Martin Fridson, Financial Statement Analysis).

Fridson also flags the trap built into the system. Comparability is supposed to be a cornerstone of accounting rules, which tempts people to assume that any two companies reporting under the same rules can be lined up fairly. He argues that this is often wrong, and that good analysis begins after the standard questions have been answered (Martin Fridson, Financial Statement Analysis). Damodaran reaches the same place from the valuation side: a multiple has to be defined and measured the same way for every company in the group, because differences in accounting can make similar firms look very different (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). He also notes that one-off gains or losses should be stripped out so that the earnings you compare reflect what the business normally earns.

So adjust first, then rank. Put every company on the same footing before you trust the order they fall into. A scorecard built on unadjusted numbers can rank the wrong company first.

## Relative cheap is not absolute cheap

Winning a comparison only means a company is cheaper or better than its rivals. It does not mean the company is actually a good buy. The whole peer group can be overpriced at the same time, and in that case the cheapest of the bunch is still expensive. Damodaran spells out the limit: when you value one company relative to its peers, you are assuming the market has priced the group correctly on average, even though it may have mispriced every one of them (Aswath Damodaran, Investment Valuation). A stock can look undervalued against its peers and still be overvalued on its own merits if the whole sector is too high.

This is why the winner of a comparison still needs an independent check on what it is actually worth. Howard Marks puts price at the center of the decision: no asset is so good that it cannot become a bad investment if you pay too high a price, and few are so bad that they cannot be good investments bought cheap enough (Howard Marks, The Most Important Thing). The protection is a margin of safety, the gap between what you pay and what the business is worth. The bigger the discount to intrinsic value, the smaller the chance of permanent loss (Modern Value Investing). And the goal is to own the best business in the group, not merely the least bad one. Darrin Erickson's whole approach is to separate a market into the best opportunities and everything else, then own only the best (Darrin Erickson, Global Investing).

So treat a leaderboard as a first filter, not a buy signal. The company that wins your comparison still has to pass an absolute test: is it a genuinely good business, and is its price below its real value?

## How this connects to the app's tools

These ideas are the engine behind the comparative surface. The leaderboard ranks an industry's companies against each other, so read it knowing the names on it must be true rivals measured on the same basis. The company scorecard is where you normalize and compare quality side by side, watching return on invested capital against the cost of capital and asking who has the durable moat and the better capital allocation. The value score reads a company's multiples as shorthand for its fundamentals, so a low score is a question to investigate, not a verdict. And the per-company discounted-cash-flow and economic-value-added estimates give you the absolute check that relative comparison cannot: they tell you whether the winner of the comparison is also worth its price, so you aim to own the best business at a fair price rather than the least bad one in an expensive crowd.
