# DuPont Interest Burden

## Part 1: Definition

The DuPont interest burden measures how much of a business's operating profit survives after interest is paid. Operating profit is what a business earns from running its core operations, before interest and tax. Pre-tax profit is what remains after interest is subtracted but before tax. The ratio is pre-tax profit divided by operating profit, so it is the share of operating profit left once lenders are paid. A higher ratio means a lighter interest drag and more profit kept for owners. A business with no debt has a ratio of 1.0, because no operating profit is lost to interest. In the industry view this lever is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how much of the whole industry's operating profit is left after interest. An industry-wide interest burden of 0.85 means the typical company in that industry keeps 85 cents of each dollar of operating profit after paying lenders.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can carry no debt inside an industry that funds itself heavily with borrowing. The industry number tells the reader how much the field as a whole gives up to lenders, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high, near 1.0 | An industry that borrows little, so almost all operating profit survives to owners. |
| Around the market average | A normal industry with an ordinary interest drag. |
| Persistently low | A heavily indebted industry where interest claims a large share of operating profit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`DuPont Interest Burden = Pre-tax Income ÷ Operating Income`

Pre-tax income is operating profit after interest is subtracted. Operating income is profit from core operations before interest and tax. The industry figure is built by combining each company's pre-tax income and operating income across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes the interest drag on where the industry's actual profit sits, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The interest burden is one of five levers that together build return on equity. The full chain reads as tax burden, then interest burden, then operating margin, then asset turnover, then financial leverage, and multiplying all five gives return on equity. In plain words, the chain starts with how well the business runs its operations, then strips out the drag of interest and tax, then accounts for how hard the assets work and how much the owner money is geared. The interest burden is the lever that captures the cost of debt. A higher interest burden lifts return on equity, because less operating profit is lost to lenders. This lever interacts with financial leverage, because heavy borrowing raises the gearing lever but lowers the interest burden lever through the interest it creates.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The interest burden isolates one specific force on an industry's profitability, the cost of its borrowing. Breaking return on equity into its levers lets the reader see why one industry rewards owners more than another, rather than seeing only the combined result. When an industry keeps a high share of its operating profit after interest, it carries little debt cost and its returns flow cleanly to owners. When an industry keeps a low share, interest is claiming a large part of what the business earns before owners see anything.

This framing matters because the interest lever reveals how much of an industry's financing risk is showing up in its profit. A field with a low interest burden is exposed to rising rates, because a larger slice of its operating profit already goes to lenders and would grow if borrowing costs climbed. Separating the interest lever tells the reader how much room the field has between what it earns and what it owes its lenders, before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is how heavily the industry funds itself with debt. An industry that borrows little, often because its profits are volatile or its assets are light, keeps almost all of its operating profit and shows an interest burden near 1.0. An industry that funds itself with heavy debt, such as utilities or real estate, gives up a large share of operating profit to interest and shows a lower figure. This structural trait changes slowly, which is why an industry's interest burden often stays in a similar range for years.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A rise in interest rates increases the cost of the industry's borrowing and lowers the figure, even if debt levels are unchanged. A wave of debt-funded buybacks across the industry adds interest and lowers the burden. A fall in operating profit during a recession, with interest costs fixed, lowers the figure sharply because the same interest now claims a larger share of a smaller profit. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries. An industry with a high interest burden borrows little and keeps most of its operating profit, while an industry with a low interest burden gives up a large share to lenders and is more exposed to financing costs.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the market, near 1.0 | A lightly borrowed field where almost all operating profit reaches owners. |
| Near the market | An ordinary interest drag with no special financing feature. |
| Far below the market | A heavily indebted field where interest claims a large share of operating profit. |

Compare like with like. Industries that fund themselves on borrowing by their nature, such as utilities or real estate, will show low interest burdens for structural reasons, so they should be compared with similarly financed fields rather than with capital-light ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate interest burden over several years. A high and steady line is the sign of an industry that funds itself mostly with owner money and gives little to lenders. A line that drifts down over many years is a warning that the industry is taking on more debt cost, whether from rising borrowing, rising interest rates, or weakening operating profit.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A temporary dip during a recession, as operating profit falls while interest stays fixed, is a cyclical swing that should ease as profits recover. A steady decline that continues through good years is the more serious signal, because it points to a permanent rise in the industry's debt cost. A drop that coincides with rising rates rather than rising debt is a sign that the field's existing borrowing has simply become more expensive. The trend matters more than any single year, because the durability of a financing position reveals itself over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DuPont Tax Burden

**Why read the two together.** The interest burden and the tax burden are the two levers in the return on equity chain that strip profit away rather than create it. The interest burden shows how much operating profit survives interest, and the tax burden shows how much of what remains survives tax. Reading the two together shows the full drag that financing and tax place on the industry's operating profit before it reaches owners.

**What the combination tells you.** When both levers are high, little of the industry's operating profit is lost to interest or tax, so the field's return on equity rests mainly on its business and its gearing. When the interest burden is low but the tax burden is high, the field is lightly taxed yet heavily weighed down by borrowing costs, which points to leverage as the constraint. The pairing tells the reader which of the two drags is shaping the industry's returns.

### Financial Leverage

**Why read the two together.** Financial leverage and the interest burden are two sides of how an industry uses debt. Leverage shows how much the field stretches owner money across its assets, and the interest burden shows the profit cost of the borrowing behind that stretch. Reading the two together shows both the benefit of gearing and its price in the same view.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry has high financial leverage and a low interest burden, its borrowing is lifting return on equity through gearing while quietly eating operating profit through interest. When high leverage sits with an interest burden still near 1.0, the field is gearing cheaply, often because rates are low or debt is modest relative to profit. The pairing tells the reader whether the industry's use of debt is adding more to returns than it is costing in interest, which decides how safe the gearing really is.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between debt-free and heavily borrowed members, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. One-time items below operating profit, such as gains, losses, or write-downs, can distort the figure for a single year, so it must be read across several years.

The figure also says nothing about business quality on its own. A low interest burden lowers return on equity, but heavy borrowing may still be sensible if the gearing lever it creates adds more than the interest costs. An industry with a high interest burden simply borrows little, which is neither good nor bad until its returns and risks are weighed together. The metric isolates the interest lever, not whether the field's overall use of debt is wise, so it is only one piece of the return on equity picture and means little on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's interest burden above or below the broad market, and has the gap held for several years or only recently?
2. How much operating profit is the industry giving up to interest, and how much room remains before rising rates would strain it?
3. Is a low interest burden the result of structural heavy borrowing, such as utilities, or of recent debt taken on?
4. Is a falling figure driven by rising rates on existing debt, by new borrowing, or by falling operating profit in a downturn?
5. Does the borrowing behind a low interest burden add more to return on equity through gearing than it costs in interest?
6. Is a low figure shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
7. Would the industry's returns hold up if interest rates rose and pushed the interest burden lower?
8. Are one-time items below operating profit distorting the figure in any single year of the series?
