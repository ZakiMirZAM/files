# R&D Intensity

## Part 1: Definition

Research and development, shortened to R&D, is the money a business spends inventing new products and improving existing ones. R&D intensity measures that spending as a share of sales. It answers a simple question: out of every dollar of sales, how much does the business plow back into inventing the future? An R&D intensity of 15 percent means the business spends fifteen cents of every sales dollar on research and development.

In the industry view this number is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how innovation-driven the whole industry is. A high industry figure marks an industry where survival depends on constant invention, such as pharmaceuticals or semiconductors. A low figure marks an industry that competes on price, scale, or service rather than on new technology.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | An innovation-driven industry where companies must keep inventing to compete. |
| Moderate | An industry where research matters but is not the main battleground. |
| Persistently low | An industry that competes on cost, scale, or service rather than on new technology. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`R&D Intensity = Research and Development Expense ÷ Revenue`

Research and development expense is the yearly cost of inventing and improving products. Revenue is total yearly sales. The industry figure is built by combining each company's research spending and sales across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual research money is being spent, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

Accounting treats research spending as a cost subtracted from this year's profit, even though its payoff arrives in future years. This means heavy research lowers reported profit today while building products that earn later. The split between research spending and current profit shows how much of the industry's earnings power is being invested in tomorrow rather than collected today.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

R&D intensity reveals what kind of contest the industry is. In a high-research industry, the winners are the companies that invent the next breakthrough, and standing still means falling behind. In a low-research industry, the contest is fought on cost, distribution, or brand, and heavy research spending would be wasted effort.

This spending connects directly to competitive structure. Research can build a moat, because a patent, a hard-won technology, or years of accumulated know-how keeps competitors out and protects the industry's returns. Research can also be a treadmill, where every company must keep spending just to stay even, and the spending protects no one. Judging the industry's research intensity first tells the reader whether innovation is the source of advantage or merely the cost of staying in the game, before any single stock is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the nature of the industry's products. Industries whose products improve rapidly, such as drugs, chips, and software, must spend heavily on research or lose relevance. Industries whose products change slowly, such as utilities or basic materials, spend little because there is little to invent. This pattern is structural and changes slowly.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. In a downturn, companies sometimes cut research to protect short-term profit, which lowers the industry figure but can quietly weaken the industry's future. A burst of new technology can push the whole industry to spend more to keep up. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the research budgets of the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

R&D intensity is most useful when ranked against other industries, because it sorts the economy into innovation-driven and non-innovation-driven groups. A figure that is normal for a drug or chip industry would be enormous for a retailer or a utility, and would signal waste rather than strength.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High intensity, technology-driven | Normal for the industry, where invention is the field of competition. |
| Low intensity, mature industry | Normal, because the industry competes on cost, scale, or service. |
| Rising intensity across the industry | Either a new technology race, or research becoming a heavier cost of staying even. |

Compare like with like. High research spending means a healthy innovation engine in one industry and would mean misdirected money in another, so the number carries meaning only against industries of the same kind.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate R&D intensity over several years. A steady high line means the industry's innovation contest is stable. A line that rises over many years can mean the industry is racing to keep up with faster technology, which may protect returns or may simply raise the cost of competing. A line that falls over years can mean the industry is maturing and inventing less.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A dip in research during a recession that recovers afterward is a cyclical swing, often a sign companies are protecting short-term profit. A steady decline that does not recover can mean the industry is under-investing in its future, which may boost reported profit today at the cost of weaker products later. The history of one consumer staples company shows that cutting brand and product investment lifted margins for a few years before market share losses forced the spending back up.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Gross Profit Ratio

**Why read the two together.** R&D intensity shows how much the industry invests in invention, while the gross profit ratio shows the pricing power its products command. Successful research should show up as high gross margins, because differentiated products can be sold at a premium. Reading them together for the industry tests whether its research is actually producing an advantage.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that spends heavily on research and earns high gross margins is converting invention into pricing power, the sign of a real moat. An industry that spends heavily on research but earns thin gross margins is on a treadmill, spending to stay even without winning pricing power. The pairing separates research that builds a barrier from research that merely keeps the industry in the race.

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** R&D intensity shows how much the industry invests in new products, while revenue growth shows whether those products are finding customers. Research is a bet on future sales, so pairing it with growth checks whether the bet is paying off. Reading them together for the industry shows whether its innovation is translating into expansion.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry with heavy research and strong revenue growth is inventing products that customers want, the most attractive backdrop for an investment. Heavy research with weak growth is a warning that the spending is not producing winners. The pairing turns a spending figure into a judgment about whether the industry's innovation is creating value or burning it.

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Research spending is a large cost that sits inside the operating income ratio, which measures the industry's profit from its core business. A high-research industry carries that cost every year, so its operating margin reflects both its pricing power and its research burden. Reading them together for the industry shows how the cost of innovation weighs on its core profitability.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that spends heavily on research and still earns a healthy operating margin has products strong enough to cover the cost of inventing them. An industry whose operating margin is thin because research eats it up is in a costly contest where invention barely pays for itself. The pairing shows whether the industry's innovation is affordable or whether it is a drag the industry cannot escape.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. R&D intensity measures how much the industry spends, not how well that money is spent, and a dollar of brilliant research counts the same as a dollar wasted. A high figure can reflect either a winning innovation engine or a wasteful one, so the raw number cannot tell good research from bad.

The figure also mixes spending with timing. Because research is charged against profit today but pays off in future years, an industry that cuts research can look more profitable for a while even as it weakens its future. A strong figure tells the reader how much the industry is investing, not whether that investment will earn its keep, so it must be paired with judgment about whether the research is producing real advantage.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's research intensity normal for its kind of business, or unusually high or low for its peers?
2. Does the industry's research build a lasting moat, or is it a treadmill where everyone must spend just to stay even?
3. Does heavy research show up as high gross margins, or is the spending failing to win pricing power?
4. Is the industry's innovation translating into revenue growth, or is the spending producing few winners?
5. Is a falling intensity a cyclical cut to protect profit, or a structural sign the industry is under-investing in its future?
6. Is the industry figure shaped by a few dominant members, or shared across most companies?
7. Can the industry's core profitability carry its research cost, or does research eat up its operating margin?
8. Are there signs that a new technology is forcing the whole industry to spend more just to keep up?
