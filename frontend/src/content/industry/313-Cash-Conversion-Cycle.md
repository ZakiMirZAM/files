# Cash Conversion Cycle

## Part 1: Definition

The cash conversion cycle measures how many days a business waits between paying for the goods it sells and collecting the cash from its customers. During that wait, the business has money tied up in inventory and in unpaid customer bills. A short cycle means cash comes back quickly. A long cycle means cash sits trapped in the business for a long time before it returns.

In the industry view this number is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how long the whole industry ties up cash in its day-to-day operations. A short industry cycle marks an industry that collects fast and funds little of its own operation. A long cycle marks an industry that must finance large inventories and slow-paying customers for months at a time.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Short or negative | The industry collects cash quickly, sometimes before it even pays suppliers. |
| Moderate | A normal gap between paying for goods and collecting from customers. |
| Long | The industry ties up cash in inventory and unpaid bills for an extended time. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Cash Conversion Cycle = Days Inventory + Days Receivables − Days Payables`

Days inventory is how long goods sit before they are sold. Days receivables is how long customers take to pay. Days payables is how long the business takes to pay its own suppliers. The industry figure is built by combining each company's inventory, receivables, and payables across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual cash is tied up, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The cycle can be negative. When an industry collects from customers before it has to pay its suppliers, suppliers effectively fund its operations, and the cycle drops below zero. A negative cycle is a sign of real bargaining strength over both customers and suppliers.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The cash conversion cycle shows how much of its own money the industry must commit just to run day to day. A long cycle means the industry constantly funds inventory and customer credit, money that could otherwise be invested or returned to owners. A short or negative cycle means the industry runs on other people's money and frees up cash for better uses.

This timing connects to bargaining power, which is part of competitive structure. An industry that can make customers pay quickly and suppliers wait shows strength on both sides of its trade, the mark of weak suppliers and dependent buyers. An industry forced to hold large inventory and grant long credit shows the opposite, a weaker position in its supply chain. Judging the industry's cash cycle first tells the reader how much working capital the industry must carry and how much power it holds over its trading partners, before any single stock is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the industry's place in its supply chain and the nature of its goods. Industries that sell perishable or fast-moving goods for cash run short cycles. Industries that build complex products, hold large parts inventories, or sell to slow-paying business customers run long cycles. This pattern is structural and changes slowly.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. In a downturn, customers pay more slowly and inventory builds up as sales weaken, which lengthens the industry cycle. A push across the industry to manage working capital more tightly can shorten it. A few very large companies with strong bargaining power can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The cash conversion cycle is most useful when ranked against other industries, because what counts as long or short depends entirely on the kind of business. A long cycle is normal for heavy manufacturing and would be alarming in a fast-food or grocery industry.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Negative cycle | Strong bargaining power. Suppliers and customers fund the industry's operation. |
| Short cycle | Cash returns quickly, leaving little tied up in working capital. |
| Long cycle | The industry funds large inventory and slow customer payment for months. |

Compare like with like. A short cycle means efficiency in one industry and would be impossible in another, so the number carries meaning only against industries that trade the same way.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate cash conversion cycle over several years. A steady line means the industry's working-capital rhythm is stable. A line that lengthens over many years is a warning that cash is getting trapped for longer, either because inventory is moving slower, customers are paying later, or the industry is losing bargaining power over its suppliers.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A lengthening that happens in a recession, when customers stretch payments and inventory builds, is a cyclical swing that recovers afterward. A slow, steady lengthening that does not recover points to a structural shift, such as the industry weakening in its supply chain. A shortening trend can signal that the industry is gaining power over its trading partners or running its operations more tightly.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio is a snapshot of short-term assets against short-term bills, while the cash conversion cycle measures how many days the industry waits to turn its operations back into cash. A short cycle lets an industry run safely on a low current ratio, because cash returns fast. Reading them together for the industry explains whether a thin liquidity buffer is risky or normal.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry has a low current ratio but a short or negative cash cycle, the low buffer is a sign of efficiency, not danger, because cash flows back before bills come due. When an industry has a low current ratio and a long cycle, cash is trapped while bills mount, a genuine warning. The pairing turns a bare liquidity snapshot into a judgment about whether the industry's cash actually flows safely.

### FCF To Revenue

**Why read the two together.** The cash conversion cycle shows how much cash the industry ties up in working capital, while free cash flow to revenue shows how much of its sales finally reach owners as cash. Money trapped in inventory and unpaid bills is money that cannot become free cash flow. Reading them together for the industry shows how working capital affects its true cash generation.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry with a short cycle and strong free cash flow turns its sales into owner cash efficiently. An industry with a long and lengthening cycle leaks cash into working capital, which holds back its free cash flow even when profits look healthy. The pairing reveals whether the industry's reported profit is actually arriving as cash or getting stuck along the way.

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** Growth consumes working capital, because a growing industry must build more inventory and extend more customer credit before the new sales pay off. The cash conversion cycle shows how cash-hungry that growth is. Reading them together for the industry shows whether its expansion funds itself or drains cash.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that grows quickly with a short cash cycle can expand without straining for funds, because cash returns before the next round of spending. An industry that grows quickly with a long cycle must keep pouring cash into working capital just to keep up, which can leave fast growth starved of funds. The pairing shows whether the industry's growth is self-funding or cash-draining.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. The cycle is built from inventory, receivables, and payables measured at a single point in time, which can be distorted by seasonal swings, so a snapshot taken at the wrong moment can mislead. This is a particular risk in industries with heavy seasonal patterns in sales and stock.

The figure also says nothing about profit. A short cycle is efficient but worthless if the industry sells at a loss, and a long cycle can be perfectly acceptable in a high-margin industry. The cash conversion cycle describes how the industry manages its working capital, not whether the underlying business is sound, so it must be paired with profitability and cash measures to mean anything.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's cash cycle short, long, or negative, and is that normal for its kind of business?
2. Does a negative or short cycle reflect real bargaining power over suppliers and customers?
3. Does a low current ratio in this industry sit alongside a short cycle, making the thin buffer safe?
4. Is a lengthening cycle a cyclical effect of a downturn, or a structural loss of supply-chain power?
5. Is the industry's growth self-funding through a short cycle, or cash-draining through a long one?
6. Is the industry figure shaped by a few dominant members, or shared across most companies?
7. Is the snapshot distorted by seasonal swings in inventory or customer payments?
8. Does cash trapped in the industry's working capital hold back its free cash flow despite healthy profits?
