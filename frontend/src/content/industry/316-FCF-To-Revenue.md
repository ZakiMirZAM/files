# FCF To Revenue

## Part 1: Definition

Free cash flow to revenue measures how much of every sales dollar a business turns into spare cash for its owners. Free cash flow is the cash left after the business pays its running costs and the spending needed to keep its assets in working order. Revenue is total sales. The ratio is free cash flow divided by revenue. It shows the share of sales that becomes cash an owner could take out, pay as a dividend, use to buy back shares, or reinvest by choice. In the industry view this ratio is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how efficiently the whole industry converts its sales into owner cash. An industry-wide free cash flow to revenue of 15 percent means the typical sales dollar in that industry leaves about 15 cents of spare cash after necessary spending.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can be cash-generative inside an industry that consumes cash to grow. The industry number tells the reader whether the field itself is a cash machine or a cash sink, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A cash-rich industry that needs little reinvestment to keep its sales, often a sign of strong pricing or light asset needs. |
| Around the market average | A normal industry with ordinary cash conversion. |
| Persistently low or negative | A cash-hungry industry that must plow most of its sales back into the business, or one with thin margins. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`FCF To Revenue = Free Cash Flow ÷ Revenue`

Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus the spending needed to maintain and grow the asset base, called capital expenditure. Revenue is total sales. The industry figure is built by combining each company's free cash flow and revenue across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual cash is being generated, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

Free cash flow to revenue is a cash-based cousin of the profit margin. Reported profit can be lifted by accounting choices, but free cash flow is harder to dress up, because it is the cash that actually arrives after real spending. An industry with strong free cash flow to revenue keeps a large slice of its sales as genuine cash. An industry with thin or negative free cash flow to revenue must spend heavily on its assets just to stand still, or earns too little on each sale to leave cash behind.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

Free cash flow to revenue is one of the clearest reads on the true economic quality of an industry. When an industry turns a large share of its sales into spare cash year after year, its business model is efficient and does not demand constant heavy reinvestment to keep running. When an industry generates little free cash from its sales, it must pour money back into assets just to maintain its position, which leaves less for owners. Cash, not reported profit, is what ultimately rewards owners, so the cash a field generates is a stronger test than its accounting earnings.

This framing matters because cash generation shapes how an industry funds itself and survives stress. A field that throws off cash can pay its owners, repay debt, and weather a downturn without raising money on poor terms. A field that consumes cash depends on outside funding to keep going, which becomes dangerous when credit tightens. Judging the industry's cash conversion first tells the reader whether the field can fund itself, before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is how much the industry must spend on its assets to earn its sales. An industry that is light on physical assets, such as branded software, converts a large share of sales into cash because it needs little capital spending. An industry that is heavy on physical assets, such as telecom networks or heavy manufacturing, converts a smaller share, because much of its cash must go back into plant and equipment. This structural trait changes slowly, which is why an industry's cash conversion often stays in a similar range for years.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A wave of expansion spending across the industry can depress free cash flow even when the underlying business is healthy, because capital expenditure rises before the new sales arrive. A recession can cut the figure as profits fall and unsold goods absorb cash. Swings in working capital, the cash tied up in inventory and customer payments, can push the figure around from year to year. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries. An industry with high free cash flow to revenue held for many years runs an efficient, self-funding model that rewards owners. An industry with thin or negative free cash flow to revenue depends on heavy reinvestment or outside money to keep going.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the market, stable | A light, cash-rich model with strong pricing and modest asset needs. |
| Near the market | Ordinary cash conversion with no special structural edge. |
| Far below the market or negative | A capital-hungry or thin-margin field that retains little cash from its sales. |

Compare like with like. Asset-heavy industries will always show lower free cash flow to revenue than asset-light ones for structural reasons, so a network or industrial field should be compared with similar capital-heavy peers rather than with software or services.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate free cash flow to revenue over several years. A high and steady line is the sign of an industry with a durable, cash-generative model where each sales dollar reliably leaves cash behind. A line that drifts down over many years is a warning that the industry is keeping less of its sales as cash, whether from rising capital needs, falling margins, or growing pressure on prices.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A dip during a recession or a heavy investment phase that recovers afterward is a swing, not a decline. A slow, steady erosion that does not recover with the economy or once the spending wave ends is the more serious signal, because it points to a permanent weakening in how much cash the field can produce. The trend matters more than any single year, because cash generation is volatile from year to year but its underlying quality reveals itself over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow to revenue shows how much of each sales dollar an industry keeps as cash, while revenue growth shows how fast the sales themselves are expanding. An industry can be cash-rich but barely growing, or fast-growing but cash-hungry. Reading the two together places the field on the map of attractiveness.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that is both high in cash conversion and growing is the most attractive backdrop for an investment, because it funds its own expansion and still leaves cash for owners. High cash conversion with no growth describes a mature field that is best at returning cash. Fast growth with thin or negative cash conversion is a warning that the field is buying its growth with cash it has not yet earned, which depends on outside funding to continue.

### FCF Growth Used

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow to revenue shows how much cash the industry generates today, while the free cash flow growth rate used in its valuation shows how fast the market expects that cash to expand. Reading the two together connects what the field actually produces to what investors are paying for. The combination shows whether the price rests on a proven cash engine or on an assumed acceleration.

**What the combination tells you.** When a high free cash flow to revenue sits with a modest assumed growth rate, the valuation rests on cash the field already produces, which is the safer footing. When a thin free cash flow to revenue is paired with a high assumed growth rate, the price depends on a sharp future improvement that has not yet appeared. The pairing tells the reader how much of the industry's valuation is built on present cash and how much on hope.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between cash-generative and cash-consuming members, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. Free cash flow swings with the timing of capital spending and working capital, so a single year can mislead, and a heavy investment phase can make a healthy field look weak.

The figure also depends on how free cash flow is measured. Spending to maintain the business and spending to expand it are mixed together in capital expenditure, so a field investing heavily for growth can look cash-poor even though its core model is strong. A high figure can also reflect an industry that is starving its assets of needed investment, which boosts cash today at the cost of tomorrow. The metric measures the cash a field produces, not whether that cash level can last, so it must be read alongside the trend and the reasons behind the spending.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's free cash flow to revenue above or below the broad market, and has the gap held for several years or only recently?
2. Is a low figure the result of heavy investment for future growth, or of a structurally cash-poor business model?
3. Is a high figure genuine efficiency, or a sign that the field is underinvesting in its assets to flatter near-term cash?
4. Is the industry both cash-generative and growing, or cash-rich but stagnant?
5. Is a falling figure a cyclical effect of a downturn or investment wave, or a structural decline in how much cash the field retains?
6. Is strong cash conversion shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
7. Does the field's asset intensity explain its cash conversion, and is it being compared with similarly asset-heavy peers?
8. Could the industry fund its own growth and reward owners from internal cash, or does it depend on outside money to continue?
