# Excess Return (ROE − Ke)

## Part 1: Definition

Excess return measures how much more a business earns on its owners' money than those owners require. It takes the return on equity, the profit earned for each dollar of shareholder money, and subtracts the cost of equity, the return shareholders require for the risk they take. What remains is the spread the business earns above its owners' hurdle. A positive spread means the business rewards owners beyond what their risk demands, which is the definition of creating value for shareholders. A negative spread means the business earns less than owners require, which destroys shareholder value even when it reports a profit. An excess return of 4 percentage points means the business earns 4 points more on owner money than owners require. In the industry view this spread is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes whether the whole industry creates or destroys value for its shareholders.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can earn a positive spread inside an industry that destroys value overall. The industry number tells the reader whether the field as a whole rewards owners for their risk, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Wide positive spread | The field earns well above its owners' hurdle, creating shareholder value. |
| Near zero | The field earns about what its owners require, creating little extra value. |
| Negative spread | The field earns below its owners' hurdle, destroying shareholder value. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Excess Return = Return on Equity − Cost of Equity`

The two inputs are the return on equity, the profit earned for each dollar of shareholder money, and the cost of equity, the return owners require for the field's risk. The difference is the spread, stated in percentage points. A positive figure means the field clears its owners' hurdle, and a negative figure means it falls short. The industry figure is built by combining each company's return on equity and cost of equity across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes the spread on where the industry's actual capital sits, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The spread is the equity version of the value-creation test. A field can report healthy profits and still fail this test if those profits fall short of what owners require for the risk. Measuring the return against the hurdle, rather than against zero, is what separates a field that truly rewards its owners from one that merely avoids losses. This makes the spread the right test for lending fields such as banks, whose return is earned on owner money.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The excess return is the clearest test of whether an industry creates value for its shareholders. A profit on its own says little, because owners gave up the chance to invest elsewhere for the same risk. Only when a field earns more than that required return does it genuinely reward the people who funded it. The spread captures this directly, turning a raw profitability figure into a verdict on value creation. A field with a wide positive spread is structurally rewarding for owners, while a field with a negative spread is consuming the value its owners entrust to it.

This framing matters most for lending industries. A bank's whole business is turning shareholder money into a return, so the right test is its return on equity against its cost of equity, the very spread this metric measures. For these fields the spread is the natural value-creation test, because their return is earned on owner money rather than on all capital. Judging the field's spread first tells the reader whether the field is worth owning for its shareholders, before picking any single stock.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the field's profitability on owner money, its return on equity, set against the return its owners require. A field with strong, durable returns and ordinary risk earns a wide positive spread. A structural barrier that keeps competitors out helps hold the spread positive for years, because outsiders cannot easily come in and compete the returns away. The cost of equity is the other half: a field that grows riskier sees its hurdle rise, which narrows the spread even if its return holds steady.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. The business cycle lifts return on equity in good years and depresses it in recessions, widening or narrowing the spread with the economy. A market panic that widens the equity risk premium raises the hurdle across all fields at once, squeezing spreads even when the underlying businesses are unchanged. Heavy borrowing can lift return on equity while also raising the field's risk and so its hurdle, which can leave the spread flattered or unchanged. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries. A field with a wide positive spread creates more shareholder value than one whose spread is thin or negative. The ranking sorts fields into those that reward owners for their risk and those that do not, which is a cleaner test than raw profitability because it already accounts for risk.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Wide positive spread versus other fields | A field that rewards owners well above their hurdle, structurally attractive. |
| Spread near zero | A field that earns about what owners require, with no real edge. |
| Negative spread while others are positive | A field that destroys shareholder value even while reporting profits. |

Compare like with like. Because the spread already nets out the field's required return, it puts risky and steady fields on a fairer footing than return on equity alone. A high-risk field with a high return may still show only a modest spread once its steep hurdle is subtracted, while a low-risk field with a modest return may show a healthy spread. The spread should still be judged against similar fields rather than across very different ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate spread over several years. A wide, steady positive spread is the sign of a field that rewards its owners year after year, often protected by a structural barrier. A spread that drifts toward zero or turns negative is a warning that the field's returns are fading, that competition is intensifying, or that its risk and so its hurdle have risen.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A spread that narrows in a recession as returns fall, then recovers, reflects the business cycle rather than a permanent change. A spread that erodes slowly over many years and does not recover points to a structural loss of advantage, or to a lasting rise in the field's risk. Because the hurdle moves with the market, part of any swing in the spread reflects the broad environment rather than the field itself. The trend matters more than any single reading, because value creation reveals itself over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is one half of the excess return, and on its own it does not say whether a return is good. The excess return supplies the missing context by subtracting the hurdle owners require. Reading them together shows both how profitable the field is on owner money and whether that profitability beats the bar.

**What the combination tells you.** When a high return on equity also leaves a wide excess return, the field's profitability is genuine value creation, not just a high number. When a high return on equity leaves only a thin or negative spread, the field's risk has raised the hurdle so far that the profit barely rewards owners. The pairing stops the reader from mistaking a high return for value creation when the required return is high too.

### Cost Of Equity

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity is the hurdle subtracted in the excess return, so it sets the bar the field must clear. The excess return is meaningless without knowing how high that bar sits. Reading them together shows whether a positive spread was earned against a steep hurdle or an easy one.

**What the combination tells you.** When a field clears a high cost of equity and still shows a positive spread, its value creation is impressive, because it rewards owners despite a demanding hurdle. When a field shows a negative spread against a low cost of equity, it fails even an easy test, which is a serious warning. The pairing tells the reader how much of the field's spread reflects genuine strength rather than a forgiving hurdle.

## Part 8: Limitations

The spread inherits the weaknesses of both inputs. Return on equity can be lifted by heavy borrowing, and the cost of equity is an estimate built on a measure of risk that may not capture the future, so the spread can mislead if either input is off. The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the field. A single industry spread can hide a wide range between value-creating and value-destroying members, which means the industry figure is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work.

The figure also reflects the past, not the future. A field can earn a wide spread right up to the moment competition or a new entrant erodes its returns. Because the hurdle moves with the market, a falling spread may say more about a rising required return than about the field itself. The metric tells the reader whether value has been created against an estimated hurdle, not whether it will continue, so it must be paired with judgment about how the field's returns and risk are changing.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's excess return positive or negative, and how does it rank against other industries?
2. Is a positive spread earned against a high cost of equity, or only against a low, easy hurdle?
3. Is a wide spread driven by genuine profitability, or by heavy borrowing that also raises the field's risk?
4. Is the spread shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
5. What barrier keeps competitors from competing the field's returns down and turning the spread negative?
6. Is a narrowing spread a cyclical swing that recovers with the economy, or a slow structural decline?
7. For a lending field, does the spread confirm that the field rewards its owners for the risk they take?
8. Has the spread narrowed because returns fell, or because the field's risk and so its required return rose?
