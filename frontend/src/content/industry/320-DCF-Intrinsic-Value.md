# DCF Intrinsic Value

## Part 1: Definition

DCF intrinsic value is an estimate of what a business is truly worth based on the cash it is expected to produce in the future. The name stands for discounted cash flow. The idea is that a business is worth the cash it will hand its owners over its life, with future cash counted as worth less than cash today. Cash arriving years from now is discounted, meaning it is marked down to a smaller value in today's money, because money in hand now can be invested and a future promise carries risk. The method projects the cash a business will generate, marks each future year down to today's money, and adds the results into one fair value per share. In the industry view this fair value is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes what the whole industry is worth on the cash it is expected to earn, which the reader can set against what the industry actually costs.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can look cheap against its own fair value inside an industry the market has priced fairly overall. The industry number tells the reader whether the field as a whole is worth more or less than its market price, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Fair value well above price | The market may be underpricing the industry's future cash, which can signal opportunity. |
| Fair value near price | The market is paying about what the industry's expected cash is worth. |
| Fair value well below price | The market is paying more than the industry's expected cash appears to justify. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Intrinsic Value = Sum of future cash flows, each divided by (1 + discount rate) raised to the number of years away`

The inputs are the future cash flows the business is expected to produce, a growth rate for those cash flows, and a discount rate that converts future cash into today's money. The discount rate reflects the return an investor requires for the risk taken, so a riskier business is discounted harder and is worth less for the same cash. Because no business can be projected forever, the method projects a set number of years in detail, then adds a terminal value that captures the worth of all cash beyond that horizon. The industry figure is built by combining each company's intrinsic value and price across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual market value sits relative to its worth, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The fair value is only as sound as its inputs. A higher assumed growth rate or a lower discount rate lifts the value, and a lower growth rate or a higher discount rate lowers it. The method does not invent a single right answer. It states clearly what the cash, the growth, and the risk would have to be for the price to make sense.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

DCF intrinsic value ties an industry's price back to the thing that ultimately matters, which is the cash the field will generate for its owners. A market price can drift far from that cash, lifted by enthusiasm or pushed down by fear. The intrinsic value gives the reader an independent anchor, built from the industry's own expected cash rather than from the mood of the market. Comparing the two tells the reader whether the field is being priced sensibly against what it can actually earn.

This framing matters because the price paid for an industry shapes the return an owner can expect. An industry trading well below its intrinsic value offers a cushion, because the buyer pays less than the field appears to be worth. An industry trading well above it leaves little room for error, because the price already assumes a bright future. Judging the industry's worth against its price first tells the reader where the bargains and the dangers sit, before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the cash the industry is expected to produce and how fast that cash is expected to grow. A field with strong, durable cash generation and good growth prospects carries a high intrinsic value. The discount rate matters just as much, because it sets how harshly future cash is marked down. A risky, cyclical industry is discounted more heavily and so carries a lower value for the same expected cash. Interest rates feed straight into the discount rate, so when rates fall, the intrinsic value of whole industries rises, and when rates climb, it falls.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A swing in the business cycle can lift or depress the near-term cash an industry is expected to earn, changing the value even when nothing structural has changed. A shift in market sentiment can move the industry's price far from its intrinsic value for a while. The estimate depends heavily on its assumptions, so a small change in the assumed growth rate or discount rate can move the figure a lot. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when the gap between value and price is ranked against other industries. An industry trading at a wide discount to its intrinsic value is, on this measure, more attractively priced than one trading at a premium. An industry trading well above its value carries more risk if its expected cash disappoints.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Price far below value | The market may be underpricing the field's future cash, a possible opportunity. |
| Price near value | The field is priced about in line with its expected cash. |
| Price far above value | A richly priced field with little cushion if its cash disappoints. |

Compare like with like. A stable, low-risk industry is discounted gently and will tend to support a higher value relative to its cash than a volatile one. The size of the discount or premium must be judged against the field's risk and growth, and against similar industries, rather than across very different ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate intrinsic value, and the gap between value and price, over several years. A field that trades persistently below its intrinsic value may be a lasting bargain or may carry risks the simple estimate does not capture. A field that trades persistently above its value is one where the market keeps paying up for the future. Watching the gap over time shows whether the field tends to be cheaply or richly priced.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A value that falls in a recession because near-term cash is expected to drop, then recovers, reflects the business cycle rather than a permanent change. A value that drifts down over many years points to a structural decline in the field's expected cash. The gap between value and price also tells the reader how patient the market is being. The trend matters more than any single reading, because an intrinsic value estimate speaks to worth over years, not months.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** DCF intrinsic value estimates what the industry is worth, while the margin of safety expresses how far the field's price sits below that worth. The two are directly linked, because the margin of safety is the discount of price to the very intrinsic value this metric computes. Reading them together turns an abstract worth into a clear read on how much cushion the price offers.

**What the combination tells you.** When the industry's intrinsic value sits well above its price, the margin of safety is wide, and the field offers a buffer against errors in the estimate. When price sits above value, the margin of safety is negative, which warns that the field is priced for a future its expected cash may not deliver. The size of the margin tells the reader how much the assumptions could be wrong before the buyer overpays.

### FCF Growth Used

**Why read the two together.** DCF intrinsic value depends heavily on the growth rate assumed for the industry's cash, and FCF growth used is exactly that assumed rate. A high intrinsic value built on an aggressive growth assumption rests on a fragile foundation. Reading the two together shows how much of the field's estimated worth depends on optimistic growth.

**What the combination tells you.** When a high intrinsic value rests on a modest, believable growth rate, the estimate is more trustworthy. When a high value rests on a steep growth rate the field is unlikely to sustain, the worth may be overstated and the apparent bargain illusory. Checking the growth rate behind the value tells the reader whether the estimate is grounded or hopeful.

## Part 8: Limitations

The estimate is only as good as its assumptions. Small changes in the assumed growth rate or discount rate can swing the intrinsic value widely, so the figure should be read as a range rather than a precise number. The size-weighted industry value can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the field. A wide gap between price and value can hide a wide spread between cheaply and richly priced members, which means the industry figure is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work.

The method also struggles with certain industries. Fields whose cash flows are highly uncertain, deeply cyclical, or far off in the future are hard to project, which makes their intrinsic value unreliable. A field can trade below its estimated value for years before the gap closes, or never close it at all if the estimate is simply wrong. The metric tells the reader what the field appears to be worth under a set of assumptions, not what the market will pay or when, so it must be paired with judgment about how realistic those assumptions are.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's price above or below its intrinsic value, and how wide is the gap compared with other industries?
2. How sensitive is the intrinsic value to the growth rate and discount rate assumed, and would a modest change in either erase the gap?
3. Is a low intrinsic value the result of genuine structural decline, or of a temporary dip in near-term cash that will recover?
4. Is a wide discount to value shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
5. How much of the industry's value depends on the terminal value, the part that captures cash far in the future and is hardest to project?
6. Is the discount rate fair for the field's risk, or has a fall in interest rates flattered the value across the whole industry?
7. Are the industry's cash flows steady enough to project with confidence, or so cyclical and uncertain that the estimate is fragile?
8. Does a persistent discount to value reflect a real bargain, or risks the simple estimate fails to capture?
