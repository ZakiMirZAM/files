# Gross Profit Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

Gross profit ratio measures how much of each sales dollar is left after paying the direct cost of making the product. That direct cost, called cost of goods sold, covers the materials and labor that go straight into the product. Gross profit is sales minus that cost. The ratio is gross profit divided by sales. In the industry view this ratio is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes the typical gross margin of the whole industry. An industry-wide gross profit ratio of 40 percent means 40 cents of each sales dollar survives the direct cost of production.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can hold a wide margin inside a thin-margin industry through a special advantage. The industry number tells the reader whether the field itself allows a healthy gap between price and direct cost, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | An industry with pricing power, often protected by brands, patents, or unique products. |
| Around the market average | A normal industry with ordinary competition on price. |
| Persistently low | A commodity-like industry where products are interchangeable and price competition is fierce. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Gross Profit Ratio = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue`

Revenue is total sales. Cost of goods sold is the direct cost of producing what was sold. The industry figure is built by combining each company's gross profit and sales across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual sales are earning their margin, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The gross profit ratio captures only the direct cost of the product. It comes before overhead, before research, and before interest and tax. An industry with a high gross profit ratio can still earn thin final profits if its overhead is heavy, so this figure is the first and broadest read on pricing power, not the last word on profitability.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The gross profit ratio is the clearest financial fingerprint of pricing power across an industry. When an industry keeps a wide gap between price and direct cost year after year, customers are willing to pay more than the product costs to make, which usually means the product is differentiated or hard to copy. When the gap is thin and stays thin, the products are close to interchangeable and customers buy on price alone.

This framing follows the idea of the economic moat. An industry whose members hold high gross margins for years has some structural barrier that keeps prices above cost, such as brands, patents, or switching costs. An industry whose margins collapse whenever a competitor enters has no such barrier. A high and stable industry gross profit ratio is the signature of a structurally protected field, which is why the metric is read before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the nature of the product. An industry selling differentiated or protected products holds a wide gross margin, because customers cannot easily find a cheaper substitute. An industry selling a commodity sees its margin compete down to a thin level, because any one supplier's product is as good as another's. The character of the product changes slowly, which is why an industry's gross margin often stays in a similar range for years.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A spike in the cost of raw materials squeezes the margin until prices are raised to match. A wave of new entrants can drive the whole industry's margin down through price competition. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries. An industry with a wide gross margin held for many years has real pricing power and a structural edge. An industry with a thin margin is one where products are interchangeable and competition keeps the gap between price and cost narrow.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the market, stable | Strong pricing power from differentiated or protected products. |
| Near the market | Ordinary competition with no special pricing edge. |
| Below the market for years | A commodity-like industry where products are interchangeable. |

Compare like with like. A grocery industry runs on thin gross margins by its very nature, while a software industry runs on wide ones, so a grocer should be compared with other grocers rather than with software.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate gross profit ratio over several years. A high and steady line is the sign of an industry with durable pricing power, where the product stays differentiated and the margin holds. A line that drifts down over many years is a warning that the products are becoming commoditized, that new entrants are competing on price, or that customers are gaining the upper hand.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A sharp dip when raw-material costs spike, followed by a recovery once prices are raised, is a cyclical swing, not a structural decline. A slow, steady erosion that does not recover is the more serious signal, because it points to a permanent loss of pricing power. The trend matters more than any single year, because pricing power reveals itself over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The gross profit ratio shows the margin before overhead, while the operating income ratio shows the margin after overhead such as research, marketing, and administration. An industry can hold a wide gross margin yet earn thin operating margins if its overhead is heavy. Reading the two together shows how much of the industry's pricing power survives the cost of running the business.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry is high on both, its pricing power is real and its overhead is well controlled. When an industry is high on gross margin but only average on operating margin, the field carries heavy fixed costs that eat into its pricing advantage. The gap between the two is a quick read on how cost-heavy the industry is to operate.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** The gross profit ratio shows pricing power on each sale, while return on invested capital shows whether that pricing power turns into a real return on all the money tied up in the industry. A wide gross margin is only valuable if the industry does not need to sink huge capital into plants and equipment to earn it. Reading the two together shows whether the industry's pricing power creates lasting value.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry high on both has pricing power and uses its capital efficiently, the hallmark of a structurally excellent field. An industry with a wide gross margin but a low return on invested capital is capital-hungry, swallowing its pricing advantage in heavy plant spending. The pairing separates true pricing power from margins that vanish into the cost of the assets needed to produce.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between strong and weak members, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. The way companies classify costs can differ, so what counts as cost of goods sold in one industry may sit in overhead in another, which makes raw comparisons across very different industry types unreliable.

The figure also reflects the past, not the future. An industry can hold a wide margin right up to the moment a new technology makes its product cheap to copy. A strong industry gross profit ratio tells the reader where pricing power has been, not where it is heading, so it must be paired with judgment about how the competitive forces are changing.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's gross profit ratio above or below the broad market, and has the gap held for several years or only recently?
2. Does the wide margin come from genuinely differentiated products, or from a temporary shortage that competitors will erode?
3. Is a high industry figure shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
4. What barrier keeps prices above direct cost in this industry: brands, patents, switching costs, or scale?
5. Is the trend a cyclical swing tied to raw-material costs, or a slow structural erosion of pricing power?
6. Does the industry's pricing power survive after overhead, measured by its operating margin?
7. Are there signs that a substitute or a new entrant is about to commoditize the industry's products?
8. Are the industry's costs classified in a way that makes its gross margin comparable with the industries it is ranked against?
