# Revenue Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Revenue growth measures how fast sales rise from one year to the next. Revenue, also called sales, is the total money a company brings in from selling its products or services before any costs are taken out. In the industry view this growth is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how fast the whole industry is expanding. An industry-wide revenue growth of 8 percent means the typical dollar of sales in that industry grew by 8 cents over the year.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can grow fast inside a shrinking industry by taking sales from rivals. The industry number tells the reader whether the whole field is getting bigger or smaller, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A young or expanding industry where demand is still rising fast. |
| Around the economy's growth | A mature industry that grows roughly in line with the broad economy. |
| Persistently low or negative | A declining industry losing ground to substitutes or shifting demand. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Revenue Growth = (Current Revenue − Prior Revenue) ÷ Prior Revenue`

Current revenue is this year's sales. Prior revenue is last year's sales. The industry figure is built by combining each company's sales across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes how the industry's actual sales base is growing, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The industry growth number reflects two separate things. Part of it is volume, meaning more units sold to more customers. Part of it is price, meaning the same units sold at higher prices. An industry growing mainly on volume is gaining real demand. An industry growing mainly on price may only be passing inflation through to its customers.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

Industry growth sets the size of the opportunity for every company in it. When an industry grows fast year after year, demand is rising and there is room for many companies to expand at once. When an industry barely grows, companies can only gain sales by taking them from rivals, which sparks price wars and thins out profits for everyone.

This framing follows the idea of the industry life cycle. A young industry grows quickly as new customers adopt its product. A mature industry grows slowly because most customers already buy it. A declining industry shrinks as a substitute takes its place. Judging the industry's growth first tells the reader where the industry sits in that cycle, before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the stage of the industry's life cycle. A young industry expands fast because demand is still being created. A mature industry grows near the pace of the broad economy. A declining industry shrinks as customers move to something better. The life cycle shifts slowly, which is why an industry's growth rate often stays in a similar range for years.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. The business cycle lifts industry growth in good years and depresses it in recessions, and the swing is larger for industries whose sales rise and fall sharply with the economy. A burst of inflation can inflate the growth figure without any real gain in units sold. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries and against the growth of the broad economy. An industry growing well above the economy for many years is in an expanding phase and offers room for its companies to scale. An industry growing below the economy is mature or in decline, where gains must come at a rival's expense.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the economy, sustained | A young, expanding industry with rising demand. |
| Near the economy's pace | A mature industry growing with the broad market. |
| Below the economy for years | A declining industry losing ground to substitutes. |

Compare like with like. Fast growth in a young industry is normal and expected, so a young industry should be compared with other young industries rather than with mature ones where slow growth is the rule.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate revenue growth over several years. A high and steady line is the sign of an industry still in its expanding phase, where demand keeps rising. A line that drifts down over many years is a signal that the industry is maturing, that demand is saturating, or that a substitute is starting to take its place.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A sharp dip in a recession that recovers afterward is a cyclical swing, not a structural decline. A slow, steady slowdown that does not recover with the economy is the more serious signal, because it points to a permanent shift in demand. The trend matters more than any single year, because the industry's life-cycle stage reveals itself over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Revenue growth shows how fast the industry is expanding, while return on equity shows how profitable it is on owner money. An industry can grow fast while earning thin returns, or grow slowly while earning rich ones. Reading the two together places the industry on the map of attractiveness, because size of opportunity and quality of profit are different things.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that is both growing and highly profitable is the most attractive backdrop for an investment, because the pie is getting bigger and owners earn well. Fast growth with low return on equity is a warning that competition is consuming the rewards of expansion. Slow growth with high return on equity describes a mature industry that generates cash but offers little room to scale.

### Gross Profit Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Revenue growth shows whether the industry is selling more, while the gross profit ratio shows how much of each sales dollar survives the direct cost of making the product. An industry can grow its sales while its margins erode under price competition. Reading the two together shows whether growth is healthy or bought with discounts.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that grows while holding its gross margin is expanding on real demand and pricing power. An industry that grows only as its gross margin falls is buying sales by cutting prices, which is a fragile kind of growth. The pairing separates durable expansion from a race to the bottom.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between fast-growing and shrinking members, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. A growth number lifted by inflation can look healthy while real demand is flat, so the raw figure can mislead in high-inflation periods.

The figure also reflects the past, not the future. An industry can grow fast right up to the moment demand saturates or a substitute arrives. A strong industry growth number tells the reader where sales have been, not where they are heading, so it must be paired with judgment about how demand and the life cycle are changing.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's revenue growth above or below the broad economy, and has the gap held for several years or only recently?
2. Is the growth driven by more units sold, or mainly by higher prices passing through inflation?
3. Is a high industry figure shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
4. Where does the industry sit in its life cycle: young and expanding, mature, or declining?
5. Is the trend a cyclical swing that recovers with the economy, or a slow structural slowdown that does not?
6. Does the industry grow while keeping its profitability, or does growth come at the cost of thinning margins?
7. Are there signs that a substitute product or a shift in customer behavior is about to slow the industry's demand?
8. Does the growth reflect lasting demand, or a temporary surge such as a one-time restocking?
