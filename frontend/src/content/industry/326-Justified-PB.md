# Justified P/B

## Part 1: Definition

Justified P/B is the price-to-book ratio that a business's own fundamentals warrant. The price-to-book ratio compares a business's market price with its book value, the owners' stake recorded on the balance sheet, which is what owners would keep after debts are subtracted from what the business owns. The justified figure is not the ratio the market is charging. It is the ratio the business deserves given how profitable it is, how fast it grows, and what its owners require. Comparing the justified figure with the actual ratio tells the reader whether the market price is fair against the field's fundamentals. A justified P/B of 2 means the field's fundamentals warrant a price of twice book value. In the industry view this fair ratio is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes the price-to-book the whole industry's fundamentals warrant.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can deserve a high ratio inside an industry whose fundamentals warrant a modest one. The industry number tells the reader what price-to-book the field's fundamentals justify, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High justified ratio | The field's fundamentals warrant a rich price to book, driven by strong, durable returns. |
| Around one | The field's fundamentals warrant a price close to its book value. |
| Low justified ratio | The field's fundamentals warrant a thin price to book, reflecting weak returns. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Justified P/B = (Return on Equity − Growth) ÷ (Cost of Equity − Growth)`

The inputs are the return on equity, the profit earned for each dollar of owner money, the growth rate of that profit, and the cost of equity, the return owners require. The formula comes from the idea that a business is worth more than its book value only when it earns more on that book than its owners require. When return on equity equals the cost of equity, the justified ratio is one, meaning the field is worth exactly its book value. The more return on equity exceeds the cost of equity, the higher the justified ratio climbs. The industry figure is built by combining each company's fundamentals across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes the fair ratio on where the industry's actual capital sits, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The formula makes plain what drives a fair price to book. A field earning well above its cost of equity deserves to trade above book, because each dollar of book value produces more than owners require. A field earning below its cost of equity deserves to trade below book, because each dollar of book value produces less. This ties the price-to-book directly to the value-creation test, which makes the justified figure especially useful for lending fields such as banks, whose worth rests on the return they earn on owner money.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

Justified P/B turns a field's fundamentals into the price its book value deserves. A raw price-to-book ratio on its own cannot say whether a field is cheap or dear, because a high ratio can be earned by strong returns and a low ratio can reflect weak ones. The justified figure supplies the benchmark, built from the field's own profitability, growth, and required return. Comparing the actual ratio with the justified one tells the reader whether the market is overpaying or underpaying for the field's fundamentals.

This framing matters most for fields whose worth rests on book value, above all lending industries. A bank's value is closely tied to the return it earns on its equity, so the price-to-book its fundamentals warrant is a natural test of fair value. A field trading below its justified ratio may be underpriced for the returns it earns, while one trading above it may be overpriced. Judging the field's justified ratio first tells the reader what its fundamentals deserve, before comparing that with what the market charges.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the gap between the field's return on equity and its cost of equity. A field earning far more on owner money than owners require warrants a high price to book, because each dollar of book value creates value. A field earning near or below its cost of equity warrants a ratio near or below one. Growth amplifies the effect: when returns exceed the hurdle, faster growth lifts the justified ratio, because the field compounds its value creation. These fundamentals set the broad altitude of the figure.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. The business cycle lifts return on equity in good years and depresses it in recessions, raising or lowering the justified ratio with the economy. A rise in the field's risk lifts its cost of equity, which lowers the justified ratio even if returns hold steady. A change in interest rates feeds through the cost of equity into the fair ratio. Because the figure depends on all three inputs, a small shift in any one can move it. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when the gap between the actual and justified ratio is ranked against other industries. A field trading well below its justified ratio is, on this measure, underpriced for the returns it earns, while a field trading above its justified ratio is overpriced. The ranking points the reader toward fields the market may be misjudging.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Actual ratio below justified | The field may be underpriced for the returns its fundamentals support. |
| Actual ratio near justified | The field is priced about in line with what its fundamentals warrant. |
| Actual ratio above justified | The field may be overpriced relative to the returns it actually earns. |

Compare like with like. A field earning high returns deserves a high price to book, so a rich ratio is not a warning if the justified figure supports it, and a low ratio is not a bargain if the field earns weak returns. The actual ratio must be judged against the justified one, and the comparison is most reliable among similar fields, especially lending fields, rather than across very different ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate justified ratio, and the gap between the actual and justified ratio, over several years. A field whose actual ratio sits below its justified ratio for a long stretch may be persistently underpriced for its returns. A field whose actual ratio sits above its justified ratio is one the market keeps paying up for. Watching the gap over time shows whether the field tends to trade cheap or dear against its fundamentals.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A justified ratio that falls in a recession as return on equity drops, then recovers, reflects the business cycle rather than a permanent change. A justified ratio that erodes over many years points to a structural decline in the field's returns, which means a lower price to book is genuinely warranted. The gap between actual and justified also shows how the market's view sits against the fundamentals. The trend matters more than any single reading, because the fair price for a field's book value shifts with its returns and its risk.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is the main driver of the justified price to book, since the ratio rises as return on equity climbs above the cost of equity. The return on equity supplies the engine, and the justified ratio translates it into a fair price for book value. Reading them together shows how the field's profitability sets the price its book deserves.

**What the combination tells you.** When a high return on equity warrants a high justified ratio and the field trades below it, the field is underpriced for the returns it earns. When a high return on equity warrants a high justified ratio but the field trades above it, even strong returns may not support the price. The pairing shows whether the market's price for book value matches the profitability behind it.

### Excess Return (ROE − Ke)

**Why read the two together.** The justified price to book rises only when return on equity exceeds the cost of equity, which is exactly the excess return. The excess return tells the reader whether the field creates value, and the justified ratio expresses how much that value creation is worth in price-to-book terms. Reading them together links value creation to fair price.

**What the combination tells you.** When a positive excess return warrants a justified ratio above one, the field deserves to trade above book because it rewards owners. When a negative excess return warrants a justified ratio below one, the field deserves to trade below book because it falls short of its hurdle. The pairing confirms that a field trading above book is genuinely creating value, not merely priced as if it were.

## Part 8: Limitations

The justified ratio depends on three estimated inputs, return on equity, growth, and cost of equity, so an error in any one can distort it. The formula is also sensitive when the growth rate approaches the cost of equity, which can produce extreme or unstable figures. The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the field. A single industry ratio can hide a wide spread between members whose fundamentals warrant rich and thin ratios, which means the industry figure is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work.

Book value itself carries weaknesses. It is an accounting figure shaped by depreciation and other choices, and it can mean little for fields with few tangible assets, such as software, which makes the price-to-book less meaningful there. The metric works best for fields whose worth rests on book value, above all lending fields. It tells the reader what price the field's fundamentals warrant under a model, not what the market will pay or when, so it must be paired with judgment about the field's returns and risk.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

2. Is a high justified ratio driven by a wide gap between return on equity and the cost of equity, or by an aggressive growth assumption?
3. Is a low justified ratio warranted by genuinely weak returns, or is the field underpriced relative to what it earns?
4. Is the gap between actual and justified ratio shared across most companies, or produced by a few dominant members?
5. For a lending field, does the justified ratio confirm whether the market price is fair against the returns the field earns?
6. Has the justified ratio fallen because returns dropped in a downturn, or because the field's returns are in structural decline?
7. Is book value meaningful for this field, or does it carry little weight because the field has few tangible assets?
8. Is the formula stable for this field, or does a growth rate close to the cost of equity make the justified ratio unreliable?
