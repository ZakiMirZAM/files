# FCF Growth Used

## Part 1: Definition

FCF growth used is the growth rate the valuation assumed for a business's free cash flow. Free cash flow is the cash a business has left after paying its running costs and funding the investment needed to keep operating, so it is the cash truly available to owners. A discounted cash flow valuation must assume how fast that cash will grow in the years ahead, and FCF growth used is that assumed rate. It is not a measured result from the past. It is the forward growth the valuation built into the estimate of worth. A FCF growth used of 6 percent means the valuation assumed the business's free cash flow would rise about 6 percent a year. In the industry view this assumed rate is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how fast the whole industry's free cash flow was assumed to grow in valuing it.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can be assigned an aggressive growth assumption inside an industry valued conservatively overall. The industry number tells the reader how optimistic or cautious the growth built into the field's valuation is, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High assumed growth | The valuation expects the field's cash to expand quickly, which it must deliver to justify the price. |
| Moderate assumed growth | The valuation expects steady expansion roughly in line with a healthy economy. |
| Low or zero assumed growth | The valuation expects the field's cash to hold flat or barely rise, a cautious view. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`FCF Growth Used = the annual growth rate applied to free cash flow in the valuation`

The input is the growth rate the valuation assigned to future free cash flow, often drawn from the field's recent growth, its reinvestment, and a view of how long fast growth can last. The rate usually steps down over time, because no business can grow far faster than the economy forever, and a high near-term rate fades toward a modest long-run rate. The industry figure is built by combining each company's assumed growth rate across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes the growth built into where the industry's actual market value sits, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

This rate is an assumption, not a fact, and it drives the valuation more than almost any other input. A higher assumed growth rate lifts the estimated worth, and a lower one cuts it. Reading the rate tells the reader how much of the field's estimated worth rests on growth that has not yet happened, which is why an aggressive rate deserves close scrutiny.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

FCF growth used exposes the single most important assumption behind a valuation. An estimate of worth can look attractive, but if it rests on a steep growth rate the field is unlikely to sustain, the worth is fragile. Reading the assumed growth rate lets the reader see how much optimism is baked into the field's valuation. A modest, believable rate signals a grounded estimate, while a steep rate signals an estimate that depends on a bright future arriving.

This framing matters because growth assumptions are where valuations most often go wrong. It is easy to justify almost any price by assuming fast growth, and the further the assumed rate sits above what the field can realistically deliver, the more the estimate of worth is built on hope. Judging the growth assumption first tells the reader how demanding the field's valuation is, before trusting any conclusion drawn from it.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is how fast the industry is genuinely expected to grow. A young, expanding field with strong reinvestment and a long runway is assigned a high growth rate, while a mature field is assigned a modest one. How long fast growth is expected to last also matters, because a field that can grow quickly for many years carries a higher assumed rate than one whose growth is about to fade. These views of the field's future set the broad altitude of the figure.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A burst of recent growth can pull the assumed rate up, sometimes beyond what is sustainable, while a recent slump can pull it down. A wave of optimism about a theme can lift the growth assumptions across a whole field. Because the rate is a judgment, the figure also reflects how cautious or aggressive the valuation method is. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries. An industry with a high assumed growth rate carries a heavier burden of proof, because its valuation depends on that growth arriving. An industry with a modest assumed rate sets a lower bar that is easier to clear.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Assumed growth well above other fields | A demanding valuation that depends on fast growth being delivered. |
| Assumed growth near the middle | A valuation built on steady expansion, like most fields. |
| Assumed growth well below other fields | A cautious valuation that does not lean on much future growth. |

Compare like with like. A genuinely fast-growing field can support a high assumed rate that would be reckless for a mature one. The rate must be judged against the field's real growth prospects, and against similar industries, rather than across very different ones. A high rate is only a warning when it sits far above what the field can deliver.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate assumed growth rate over several years. A rate that holds steady near the field's real growth prospects suggests valuations are being set in a grounded way. A rate climbing far above the field's history is a warning that valuations are leaning ever harder on optimistic growth, which leaves more room for disappointment. A rate falling over time suggests the market expects the field's growth to slow.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A temporary jump in the assumed rate after a strong year may simply reflect recent results being projected forward, and should fade as growth normalizes. A steady climb in the assumed rate over many years points to rising optimism about the field's future, which may or may not prove justified. The trend matters more than any single reading, because it shows whether the growth built into the field's valuation is becoming more or less demanding over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DCF Intrinsic Value

**Why read the two together.** FCF growth used is one of the most powerful inputs to the intrinsic value, since a higher assumed growth rate lifts the estimated worth. The intrinsic value is the output, and the growth rate is the assumption that shapes it most. Reading them together shows how much of the field's estimated worth rests on assumed growth.

**What the combination tells you.** When a high intrinsic value rests on a modest, believable growth rate, the estimate is robust. When a high value rests on a steep growth rate the field is unlikely to sustain, the worth is fragile and the apparent bargain may vanish if growth disappoints. Checking the growth rate behind the value tells the reader whether the estimate is grounded or built on hope.

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** FCF growth used is the forward growth the valuation assumed, while revenue growth is the actual sales growth the field has delivered. The two cross-check each other, because an assumed cash growth rate far above the field's real sales growth is hard to justify. Reading them together shows whether the assumption matches the field's track record.

**What the combination tells you.** When the assumed growth rate sits roughly in line with the field's delivered revenue growth, the assumption is grounded in reality. When the assumed rate sits far above the field's actual growth, the valuation is leaning on a future the field has not yet shown it can produce. The gap between assumption and reality is a quick read on how demanding the field's valuation has become.

## Part 8: Limitations

The figure is an assumption, not a measured outcome, so it carries all the uncertainty of a forecast. A growth rate that looked reasonable can prove far too high or too low as the field's future unfolds. The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the field. A single industry rate can hide a wide spread between members assigned aggressive and cautious assumptions, which means the industry figure is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work.

The rate also tells the reader nothing on its own about whether the field is cheap or dear. A high assumed growth rate is not automatically a warning, because a genuinely fast-growing field can earn it, and a low rate is not automatically safe. The figure must be read against the field's real growth prospects and against the valuation it supports. It reveals how much the estimate of worth depends on growth, not whether that growth will actually arrive, so it must be paired with judgment about the field's future.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's assumed growth rate high, moderate, or low, and how does it rank against other industries?
2. Does the assumed rate sit roughly in line with the field's delivered revenue growth, or far above it?
3. Is a high assumed rate justified by a genuine long runway, or is it projecting a recent burst of growth that will fade?
4. Is the assumed rate consistent across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
5. How much of the field's estimated worth would disappear if the assumed growth rate were cut to a more cautious level?
6. Has the assumed rate climbed over time, signaling valuations that lean ever harder on optimistic growth?
7. Is the assumed long-run rate believable, or does it imply the field can outgrow the broader economy forever?
8. Does a low assumed rate reflect genuine caution about the field, or an overly conservative valuation that understates its worth?
