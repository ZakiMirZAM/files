# How to Read an Industry

## What it is

This overview is the method behind the Define This Industry First questions. An industry is the working level of competition: the set of companies that sell substitutable products to the same buyers. Sectors set the backdrop, but competition, pricing, and failure happen at industry level, which is why an industry can prosper inside a struggling sector and starve inside a booming one. The averages and the strategic-group map on this page describe the industry's output; this section asks you to understand its machinery first, the way a specialist researcher would.

A researcher who covers one industry can answer four things without looking anything up: what the product is and how the buying decision gets made, who the players are and how they are positioned against each other, what one sale earns after its costs, and which one or two operating numbers the whole industry watches. Those four are exactly what the question deck asks you to build.

## What defining an industry requires

| Step | The question | Why it comes before the numbers |
|---|---|---|
| The work itself | What does this industry do all day, and how does the customer decide to buy | The buying decision, its price sensitivity, its switching cost, is the root of every margin below |
| Players and groups | Who are the main companies, and which strategic groups do they form | An industry is usually several games in one: premium versus low-cost, integrated versus specialist. Averages blend the games together |
| Unit economics | What does one typical sale earn: price, cost to serve, contribution margin | Explains operating leverage, meaning which companies get dramatically better with scale and which do not |
| Insider KPIs | Which one or two operating metrics do insiders actually watch | Utilization, backlog, same-store sales, ARPU, loss ratios: the KPI leads the financials by quarters |
| Growth source | Is growth volume, price, or share shifting between players | Share-shift growth nets to zero across the industry; only volume and price grow the whole pie |
| Life cycle | Is the industry emerging, growing, shaking out, mature, or declining | The stage sets what margins and growth are even possible, and which strategy wins |

The strategic-group map below this section is the positioning tool: it splits the industry's largest companies on margin and scale, and the barriers that stop a company moving between groups are what protect the profitable group's returns. The life-cycle panel is the time dimension: differentiation tends to win early growth, cost leadership tends to win maturity, and a company running the wrong strategy for the stage is structurally exposed however good its current numbers look.

## Why it matters

Industry structure sets the profit ceiling for every company inside it. The five forces decide how much of the value the industry creates its companies get to keep; the strategic groups decide which companies inside it keep the most; the life-cycle stage decides how long any of it lasts. A high industry ROIC with low entry barriers is an invitation to competitors, not an asset. Every judgment the later stages ask for, which companies to compare, what growth to trust, what competitive advantage period a valuation deserves, is borrowed from the understanding you build here.

## Common traps

The first trap is judging the industry by its averages when the industry is really two or three distinct games. If the premium group earns 20 percent margins and the discount group earns 4, the blended 11 describes nobody.

The second trap is trusting reported financials over the operating KPI. Utilization, backlog, and churn turn before revenue does; the researcher who watches the KPI is quarters ahead of the one who waits for the income statement.

The third trap is mistaking share-shift for growth. An industry where one player's growth is another's decline is flat, and paying a growth multiple for a share-taker requires knowing exactly whose share it is taking and why that continues.

The fourth trap is ignoring the stage. Mature industries punish the strategies that built the growth stage: capacity additions that once met demand now start price wars. The same management playbook flips from right to wrong when the life cycle turns, which is why the stage question must be answered, not assumed.
