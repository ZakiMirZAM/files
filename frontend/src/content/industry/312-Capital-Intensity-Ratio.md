# Capital Intensity Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The capital intensity ratio measures how many dollars of assets a business must hold to produce each dollar of sales. Assets here are the hard resources the business needs to operate, such as factories, machinery, networks, and equipment. A high ratio means the business must lock up a large amount of capital in physical assets to generate its sales. A low ratio means it produces its sales with little hard investment.

In the industry view this number is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how capital-hungry the whole industry is. A high industry figure marks an asset-heavy industry, such as utilities or telecom, that must build expensive infrastructure before it can sell. A low figure marks an asset-light industry, such as software or consulting, that needs little hard capital to operate.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A capital-heavy industry that must build costly assets before it can earn. |
| Around the market average | A normal industry with an ordinary level of physical investment. |
| Persistently low | A capital-light industry that produces sales with little hard investment. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Capital Intensity Ratio = Total Assets ÷ Revenue`

Total assets are everything the business owns. Revenue is total yearly sales. The industry figure is built by combining each company's assets and sales across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual capital is committed, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The capital intensity ratio is the reverse of asset turnover. Where asset turnover asks how many sales each dollar of assets produces, capital intensity asks how many dollars of assets each sale requires. A high capital intensity ratio always goes with a low asset turnover, and both describe the same fact: the industry needs a large asset base to do its business.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The capital intensity ratio shows how much money the industry must commit before it can earn anything. A capital-heavy industry has to fund factories, pipelines, or networks up front, and that requirement shapes everything about how it competes and how risky it is. A capital-light industry can grow and adapt without sinking large sums into hard assets.

This requirement is closely tied to barriers to entry. When an industry demands enormous up-front investment, a newcomer must spend vast sums before competing at all, which can keep rivals out and protect the industry's returns. The same heavy base also locks the industry into its assets, so it cannot shrink quickly when demand falls. Judging the industry's capital intensity first tells the reader how much capital the industry must tie up and how protected it is by the cost of entry, before any single stock is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the physical nature of the industry's business. Industries that move goods, generate power, or run physical networks need vast hard assets and run a high capital intensity ratio. Industries built on people, brands, or code need little hard capital and run a low ratio. This pattern is structural and changes slowly.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A wave of building across the industry, adding plant and equipment ahead of the sales it will produce, raises capital intensity for a time. In a downturn, sales fall while the asset base stays fixed, which pushes the ratio up even though nothing structural has changed. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The capital intensity ratio is most useful when ranked against other industries, because it sorts the economy into capital-heavy and capital-light groups. A high ratio is normal for a utility or a railroad and would be a warning sign in a services or software industry.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High ratio, infrastructure-based | Normal, often protected by the huge cost a newcomer would face. |
| Low ratio, asset-light | Can grow and adapt quickly without heavy up-front investment. |
| Ratio rising over years | Either a wave of building, or sales weakening against a fixed asset base. |

Compare like with like. A high capital intensity means a protective barrier in one industry and would mean trapped capital in another, so the number carries meaning only against industries built the same way.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate capital intensity ratio over several years. A steady line means the industry's appetite for hard assets is stable. A line that rises over many years can mean the industry is becoming more capital-hungry, needing ever more investment to produce the same sales, which is a warning that returns may be getting harder to earn.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A ratio that rises in a recession because sales fell against a fixed asset base is a cyclical swing that recovers afterward. A steady structural rise is more serious, because it means the industry must keep pouring in capital to stand still. Academic work on capital spending suggests that industries which raise hard-asset investment faster than their peers often see weaker profits and returns afterward, so a steadily rising ratio deserves caution rather than applause.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Asset Turnover

**Why read the two together.** The capital intensity ratio and asset turnover are two views of the same fact, one the reverse of the other. Capital intensity asks how many assets each sale requires, while asset turnover asks how many sales each asset produces. Reading them together for the industry confirms how asset-heavy it truly is and how efficiently it uses what it holds.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry with high capital intensity and low asset turnover is firmly asset-heavy, and its returns depend on getting good use out of a large base. If turnover improves while capital intensity falls, the industry is learning to do more with less, a sign of strengthening efficiency. The pairing shows whether the industry's heavy capital is working hard or sitting idle.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Capital intensity shows how much capital the industry must commit, while return on invested capital shows what the industry earns on all that capital. A capital-heavy industry only rewards owners if it earns a return above the cost of the capital it ties up. Reading them together for the industry tests whether the heavy investment is justified.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry with high capital intensity and a return on invested capital above its cost of capital is putting its heavy assets to profitable use, and the cost of entry may protect it. An industry with high capital intensity but a return below its cost of capital is destroying value, pouring money into assets that do not earn their keep. The pairing turns a measure of how much capital the industry uses into a judgment about whether that capital should be there at all.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Capital-heavy industries must fund their large asset bases somehow, and much of that funding often comes from borrowing. The capital intensity ratio shows how much capital the industry must raise, while debt to equity shows how much of it comes from debt. Reading them together for the industry shows how the heavy asset base is financed.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that is both capital-heavy and heavily borrowed carries double risk, because a downturn squeezes profit while the debt still must be serviced. An industry that funds its heavy assets mostly with owner money is more resilient. The pairing shows how fragile the industry's capital base would be if business conditions or financing markets turned against it.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. The ratio measures assets at their accounting value, which can be far from their real worth, so an industry with old plant carried at low book value can look less capital-hungry than it really is. This makes raw comparisons across very different industry types unreliable.

The figure also says nothing about profit. A high capital intensity is fine if the industry earns rich returns on its assets and is dangerous only if it does not. Capital intensity describes how much capital the industry must commit, not whether that commitment pays off, so it must be paired with a return measure to mean anything.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry capital-heavy or capital-light, and is that level normal for its kind of business?
2. Does the industry's heavy capital requirement act as a barrier that keeps new competitors out?
3. Does the industry earn a return on its capital above the cost of that capital, or is it destroying value?
4. Is a rising ratio a wave of building, or sales weakening against a fixed asset base?
5. Is the heavy asset base funded mostly by owner money or by borrowing?
6. Is the industry figure shaped by a few dominant members, or shared across most companies?
7. Are the industry's assets carried at values close to their real worth, or far below it?
8. Can the industry shrink its capital base if demand falls, or is it locked into heavy fixed investment?
