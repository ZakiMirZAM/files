# Current Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The current ratio measures whether a business can pay its short-term bills with its short-term resources. Current assets are things expected to turn into cash within a year, such as cash itself, money owed by customers, and inventory. Current liabilities are bills due within a year, such as money owed to suppliers and the part of debt coming due soon. The current ratio divides one by the other. A ratio of 2 means the business holds two dollars of short-term assets for every dollar of short-term bills.

In the industry view this number is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes the short-term safety cushion of the whole industry. The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. It tells the reader whether the industry as a whole runs with a comfortable buffer or operates close to the edge.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Comfortably above 1 | The industry holds a healthy short-term buffer and is not strained for cash. |
| Around 1 | Short-term assets roughly match short-term bills, a normal level for many industries. |
| Persistently below 1 | The industry leans on steady incoming cash or fresh borrowing to meet its bills. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities`

Current assets are the resources due to become cash within a year. Current liabilities are the bills due within a year. The industry figure is built by combining each company's current assets and current liabilities across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual short-term resources and bills sit, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

A ratio above 1 means short-term assets exceed short-term bills. A ratio below 1 does not always signal trouble, because some industries collect cash from customers before they pay suppliers and can run safely on a thin buffer. The level that counts as healthy depends on the industry's normal way of operating.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The current ratio shows the financial breathing room of the industry. An industry that runs with a thick short-term buffer can absorb a sudden drop in sales or a delay in customer payments without scrambling for cash. An industry that runs thin must keep money flowing in steadily, which makes it more fragile when conditions tighten.

This buffer reflects how the industry's business model works. Industries that sell for cash and pay suppliers later can operate safely on a low ratio, because money arrives before bills come due. Industries that carry large inventory or wait a long time to collect from customers need a higher ratio to stay safe. Judging the industry's liquidity first tells the reader how much shock the industry can take before it is forced to raise money or cut back, before any single stock is chosen.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the industry's business model. An industry that collects from customers quickly and pays suppliers slowly naturally holds a low current ratio and is still safe, because cash comes in ahead of bills. An industry that ties up cash in inventory or grants customers long payment terms needs a higher ratio to cover the gap. This pattern is structural and tends to hold for years.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. In a downturn, customers pay more slowly and inventory piles up, which can swell current assets while sales fall, so the ratio can rise for an unhealthy reason. A wave of short-term borrowing across the industry lifts current liabilities and pushes the ratio down. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The current ratio is most useful when ranked against other industries, because a safe level for one industry is risky for another. Retailers and other fast-cash industries operate safely on low ratios. Manufacturers and others that hold heavy inventory usually need higher ratios to stay safe.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| High ratio, inventory-heavy industry | A normal cushion for an industry that ties up cash in stock and slow collection. |
| Low ratio, fast-cash industry | Safe, because the industry collects from customers before paying suppliers. |
| Low ratio, slow-cash industry | A warning, because bills come due before the industry's cash arrives. |

Compare like with like. The same ratio means safety in one industry and strain in another, so the number carries meaning only against industries that operate the same way.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate current ratio over several years. A steady line means the industry's short-term safety is stable. A line that drifts down over many years is a warning that the industry is operating closer to the edge, either because it is taking on more short-term debt or because its cushion is thinning.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A ratio that jumps in a recession because inventory is not selling is a cyclical swing, not a sign of strength. A slow, steady decline that does not recover with the economy points to a structural change in how the industry funds itself. A rising ratio is not always good and a falling ratio is not always bad, so the trend must be read together with why the parts are moving.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Quick Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio counts inventory as a short-term resource, but inventory can be slow to sell or worth less than its stated value. The quick ratio removes inventory and counts only the most liquid assets. Reading the two together for the industry shows how much of its short-term cushion depends on selling inventory.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry's current ratio is high but its quick ratio is low, the industry's safety rests on inventory it may not be able to sell quickly in a downturn. When both are healthy, the industry's liquidity is real and does not depend on moving stock. The gap between the two is a quick read on how inventory-dependent the industry's buffer really is.

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio is a snapshot of short-term assets against short-term bills, while the cash conversion cycle measures how many days the industry waits between paying for goods and collecting from customers. An industry with a short cycle can run safely on a low current ratio, because cash returns fast. Reading the two together explains whether a low ratio is risky or normal.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry has a low current ratio and a short cash conversion cycle, the low ratio is a sign of efficiency, not danger. When an industry has a low current ratio and a long cycle, cash is tied up while bills come due, which is a genuine warning. The pairing turns a bare liquidity number into a judgment about whether the industry's working capital flows safely.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. The ratio counts inventory and customer balances at their stated value, but in a downturn inventory may not sell and customers may not pay, so the cushion can be thinner than it looks. A high ratio is not always healthy, because it can mean cash is trapped in unsold stock or uncollected bills rather than working for the business.

The figure is also a single snapshot, not a flow. It shows the balance on one day, not how reliably cash moves through the industry over a year. An industry can show a low ratio and be perfectly safe, or a high ratio and still be poorly run, so the number must be paired with judgment about how the industry's cash actually cycles.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's current ratio normal for its business model, or low for an industry that collects cash slowly?
2. How much of the industry's short-term cushion is inventory that might not sell in a downturn?
3. Does a low industry ratio reflect efficient fast-cash operations, or genuine short-term strain?
4. Is the trend a cyclical swing from a downturn, or a steady structural decline in the industry's buffer?
5. Is a falling ratio caused by rising short-term debt across the industry, or by a thinning asset cushion?
6. Is the industry figure shaped by a few dominant members, or shared across most companies?
7. Would the industry's liquidity survive a sudden drop in sales or a delay in customer payments?
8. Does the industry depend on steady fresh borrowing to meet its short-term bills?
