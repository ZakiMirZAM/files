# Value Score

## Part 1: Definition

Value Score is the app's composite rating of how attractive a business looks, expressed as a single number from 0 to 100. It blends several measures of value and quality into one figure, so the reader can rank candidates quickly. A higher score means the business screens as more attractive on the blend of measures, and a lower score means it screens as less attractive. The score is a screening rank, not a valuation. It does not say what a business is worth in dollars. It is a quick way to sort a field and decide where to look harder. In the industry view the score is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how attractive the whole industry screens against the same blend of measures.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can score well inside an industry that screens poorly overall. The industry number tells the reader whether the field as a whole looks attractive on the blend, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| High score | The field screens as attractive on the blend of value and quality measures. |
| Middle score | The field screens as ordinary, with no standout strength or weakness. |
| Low score | The field screens as unattractive on the blend, warranting caution. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Value Score = a weighted blend of several value and quality measures, scaled to a 0 to 100 range`

The inputs are several underlying measures, such as how cheaply the field is priced and how profitable and durable its businesses are. Each measure is scored, the scores are weighted, and the blend is scaled onto a 0 to 100 range so the result is easy to read and compare. The score is relative, meaning it rates a business against others on the same scale rather than against an absolute standard of worth. The industry figure is built by combining each company's score across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes how the industry's actual market value screens, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The score is a summary, so it hides the detail behind it. Two fields with the same score can be attractive for opposite reasons, one cheap but low quality and the other expensive but excellent. The score points the reader toward fields worth studying. It does not replace the underlying measures or a proper valuation.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The Value Score exists to make sorting fast. An investor comparing many industries cannot weigh every measure for every field at once, so a single composite gives a quick first cut. A high industry score flags a field that looks attractive on the blend and deserves a closer look. A low score flags a field that screens poorly and may not be worth the time. The score turns a pile of separate measures into one ranking the reader can act on quickly.

This framing matters because screening is the first step, not the last. The score does not prove a field is cheap or excellent. It says the field ranks well on the chosen blend, which is a reason to investigate, not a reason to buy. Judging the industry's score first tells the reader where to focus, before doing the deeper work of valuation and structural analysis on any single field.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is how the field stacks up on the underlying measures the score blends. A field that is both cheaply priced and made of profitable, durable businesses scores high, while a field that is expensive or weak scores low. Because the score is a blend, a field can earn a middling score by being strong on some measures and weak on others, so the headline number can mask the mix beneath it. The weights chosen for each measure also shape the figure, since a blend that leans on cheapness will rank fields differently from one that leans on quality.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A market selloff that cuts prices can lift a field's score by making it screen cheaper, even if nothing about the businesses has changed. A swing in profitability over the business cycle can move the quality measures the score blends. Because the score is relative, a field's score can shift simply because other fields have changed. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is built for ranking, so it is most useful set against other industries. A field scoring near the top of the range screens as more attractive on the blend than one scoring near the bottom. The ranking points the reader toward fields worth studying and away from fields that screen poorly.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Score well above other fields | The field screens as attractive on the blend, a candidate for closer study. |
| Score near the middle of the pack | The field screens as ordinary, like most others. |
| Score well below other fields | The field screens as unattractive, warranting caution. |

Compare scores with care. Because the score blends several measures, two fields with the same score can be attractive for different reasons, so the reader should look at what drove a field's score before trusting the rank. The score is a guide to where to look, and it should be checked against the underlying measures rather than treated as a final verdict.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate score over several years. A score that holds high suggests the field has screened as attractive on the blend for a sustained stretch. A score climbing over time suggests the field is becoming cheaper, stronger, or both, on the measures the score blends. A score falling over time suggests the field is becoming more expensive or weaker.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A score that jumps in a downturn because prices fell, then settles as the field recovers, reflects the business cycle rather than a lasting improvement. A score that climbs steadily over many years points to a genuine, structural shift in how the field screens. Because the score is relative, a change can also reflect shifts in other fields rather than in this one. The trend matters more than any single reading, because a screening rank is most useful when its direction is clear.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DCF Intrinsic Value

**Why read the two together.** The Value Score is a quick screening rank, while DCF intrinsic value is a considered estimate of worth in dollars. The score points to fields worth studying, and the intrinsic value does the deeper work the score cannot. Reading them together pairs a fast first cut with a proper valuation.

**What the combination tells you.** When a high score lines up with a field trading below its intrinsic value, the quick screen and the deeper valuation agree, which strengthens the case for a closer look. When a high score sits with a field trading above its intrinsic value, the screen and the valuation disagree, which is a signal to dig into why before trusting the score. The pairing stops the reader from acting on a screening rank without the valuation to back it.

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** The Value Score ranks how attractive the field screens, while the margin of safety measures the discount of price to estimated worth. Both speak to attractiveness, but the score is a blend and the margin is a single, valuation-based read. Reading them together checks whether the screen's verdict matches the cushion the price offers.

**What the combination tells you.** When a high score lines up with a wide margin of safety, the screen and the valuation both point to an attractive, well-priced field. When a high score sits with a thin or negative margin, the field may screen well on quality while offering no price cushion, which the score alone would hide. The pairing separates a field that is genuinely cheap from one that merely screens well on other measures.

## Part 8: Limitations

The score is a screening rank, not a valuation, so it never says what a field is worth in dollars. It blends several measures into one number, which hides the detail beneath, so two fields with the same score can be attractive for opposite reasons. The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the field. A single industry score can hide a wide spread between high and low scoring members, which means the industry figure is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work.

The score also depends on the measures chosen and the weights assigned, so it reflects the method behind it as much as the field itself. A field can score well on the blend yet face risks the chosen measures do not capture, such as a fading moat or a coming regulatory change. Because the score is relative, it shows how the field ranks against others, not whether the field is attractive in absolute terms. The metric tells the reader where to look first, not what to conclude, so it must be paired with valuation and judgment.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's Value Score high, middling, or low, and how does it rank against other industries?
2. Which underlying measures drove the score, cheapness, quality, or a mix, and does the blend match the reader's priorities?
3. Does a high score line up with a field trading below its intrinsic value, or do the screen and the valuation disagree?
4. Is a high score shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
5. Has the score risen because the field became genuinely stronger, or only because a price fall made it screen cheaper?
6. Does the score hide risks the chosen measures do not capture, such as a fading moat or a regulatory threat?
7. Is the field's score high in absolute terms, or only relative to other fields that screen even worse?
8. Does a high score survive a proper valuation, or is it a screening flag that the deeper work does not support?
