# Quick Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The quick ratio measures whether a business can pay its short-term bills using only its most reliable assets. Short-term bills are amounts owed within a year, called current liabilities. The most reliable assets are cash, money market holdings, and money owed by customers, which together are called quick assets. The quick ratio leaves out inventory, because inventory must first be sold before it turns into cash. In the industry view this ratio is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how easily the whole industry can cover its near-term obligations without relying on selling stock first. An industry-wide quick ratio of 1.0 means the typical company in that industry holds one dollar of fast-access assets for each dollar owed within the year.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can keep a thick cash cushion inside an industry that runs lean. The industry number tells the reader how much liquidity stress the field carries by its nature, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | An industry that holds plenty of fast assets, often because its members keep large cash balances or collect from customers quickly. |
| Around the market average | A normal industry with an ordinary short-term cushion. |
| Persistently low | An industry that runs on thin liquidity, often because it relies on inventory or steady incoming cash to meet its bills. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities`

Current assets are what a business expects to turn into cash within a year. Inventory is the stock of unsold goods, removed here because selling it takes time. Current liabilities are the bills due within a year. The industry figure is built by combining each company's quick assets and current liabilities across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual short-term cash strength sits, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The quick ratio is a stricter test than the current ratio, which counts inventory. By stripping out inventory, the quick ratio shows what an industry could pay right away if sales stopped. A high industry figure signals a comfortable buffer. A low industry figure signals that the field depends on inventory turning into sales, or on fresh cash arriving on time.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The quick ratio is a clear read on how much short-term financial stress an industry can absorb. When an industry holds plenty of fast assets year after year, its members can meet their bills even if business slows suddenly. When an industry runs on thin liquidity, a stall in sales or a delay in collecting from customers can push its members toward a cash squeeze. Liquidity is the first thing a careful analyst checks, because a business can be profitable on paper and still fail if it cannot pay its bills on time.

This framing matters because liquidity shapes how an industry behaves under pressure. An industry with a thin quick ratio is fragile when credit tightens or a downturn delays customer payments. An industry with a thick quick ratio can ride out a shock without scrambling for emergency funding. Judging the industry's liquidity first tells the reader how resilient the field is likely to be, before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is how the industry's business model handles cash and inventory. An industry that collects from customers before it pays suppliers, such as some subscription businesses, naturally holds strong liquidity. An industry that carries heavy inventory and sells on long credit terms, such as some manufacturers, runs a thinner quick ratio by design. This structural trait changes slowly, which is why an industry's liquidity often stays in a similar range for years.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A recession can drain the aggregate as customers pay later and cash piles into unsold inventory. A stretch of strong profits can lift it as cash builds up on the balance sheet. A wave of large dividends or buybacks across the industry can pull cash out and lower the ratio. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries. An industry with a high quick ratio held for many years funds its near-term needs with a comfortable cushion, which lowers its financial risk. An industry with a thin quick ratio operates close to the edge and depends on cash arriving as expected.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the market, stable | A liquidity-rich field, often one that collects cash fast and carries little inventory. |
| Near the market | An ordinary short-term cushion with no special liquidity edge. |
| Far below the market | A field that runs lean on fast assets, more exposed to a sudden cash squeeze. |

Compare like with like. Industries with steady incoming cash, such as some consumer staples, can run safely on a thinner quick ratio than industries with lumpy or uncertain sales, so they should be compared with similar business models rather than across very different ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate quick ratio over several years. A steady line is the sign of an industry with a settled approach to liquidity that matches the rhythm of its cash flows. A line that drifts down over many years is a warning that the industry is operating with a thinner safety margin. The thinner cushion may reflect slower collections, rising inventory, or cash being returned to owners faster than it is earned.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A dip during a recession, as sales slow and cash gets tied up, is a cyclical swing that should ease as the economy recovers. A slow, steady decline that continues through good years is the more serious signal, because it points to a permanent shift toward tighter liquidity. The trend matters more than any single year, because the durability of a liquidity buffer reveals itself over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio counts inventory among the assets that can cover short-term bills, while the quick ratio leaves inventory out. Reading the two together shows how much of an industry's short-term safety depends on selling its stock of goods. The gap between them is a quick read on how inventory-heavy the field is.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry's current ratio and quick ratio are close, its liquidity is genuine and does not lean on inventory. When the current ratio is comfortable but the quick ratio is thin, the industry's apparent safety rests on unsold goods that must first find buyers. The pairing tells the reader how solid the industry's near-term cushion really is once slow-moving inventory is set aside.

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** The quick ratio shows how much fast cash an industry holds against its bills, while the cash conversion cycle shows how long the industry ties up cash before collecting it. Reading the two together shows whether a thin quick ratio is a real danger or simply the result of an industry that turns cash over quickly. The combination connects the snapshot of liquidity to the speed of the cash engine behind it.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry has a thin quick ratio but a short cash conversion cycle, its cash returns fast enough to keep the lean buffer safe. When a thin quick ratio sits alongside a long cash conversion cycle, the industry holds little fast cash and is slow to refill it, which is a fragile combination. The pairing tells the reader whether thin liquidity is a managed efficiency or a genuine source of risk.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between cash-rich and cash-poor members, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. The ratio is a static snapshot of one moment, usually the balance sheet date, so it can miss seasonal swings in cash and inventory that matter for many industries.

The figure also says nothing about quality. The money owed by customers is counted as a quick asset, but some of it may never be collected, which flatters a weak industry. A high quick ratio can even signal that an industry is hoarding idle cash rather than putting it to work. The metric measures whether an industry can pay its near-term bills, not whether it is using its assets well, so it must be read alongside profitability and the speed of its cash cycle.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's quick ratio above or below the broad market, and has the gap held for several years or only recently?
2. How wide is the gap between the industry's quick ratio and its current ratio, and does that show heavy reliance on inventory?
3. Is a low industry figure a sign of fragility, or simply the result of a field that collects cash quickly and turns it over fast?
4. Is a high figure genuine liquidity strength, or a sign of idle cash that the industry is failing to put to work?
5. Is a falling ratio a cyclical effect of a slowdown, or a structural drift toward thinner liquidity that continues through good years?
6. Is a thin quick ratio shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
7. Would the industry's members be able to meet their bills if a downturn delayed customer payments or froze inventory?
8. Does the industry's business model collect cash before it pays suppliers, or the other way around?
