# DuPont Tax Burden

## Part 1: Definition

The DuPont tax burden measures how much of a business's pre-tax profit it keeps after paying income tax. Pre-tax profit is what is left once all operating costs and interest are paid but before tax is taken out. Net income is what remains after tax. The ratio is net income divided by pre-tax profit, so it is the share of profit that survives the tax bill. A higher ratio means a lighter tax drag and more profit retained for owners. In the industry view this lever is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how much of the whole industry's pre-tax profit reaches owners after tax. An industry-wide tax burden of 0.80 means the typical company in that industry keeps 80 cents of each dollar of pre-tax profit, with 20 cents lost to tax.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can shelter its profit through credits or losses inside an industry that pays full tax. The industry number tells the reader how heavily the field as a whole is taxed, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high, near 1.0 | A lightly taxed industry, often because of credits, foreign income, or favorable tax treatment. |
| Around the market average | A normal industry paying an ordinary effective tax rate. |
| Persistently low | A heavily taxed industry, often domestic and without large shelters or credits. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`DuPont Tax Burden = Net Income ÷ Pre-tax Income`

Net income is profit after tax. Pre-tax income is profit before tax. The industry figure is built by combining each company's net income and pre-tax income across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes the tax drag on where the industry's actual profit sits, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The tax burden is one of five levers that together build return on equity. The full chain reads as tax burden, then interest burden, then operating margin, then asset turnover, then financial leverage, and multiplying all five gives return on equity. In plain words, the chain starts with how well the business runs its operations, then strips out the drag of interest and tax, then accounts for how hard the assets work and how much the owner money is geared. The tax burden is the final lever, the share of profit that survives tax and actually reaches owners. A higher tax burden lifts return on equity, because more of each dollar of pre-tax profit is kept rather than paid to the government.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

The tax burden isolates one specific force on an industry's profitability, the weight of income tax. Breaking return on equity into its levers lets the reader see why one industry rewards owners more than another, rather than seeing only the combined result. When an industry keeps a high share of its pre-tax profit, a lighter tax drag is part of why its returns look strong. When an industry keeps a low share, tax is quietly eating into what owners receive, even if the underlying business is healthy.

This framing matters because the tax lever can flatter or depress an industry's headline return for reasons that have nothing to do with how good the business is. An industry that benefits from credits or favorable treatment shows a high return partly because of tax policy, not pricing power or efficiency. Separating the tax lever tells the reader how much of the field's profitability is durable business quality and how much is a tax advantage that policy could remove, before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is the tax treatment built into the industry's structure. An industry with large research credits, heavy foreign earnings taxed at lower rates, or favorable depreciation rules keeps a high share of its pre-tax profit. An industry that earns mostly domestic income with few shelters carries a heavier tax drag. This structural trait changes slowly, although less slowly than the underlying business, because it depends partly on tax law.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A change in the headline corporate tax rate shifts the whole industry's tax burden at once. A wave of one-time items, such as a write-down or a tax settlement, can distort the figure for a year. The mix of where an industry earns its income, at home or abroad, can drift and move the aggregate. A few very large companies with aggressive tax planning can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries. An industry that keeps a high share of its pre-tax profit enjoys a tax advantage that lifts its returns, while an industry that keeps a low share carries a tax drag that other levers must overcome.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the market, near 1.0 | A lightly taxed field, often rich in credits or foreign income, with returns partly owed to tax. |
| Near the market | An ordinary effective tax rate with no special tax edge. |
| Far below the market | A heavily taxed field whose returns must be earned despite a large tax drag. |

Compare like with like. Industries with heavy research spending or large foreign operations tend to enjoy lighter tax burdens for structural reasons, so they should be compared with similar fields rather than with purely domestic, fully taxed ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate tax burden over several years. A steady line is the sign of an industry with a settled tax position that reflects its structure and the prevailing tax law. A line that steps up or down sharply usually marks a change in tax policy or a shift in where the industry earns its income, rather than a change in the business itself.

Separate the policy shift from the business. A sudden jump in the tax burden across the whole field, with no change in operations, almost always reflects a cut in the corporate tax rate. A gradual drift can reflect a slow change in the industry's mix of domestic and foreign earnings. The trend matters because a tax advantage that has held for years is more dependable than one that appeared with a single law change and could be reversed by the next.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DuPont Interest Burden

**Why read the two together.** The tax burden and the interest burden are the two levers in the return on equity chain that strip profit away rather than create it. The interest burden shows how much operating profit survives interest, and the tax burden shows how much of what remains survives tax. Reading the two together shows the full drag that financing and tax place on the industry's operating profit before it reaches owners.

**What the combination tells you.** When both levers are high, little of the industry's operating profit is lost to interest or tax, so the field's return on equity rests mainly on its business and its gearing. When the tax burden is high but the interest burden is low, the field is lightly taxed yet heavily weighed down by interest, which points to leverage rather than tax as the constraint. The pairing tells the reader which of the two drags is shaping the industry's returns.

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is the combined result that the tax burden helps build, so reading the lever next to the whole figure shows how much of the industry's headline return depends on its tax position. The tax burden is one of five pieces, and isolating it reveals whether a strong return is earned in the business or helped by light taxation. The combination connects the part to the whole.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry has a strong return on equity and a high tax burden, part of that return is a tax advantage that policy could remove rather than pure business strength. When a strong return on equity sits with a low tax burden, the field earns its return despite heavy tax, which points to genuine operating quality or gearing. The pairing tells the reader how durable the industry's return is if its tax treatment changed.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies with aggressive tax planning, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between members that pay full tax and members that shelter most of theirs, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. One-time items such as tax settlements or write-downs can distort the figure for a single year, so it must be read across several years.

The figure also says nothing about business quality. A high tax burden lifts return on equity, but it reflects tax policy rather than pricing power, efficiency, or a moat. An industry can show an attractive return largely because of a tax advantage that the next change in law could erase. The metric isolates the tax lever, not whether the underlying business is strong, so it is only one piece of the return on equity picture and means little on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's tax burden above or below the broad market, and has the gap held for several years or only recently?
2. How much of the industry's return on equity depends on a light tax burden rather than on the business itself?
3. Does a high tax burden come from durable features such as research credits, or from items that a change in tax law could reverse?
4. Is a recent jump in the tax burden the result of a corporate tax rate change rather than any improvement in the business?
5. Is a high figure shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members with aggressive tax planning?
6. Does the industry earn heavily abroad in ways that lower its tax burden, and how stable is that arrangement?
7. Would the industry's headline return still look attractive if it paid the full domestic tax rate?
8. Are one-time tax items distorting the figure in any single year of the series?
