# Return On Equity

## Part 1: Definition

Return on equity measures how much yearly profit is earned for each dollar of shareholder money. Shareholder money, called equity, is what owners keep after debts are subtracted from what the companies own. In the industry view this number is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how profitable the whole industry is on the money its owners have invested. An industry-wide return on equity of 20 percent means the typical dollar of equity in that industry earns 20 cents of profit a year.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can be excellent inside a poor industry. The industry number tells the reader whether the playing field itself rewards owners well, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | A structurally profitable industry, often protected by barriers that keep new competitors out. |
| Around the market average | A normal industry with ordinary competition. |
| Persistently low | A tough industry where competition keeps returns thin for almost everyone. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity`

Net income is profit after all costs. Shareholders' equity is the owners' stake. The industry figure is built by combining each company's profit and equity across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual capital is earning its return, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

The same three-part split used for a single company applies to the industry. An industry's return on equity rises from strong profit margins, from assets that produce a lot of sales, or from heavy use of debt. An industry that earns its return mainly through borrowing, such as banking, is structurally different from one that earns it through margins, such as branded software.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

Industry profitability is the backdrop against which every company in it is judged. When an industry earns a high return on equity year after year, the structure of that industry is favorable, which usually means something keeps new competitors from flooding in and competing the profits away. When an industry earns a low return for years, competition is fierce and even good companies struggle to reward owners.

This framing follows the idea that an industry's profitability is set by its competitive forces: the power of suppliers and customers, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, and the intensity of rivalry. A high and stable industry return on equity is the financial fingerprint of weak competitive forces. Judging the industry first tells the reader whether to expect tailwinds or headwinds before picking any single stock.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is industry structure. An industry with few competitors and high barriers to entry holds high returns, because outsiders cannot easily come in and compete them down. An industry that is easy to enter sees any high return quickly competed away. Industry structure changes slowly, which is why a high industry return often persists for years.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. The business cycle lifts the aggregate in good years and depresses it in recessions, and the swing is larger for industries whose sales rise and fall sharply with the economy. A wave of debt-funded buybacks across the industry can lift the aggregate without any real improvement. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries and against the broad market. An industry earning well above the market for many years is structurally attractive and tends to hold its advantage. An industry persistently below the market is one where capital struggles to earn a fair return.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the market, stable | Strong barriers to entry. A structurally rewarding place to own a business. |
| Near the market | Ordinary competition with no special structural edge. |
| Below the market for years | Heavy competition or chronic overcapacity keeping returns thin. |

Compare like with like. Industries that run on heavy borrowing, such as banks, will show high returns for structural reasons, so they should be compared with other lending industries rather than with capital-light ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate return on equity over several years. A high and steady line is the sign of a structurally profitable industry where the competitive forces stay weak. A line that drifts down over many years is a warning that the industry is maturing, that competition is intensifying, or that new entrants and substitutes are eroding the old advantage.

Separate the cycle from the structure. A sharp dip in a recession that recovers afterward is a cyclical swing, not a structural decline. A slow, steady erosion that does not recover with the economy is the more serious signal, because it points to a permanent change in how the industry competes. The trend matters more than any single year, because industry structure reveals itself over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** An industry's return on equity can be lifted by heavy borrowing across its members, which makes the industry look more profitable than it really is. Return on invested capital measures profit against all the capital in the industry, debt included, so borrowing cannot flatter it. Reading the two together shows whether the industry's profitability is real or leverage-driven.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry is high on both, the profitability is structural and trustworthy. When an industry is high on return on equity but only average on return on invested capital, the industry leans on debt, which makes the whole industry more fragile in a downturn. The gap between the two is a quick read on how much of the industry's apparent strength would survive harder financing conditions.

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity shows how profitable the industry is, while revenue growth shows how fast it is expanding. An industry can be highly profitable but shrinking, or fast-growing but unprofitable. Looking at both places the industry on the map of attractiveness.

**What the combination tells you.** An industry that is both highly profitable and growing is the most attractive backdrop for an investment, because owners earn well and the pie is getting bigger. High profitability with no growth describes a mature cash-generating industry. Fast growth with low profitability is a warning that competition is consuming the rewards of expansion.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between strong and weak members, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. Industries built on borrowing will always show high returns for structural reasons, so the raw number can mislead across very different industry types.

The figure also reflects the past, not the future. An industry can earn high returns right up to the moment a new technology or a new entrant changes its structure. A strong industry number tells the reader where profits have been, not where they are heading, so it must be paired with judgment about how the competitive forces are changing.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's return on equity above or below the broad market, and has the gap held for several years or only recently?
2. Does the industry's strength survive when measured by return on invested capital, or does it depend on heavy borrowing?
3. Is a high industry figure shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
4. What barrier keeps new competitors from entering this industry and competing the returns away?
5. Is the trend a cyclical swing that recovers with the economy, or a slow structural decline that does not?
6. Is the industry both profitable and growing, or profitable but shrinking?
7. Are there signs that a new technology, substitute product, or new entrant is about to change the industry's structure?
8. Does the high return reflect real pricing power, or temporary conditions such as a recent shortage?
