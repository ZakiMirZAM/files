# Strategic Groups

## What this section is

An industry is rarely one competition. It is usually several, running side by side, and the companies in each one are playing a different game from the companies in the next.

A strategic group is a set of companies inside one industry that compete in a similar way: similar breadth of product line, similar pricing, similar spending to defend the position. Porter's claim in Competitive Strategy Ch.7 is that these groups are not a way of tidying up a chart. They are a real feature of the industry. Groups exist because something stops a company in one group from copying the strategy of a better one. If nothing stopped it, every profitable strategy would be imitated and returns would flatten across the industry. They do not flatten. That is the evidence.

The thing doing the stopping is a mobility barrier, and the map is how you see it.

## Reading the chart

Every dot is a company. The axes are its operating margin and its market value. The dashed lines are the medians of the companies currently on the map, and they cut the space into four quadrants.

| Group | Where it sits | What being there usually means |
|---|---|---|
| Scale Leaders | High margin, large | Size and profitability together. Normally the tier with the strongest protection around it. |
| Premium Niche | High margin, small | Strong margins on a narrow position. Profitable, and exposed if a larger rival decides the niche is worth taking. |
| Volume Incumbents | Low margin, large | Big, competing on volume and cost. Size without pricing power. |
| Challengers | Low margin, small | Still building a position. The group most likely to try to move up, and the most likely to fail at it. |

Two badges sit on top of the quadrant. Debt above the peer median marks a company leveraged. Revenue growth above the peer median marks it fast-growing. A company with no margin or no market value on file is listed as unclassified rather than guessed into a quadrant.

Two things about this chart deserve to be said plainly.

**The medians move.** They are computed over the companies on the map, not against a fixed industry standard, so widening the company count can shift a company from one quadrant into another. That is not a defect. A strategic group only means something relative to the peers you measure against, and Porter's own advice is to map an industry more than one way before trusting any single picture. The company count control exists for exactly that.

**The axes are a compromise.** Margin and size are available for every listed company, which is why they are the two on offer. They are not always the dimensions that matter most. In soft drinks the real barriers are brand and distribution. In banking they are regulatory capital and the deposit franchise. Porter's first principle for choosing axes is that they should reflect the mobility barriers actually operating in that industry, and margin and size only sometimes do. Treat the map as a starting hypothesis about where the groups are, then ask whether the dimensions that produced it are the ones the industry really competes on.

## Mobility barriers

A mobility barrier is whatever raises the cost of moving from one position to a better one. Entry barriers are a special case of it: entry is movement from outside the industry to inside. Whatever keeps new entrants out is usually the same thing keeping existing companies from crossing between groups.

| Barrier | How it shows up | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | Smaller groups cannot match the cost base | Capex intensity, cost per unit |
| Brand | A price premium that survives a downturn | Gross margin gap, pricing power when demand falls |
| Distribution | The channels are already locked up | Exclusivity, shelf space, distributor economics |
| Switching costs | Customers are stuck | Churn, contract length, retention |
| Regulation | Licences, capital ratios, certification | Regulatory filings, compliance cost |
| Capital | The move itself is unaffordable | Capex the move needs, against the balance sheet that would fund it |
| Experience | Cost falls with accumulated volume | Cost trend against cumulative output |

The distinction worth holding onto is between barriers built from assets and barriers built from capability. A distribution network or a plant can be bought. A service culture, or thirty years of customer trust, cannot. Capability barriers are the durable ones.

## Why it matters for valuation

A discounted cash flow rests on two numbers: how far returns sit above the cost of capital, and how long they stay there. Strategic group analysis is where both come from.

Barrier height sets the duration. A company inside a group protected by brand, switching costs and scale can hold excess returns for a long time. A company in a group with nothing protecting it sees them competed away within a few years. Position inside the group sets the size of the spread: the scale leader within a group earns more on the same strategy than a smaller or later arrival.

The map also disciplines the terminal value. If groups are converging, meaning the distance between how they compete is shrinking, terminal returns belong closer to the cost of capital than a static reading would suggest. A company with no coherent group at all deserves a lower return and a shorter runway, because anything it improves can be copied at once.

Run a DCF without this and you are forecasting cash flows without knowing whether the conditions that produced them will hold.

## Where it misleads

| Trap | Why it bites |
|---|---|
| Reading financial similarity as strategic similarity | Two companies with the same margin are not necessarily running the same strategy. A group is defined by strategy. Margin is a consequence of it. |
| Trusting the axes because the data was available | Size and margin are easy to obtain. That is not the same as being the dimensions the industry competes on. |
| Treating an empty space as an opportunity | The map shows what exists. It does not prove that what is missing is possible. Some positions are empty because they were tried and they failed. |
| Ignoring the fight inside a group | A group can be well protected from outside and still earn poor returns, because its members are competing the profit away among themselves. |
| Reading a snapshot of an industry being rebuilt | When technology is redrawing distribution, today's map describes groups that are about to stop existing. |
| Blaming structure for what is really execution | Two companies in one group, one earning 18% and one 11%, usually face identical conditions. One is simply run better. That is a different finding, with a different half-life. |

*Source: Porter, Competitive Strategy, Ch.7 (Structural Analysis Within Industries) and Ch.2 (Generic Competitive Strategies). Besanko and others, Economics of Strategy, Ch.8, for the entry-barrier context.*
