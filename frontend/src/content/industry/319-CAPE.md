# CAPE

## Part 1: Definition

CAPE measures how expensive a business is compared with its earning power averaged over many years. The name stands for cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings. A plain price-to-earnings ratio divides price by a single year of profit, which can be misleading when that year was unusually good or bad. CAPE instead divides the price by an average of profit over the past ten years, with each year adjusted for inflation so older figures are comparable to recent ones. The long average smooths out the booms and busts of the business cycle and shows what a business earns through a full cycle. In the industry view this measure is combined across every company in the industry into one figure, weighted so that larger companies count more. The result describes how richly or cheaply the whole industry is priced against its long-run earning power. An industry-wide CAPE of 25 means the typical company in that industry is priced at 25 times its average inflation-adjusted profit of the past decade.

The industry figure answers a different question from a single company's figure. A single company can be cheap inside an industry that the market has bid up overall. The industry number tells the reader whether the field as a whole is expensive or cheap against its long-run earnings, before any single company is chosen.

| Industry-wide level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Persistently high | An industry the market prices richly, often because it expects strong future growth, which leaves less room for error. |
| Around the market average | A field priced in line with its long-run earning power. |
| Persistently low | A cheaply priced industry, either out of favor or in long-term decline, which can mean opportunity or a value trap. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`CAPE = Price ÷ Ten-Year Average of Inflation-Adjusted Earnings`

Price is the market value of the business. The denominator is profit averaged over the past ten years, with each year's profit adjusted upward for inflation so all ten are stated in today's money. The ten-year average is used because a single year of profit can be distorted by a boom or a recession, while a full decade captures earning power across the cycle. The approach follows the idea, proposed by Benjamin Graham and David Dodd and later built into a formal measure by Robert Shiller and John Campbell, that valuation should rest on smoothed earnings rather than one volatile year. The industry figure is built by combining each company's price and long-run earnings across the industry and weighting by company size, so a giant company influences the industry number more than a tiny one. Size weighting is used because it describes where the industry's actual market value sits relative to its earning power, rather than letting a small company swing the average.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to judge an industry

CAPE is a long-run valuation gauge that tells the reader whether an industry is cheap or expensive against the profit it earns through a full cycle. A plain price-to-earnings ratio can make a cyclical industry look cheap at the top of a boom, when one year of profit is unusually high, and expensive at the bottom of a bust, when profit is temporarily depressed. By averaging ten years of earnings, CAPE removes that distortion and shows the underlying valuation more honestly. This is most valuable for industries whose profits swing sharply with the economy, where a single year tells the reader very little.

This framing matters because the price paid for an industry shapes the return an owner can expect. An industry bought at a high CAPE has a lot of optimism already built into its price, which leaves little cushion if growth disappoints. An industry bought at a low CAPE carries more modest expectations, which can mean a better entry point or a field in genuine decline. Judging the industry's long-run valuation first tells the reader how much the market is already charging for the field, before any single stock is picked.

## Part 4: What influences the industry aggregate

The deepest driver is what the market expects from the industry's future. A field expected to grow fast and earn high returns for years is priced at a high CAPE, because investors pay up for that future. A field expected to stagnate or decline is priced at a low CAPE. Interest rates also matter, because low rates make future earnings more valuable today and lift the level at which whole industries trade. These forces set the broad altitude of the figure.

Shorter-term forces also move the number. A surge of enthusiasm for a theme can lift an industry's CAPE far above its long-run norm, as happened in past speculative episodes. A spell of depressed earnings at the bottom of a cycle is exactly what the ten-year average is meant to smooth, so CAPE should hold steadier than a plain price-to-earnings ratio through a downturn. A structural change in the industry, such as a permanent shift in profitability, can leave the ten-year earnings average stale for a while, distorting the figure until the old years roll off. A few very large companies can dominate the size-weighted figure, so the industry number may describe the giants more than the typical member.

## Part 5: How this industry compares with other industries

The industry number is most useful when ranked against other industries and against the broad market. An industry trading well above the market on CAPE carries high expectations and a thin margin for disappointment. An industry trading well below the market is either a bargain or a field the market believes is in lasting decline.

| Cross-industry pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Far above the market | High expectations already priced in, with little room for error if growth slows. |
| Near the market | A field priced roughly in line with its long-run earning power. |
| Far below the market | A cheap field, either a genuine bargain or a value trap in structural decline. |

Compare like with like. A high-growth industry will naturally trade at a higher CAPE than a mature one, so a rich figure is not automatically a warning, and a low figure is not automatically a bargain. The level must be judged against the field's growth prospects and against similar industries rather than across very different ones.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The industry chart shows the aggregate CAPE over several years. A figure hovering near the field's own long-run norm is the sign of an industry priced in a settled, reasonable way. A figure climbing far above its historical range is a warning that optimism may be running ahead of the field's earning power, which has often preceded weak long-run returns. A figure sinking far below its range can signal either a buying opportunity or a market judgment that the industry's best days are behind it.

Separate the cycle from the structure. Because CAPE already smooths earnings over ten years, large swings in the figure usually reflect changing prices and expectations rather than a single weak or strong year of profit. A rise that follows a genuine, lasting improvement in the field's earning power can be justified, while a rise driven only by enthusiasm tends to reverse. The trend matters more than any single reading, because a long-run valuation gauge speaks to returns over years, not months.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** CAPE shows how richly the market prices the industry against its past earning power, while revenue growth shows how fast the field is actually expanding. A high CAPE is only justified if growth is strong enough to support it. Reading the two together shows whether the price the market is charging matches the growth the field is delivering.

**What the combination tells you.** When an industry carries a high CAPE backed by strong, durable revenue growth, the rich price may be earned rather than speculative. When a high CAPE sits with weak or slowing growth, the field is priced on hope that its results do not support, which raises the risk of disappointing long-run returns. A low CAPE paired with healthy growth can point to an overlooked bargain. The pairing tells the reader whether the industry's valuation and its growth are in step.

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** CAPE measures how expensive the industry is against its long-run earnings, while the margin of safety measures the discount of price to an estimate of intrinsic value. Both speak to whether the field is cheap or dear, from different angles, so they cross-check each other. Reading them together gives a fuller read on the price being paid.

**What the combination tells you.** When a low CAPE lines up with a wide margin of safety, two independent gauges agree that the industry is cheaply priced, which strengthens the case. When a high CAPE sits with a negative margin of safety, both signals warn that the field is expensive and offers little cushion. When the two disagree, the reader should dig into why, since one measure may be distorted by a stale earnings average or an aggressive value estimate. The pairing turns a single valuation read into a more reliable judgment.

## Part 8: Limitations

The size-weighted industry number can be dominated by a few giant companies, so it may not describe the typical company in the industry. A high industry figure can hide a wide spread between richly and cheaply priced members, which means the industry average is a starting point, not a substitute for company-level work. The ten-year earnings average can become stale when an industry has changed structurally, because old years that no longer reflect the field's economics still weigh on the figure until they roll off.

The figure is also a guide to long-run returns, not a timing tool. An industry can stay expensive on CAPE for years before its price corrects, and a cheap field can stay cheap for just as long. A low CAPE can mark a genuine bargain or a value trap in lasting decline, and the measure alone cannot tell the two apart. Accounting changes over a decade can also make the earnings average less consistent. The metric tells the reader where the price stands against long-run earnings, not when or whether that price will move, so it must be paired with judgment about the field's future.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the industry's CAPE above or below the broad market, and how does it compare with the field's own long-run range?
2. Is a high CAPE justified by strong, durable growth, or is it built on optimism the field's results do not support?
3. Is a low CAPE a genuine bargain, or a value trap signaling the industry is in lasting decline?
4. Has the field changed structurally in a way that makes its ten-year earnings average stale and the figure misleading?
5. Do CAPE and the margin of safety agree that the industry is cheap or dear, or do they point in different directions?
6. Is a rich valuation shared across most companies, or is it produced by a few dominant members?
7. How much of the industry's CAPE level reflects low interest rates rather than the strength of the field itself?
8. Is the current figure near a historical extreme that has preceded weak long-run returns in the past?
