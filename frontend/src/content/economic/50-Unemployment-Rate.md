# Unemployment Rate (U-3)

## Part 1: Definition

The Unemployment Rate is the headline measure of labor-market slack in the United States. It is the percentage of the civilian labor force that does not have a job but is actively looking for one and is available to work. It is published every month by the Bureau of Labor Statistics from the household survey and is reported as the "U-3" rate, the figure most commonly quoted as *the* unemployment rate. The denominator is the labor force, defined as everyone who is either working or actively seeking work; people who are not looking are excluded from the labor force entirely and therefore do not raise the rate even when they want a job. This is why the U-3 rate can fall for a healthy reason (people finding jobs) or for an unhealthy reason (discouraged people leaving the labor force), and why it is read alongside the participation rate and the broader U-6 measure.

The rate is constructed by dividing the number of unemployed-but-searching people by the total labor force, then multiplying by 100. The "actively looking" condition is included because the metric is meant to capture people who are exerting pressure on the labor market, competing for jobs and bidding on wages, rather than everyone without work. The series is monthly and is the timeliest broad read on the employment side of the economy, which is why markets and the Federal Reserve treat each release as a primary input.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The unemployment rate matters for sector positioning because it is the single most reliable real-time signal of the business cycle's turning points. Household income drives roughly two-thirds of economic activity, and the unemployment rate is the cleanest gauge of whether that income base is expanding or contracting. When the rate is low and falling, households have rising income that funds discretionary spending, lifting Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Real Estate, and Industrials. When the rate is rising, those same cyclical sectors lose volume and pricing power while defensive sectors with inelastic demand, Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities, take leadership. The rate's cycle-timing reliability is its defining feature: a three-month average rising half a percentage point above its prior twelve-month low (the Sahm rule) has identified the onset of every postwar U.S. recession with almost no false positives, which makes a rising rate one of the few macro signals strong enough to justify a defensive rotation on its own.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low and stable (near cycle lows) | The labor market is tight and the economy is at or near full employment. Wage growth is firm and household income supports discretionary spending, but inflation pressure can build and eventually force a policy response. | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Real Estate, Industrials | Consumer Staples, Utilities |
| Falling from a high base | Slack is being absorbed as people return to work. Rising income supports a broad cyclical recovery while inflation pressure is still modest. | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Industrials, Information Technology | Consumer Staples, Utilities |
| Rising 0.5pp or more from a recent low | The Sahm-rule trigger. The labor market is turning and a recession is historically imminent even before output data confirms. Households pull back, savings rise, credit demand falls. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Real Estate, Industrials |
| Elevated with job losses | The economy is in contraction and household income is falling. Discretionary spending collapses and only sellers of essentials hold revenue. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Industrials, Materials |

## Part 4: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why to read it alongside the Unemployment Rate |
|---|---|
| Total Nonfarm Payrolls | Payrolls measure the *level* of jobs while the unemployment rate measures the *share* of the labor force without work. Read together they confirm whether a falling rate reflects genuine hiring (payrolls rising) or shrinking participation (payrolls flat while people leave the labor force). |
| Labor Force Participation Rate | A falling unemployment rate is only unambiguously healthy when participation is steady or rising. A rate that drops because discouraged workers exit the labor force is a hidden-weakness signal, not a strength signal. |
| U-6 Unemployment Rate | The gap between U-6 and the headline U-3 rate measures hidden slack, discouraged and involuntarily part-time workers. A widening gap warns of deterioration beneath a healthy-looking headline. |
| CPI Inflation | A tight labor market (low unemployment) generates wage pressure that feeds inflation. Reading the two together tells you whether the current employment level is sustainable or is forcing a policy tightening that will reshape sector leadership. |
