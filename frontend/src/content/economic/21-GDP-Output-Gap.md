# GDP Output Gap

## Part 1: Definition

The GDP Output Gap measures the difference between what the economy is actually producing and what it could produce if it used all its resources at sustainable levels. It is expressed as a percentage of potential output, where a negative gap means the economy is producing below its potential (with idle workers and factories) and a positive gap means the economy is producing above its sustainable potential (running hot). The figure is estimated quarterly alongside GDP data, with potential output projected by government and independent forecasters. The gap is useful because it reveals whether the economy has slack that allows non-inflationary growth or is overheating in a way that generates inflation.

The gap is constructed by subtracting estimated potential GDP from actual GDP and expressing the difference as a percentage of potential. Actual GDP is included because it is the real, measured output of the economy as captured in the national accounts. Potential GDP is included because it represents the level of output the economy could sustain when labor is fully employed at its natural rate and capital is used at normal levels, which serves as the benchmark for whether the economy is running below or above its capacity. Potential output is estimated from the size of the labor force, the capital stock, and the trend rate of productivity growth, because these are the inputs that determine how much the economy can produce over the long run. The gap is included as an indicator because the relationship between actual and potential output signals inflation pressure: when actual output exceeds potential, demand outstrips the economy's sustainable capacity and prices rise, while when actual output falls short, idle resources put downward pressure on prices and signal room for recovery.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The GDP Output Gap matters for sector investing because it reveals where the economy sits relative to its sustainable capacity, which signals both the room for further growth and the inflation pressure that shapes sector leadership. When the gap is deeply negative, the economy has substantial slack, meaning it can grow rapidly without generating inflation, which supports an early-cycle environment favorable to cyclical sectors like Consumer Discretionary, Financials, and Industrials. When the gap closes and turns positive, the economy is running above its sustainable potential, which generates inflation pressure that favors Energy and Materials and warns of a coming policy response and cycle peak. The metric matters because it ties together the growth and inflation signals into a single read on the cycle stage, which directly informs the cyclical-versus-defensive rotation. The output gap also guides expectations for monetary policy, since a positive gap pushes the central bank toward tightening while a negative gap supports easing. The combination of a cycle-stage signal, an inflation-pressure read, and a guide to policy direction makes the output gap a useful framework for positioning across the full cycle.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the estimate of potential output that underlies the gap reliable, given that potential output cannot be observed directly and must be estimated from assumptions about the labor force, capital stock, and productivity trend that can be revised? Because the gap depends on an unobservable estimate, the investor must consider how much confidence to place in the specific gap figure and watch for revisions that change the picture.

2. Is the gap negative because of weak demand (which calls for stimulus and signals room to recover) or because of damaged supply capacity (which means the economy cannot grow as much as the gap suggests without inflation)? A demand-driven gap supports a cyclical recovery, while a supply-damaged gap means apparent slack may not translate into non-inflationary growth.

3. Is the labor market signaling the same degree of slack as the output gap, or is one measure showing more tightness than the other in a way that changes the inflation outlook? When the labor and output measures of slack disagree, the inflation pressure is uncertain, which affects how to position between cyclical and inflation-benefiting sectors.

4. Has the productivity assumption built into potential output shifted because of new technology or structural changes, in a way that would raise or lower the estimate of how much the economy can produce? A change in the productivity trend changes potential output and therefore the gap, which affects the read on how much room the economy has to grow.

5. Is the gap closing because actual output is rising toward potential (a healthy recovery) or because potential output is being revised down toward actual output (a sign of damaged capacity)? The reason the gap closes matters, since a recovery-driven close is bullish for cyclicals while a downward potential revision signals lasting damage.

6. Is the economy approaching the point where the gap turns positive and inflation pressure builds, and what does that say about the timing of the rotation from early-cycle to late-cycle sectors? Because the gap turning positive marks the shift toward inflation pressure, anticipating that point helps time the move toward late-cycle Energy and Materials.

7. Is the gap consistent with what other slack indicators such as capacity utilization and the broad unemployment rate are showing, or do they disagree about how much room the economy has? Confirmation across slack measures strengthens the cycle read, while disagreement requires judging which measure better captures the current conditions.

8. How quickly is the gap changing, and does the speed of change suggest the economy is moving rapidly through the cycle or slowly, which affects how soon sector rotations should occur? A rapidly closing gap calls for faster rotation than a slowly closing one, which affects the timing of sector shifts.

9. Is the central bank likely to respond to the current gap by tightening or easing policy, and how would that policy response affect rate-sensitive sectors? Because the gap guides policy, anticipating the central bank's response helps position rate-sensitive sectors ahead of the policy move.

10. Is the gap large enough and the direction clear enough to warrant a meaningful sector rotation, or is it small and uncertain enough that other indicators should carry more weight in the decision? When the gap is small or its estimate is uncertain, other cycle indicators may provide a clearer signal for sector positioning.
