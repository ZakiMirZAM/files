# Bank Net Interest Margin

## Part 1: Definition

Bank Net Interest Margin measures the difference between the interest income that banks earn on their loans and investments and the interest they pay on their deposits and borrowing, expressed as a percentage of their interest-earning assets. It captures the core profitability of traditional banking, since banks make money by lending at higher rates than they pay to fund those loans. The data is reported quarterly as banks release earnings, and industry-wide figures are compiled from regulatory filings. A wider margin means banks are earning more on the spread between lending and funding, while a narrower margin means that spread is compressing.

The margin is constructed by subtracting the interest banks pay from the interest they earn and dividing by their interest-earning assets. The interest income is included because it represents what banks earn on their loans, mortgages, and securities holdings, which is the revenue side of traditional banking. The interest expense is included because it represents what banks pay to fund those assets through deposits and borrowing, which is the cost side. Dividing the difference by interest-earning assets is included because expressing the margin as a percentage of the asset base allows comparison across banks of different sizes and over time. The margin is included as an indicator because it captures the fundamental profitability of lending, which depends on the gap between the rates banks charge and the rates they pay. The margin is shaped by the level and shape of interest rates, the competition for deposits, and the mix of assets and funding, making it a direct gauge of how profitable the core lending business is under current conditions.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Bank Net Interest Margin matters for sector investing because it is the core profitability gauge for the banking industry, which makes it a direct driver of earnings for the Financials sector and a signal about the interest rate environment that affects rate-sensitive sectors. When margins are widening, banks are earning more on the spread between lending and funding, which directly increases their profitability and benefits the Financials sector. When margins are compressing, bank profitability falls, which pressures Financials and can signal an interest rate environment, such as an inverted yield curve, that also warns of broader economic concerns. The metric matters with particular force for Financials because net interest income is the largest revenue source for most banks, so the margin directly determines their earnings. The margin also reflects the shape of the yield curve and the level of rates, which connects it to the broader rate environment that affects Real Estate, Utilities, and other rate-sensitive sectors. The combination of a direct earnings link to banks and a signal about the rate environment makes the margin a focused but important input for positioning Financials and reading rate conditions.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the margin widening because long-term lending rates are rising faster than short-term funding costs, which reflects a healthy upward-sloping yield curve, or because of other factors such as a shift in the bank's asset mix? A curve-driven widening reflects a favorable rate environment, while a mix-driven change reflects bank-specific decisions.

2. Is the margin compressing because the yield curve has flattened or inverted, which would also signal recession risk, or because of intense deposit competition that is raising funding costs? A curve-driven compression carries a broader economic warning, while a deposit-competition compression is more specific to bank funding dynamics.

3. Are banks able to keep deposit costs low as rates rise, which would widen margins, or are depositors demanding higher rates and shifting to higher-yielding alternatives, which would compress margins? The stickiness of deposit costs is a key driver of whether rising rates help or hurt bank margins.

4. Is the margin being affected by the mix of the bank's assets, such as the balance between fixed-rate and floating-rate loans, which determines how quickly lending income responds to rate changes? Banks with more floating-rate loans benefit faster from rising rates, while those with fixed-rate loans see slower margin improvement.

5. Are banks holding large amounts of low-yielding securities purchased when rates were low, which would drag on margins as funding costs rise, creating a hidden squeeze? When banks are stuck with low-yielding assets funded at rising costs, margins compress in a way that may not be obvious from the rate environment alone.

6. Is the margin trend consistent across large banks and smaller regional banks, or are they diverging in a way that signals different funding pressures? Divergence between large and regional banks can reveal where funding stress is concentrated within the sector.

7. Is the margin change being accompanied by changes in loan volume, since bank profitability depends on both the margin and the amount of lending? A widening margin with falling volumes may not improve overall profitability, so the two must be read together.

8. Is the margin trend consistent with what the yield curve and interest rates are signaling, or is there a divergence that suggests bank-specific factors are at work? Confirmation from the rate environment strengthens the read, while a divergence points to factors specific to the banking industry.

9. Are rising margins being offset by rising credit losses, since the same rising-rate environment that widens margins can also strain borrowers and increase defaults? A widening margin accompanied by rising loan losses may not translate into higher net profitability, which affects the read on Financials.

10. Is the change in the margin large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect bank profitability and signal a shift in the rate environment, or is it a quarterly fluctuation that does not change the picture? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants adjusting Financials positioning, since margins can fluctuate quarter to quarter for temporary reasons.
