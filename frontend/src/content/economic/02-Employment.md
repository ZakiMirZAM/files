# Employment

## Part 1: Definition

The Employment metric tracks how many people in the working-age population currently have jobs, expressed two ways: the total number of people on company payrolls, measured in thousands of jobs added or lost each month, and the unemployment rate, which is the percentage of the labor force that is actively looking for work but does not have a job. Both numbers are released every month and capture the labor market position of the prior month. The most cited figure for sector investing is the net change in nonfarm payroll jobs added in the past month and the level of the unemployment rate.

The metric is constructed from two separate surveys conducted on the same reference week of each month. The first survey collects payroll counts directly from a large sample of businesses across the country, covering manufacturing, services, construction, government, and most other industries. These industries are included because they represent the formal labor market where most economic activity occurs and where workers receive a regular wage that supports consumption. Farm employment is excluded because it is highly seasonal and would distort the trend if included. The second survey calls a representative sample of households and asks who is working, who is looking for work, and who is neither. Both surveys are needed because the payroll survey misses self-employed workers and the household survey is too small to give reliable industry breakdowns. The unemployment rate is then calculated by dividing the number of people in the household survey who say they are actively looking for work by the total labor force (people working plus people looking for work). People who have stopped looking are excluded from the labor force because they are not exerting pressure on wages or competing for jobs, so including them would inflate the unemployment rate without reflecting actual labor market slack.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Employment is one of the highest-priority macroeconomic inputs for sector investing because it directly drives household income, which in turn drives roughly two thirds of all economic activity and a much larger share of revenue for consumer-facing sectors. When payrolls are expanding and the unemployment rate is falling, households have more money to spend on cars, vacations, homes, and dining out, which lifts Consumer Discretionary, Real Estate, and parts of Industrials. When payrolls are contracting and unemployment is rising, those same sectors lose pricing power and volumes while defensive sectors with inelastic demand (Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities) outperform. Employment also signals the cycle phase with unusual reliability because rising unemployment from a low base has preceded every postwar recession with very few false signals, and falling unemployment from a high base has marked every sustained expansion. Beyond pure cyclicality, employment changes affect wage pressure, which then feeds into inflation and forces a monetary policy response that further reshapes sector leadership. The combination of direct revenue linkage, cycle-phase signaling, and feedback into the inflation regime makes Employment data the most useful single labor indicator for choosing between cyclical and defensive positioning.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unemployment falling below 4% with strong monthly payroll gains above 200,000 | The labor market is tight. Firms are competing for workers, wages are growing, and households have rising income that funds discretionary spending. This typically occurs in mid to late expansion when the economy is operating near full capacity. Consumer-facing sectors capture the spending growth, financial services benefit from loan demand and asset growth, and industrials benefit from continued business investment. The risk is that wage pressure starts to feed inflation, which eventually forces a policy response that ends the cycle. | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Real Estate, Industrials, Information Technology, Communication Services | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Energy, Materials |
| Unemployment stable around 4 to 5% with moderate monthly payroll gains between 100,000 and 200,000 | The labor market is at full employment with sustainable hiring. Households have stable income, but the economy is not generating accelerating wage pressure. This is the mid-cycle steady state where most sectors grow at trend rates. Sector leadership becomes harder to identify from this signal alone, and stock selection inside sectors matters more. | Information Technology, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, Communication Services | Energy, Materials | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Real Estate |
| Unemployment rising from a recent low by 0.5 percentage points or more | The labor market is turning, which historically signals an imminent recession even before output data confirms. Households start to pull back on discretionary purchases, savings rates rise, and credit demand falls. Cyclical sectors face revenue compression and margin pressure. Defensive sectors gain leadership as investors rotate toward earnings stability. A 3-month average unemployment rate rising 0.5 percentage points above its 12-month low has identified every postwar US recession in real time. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Real Estate, Industrials, Information Technology, Communication Services, Materials, Energy | (none) |
| Unemployment above 6% with payroll losses | The labor market is in contraction, the economy is in recession, and household income is falling. Discretionary spending collapses, residential investment falls, and credit losses rise. Only sectors selling essentials maintain stable revenue. The phase typically lasts 6 to 18 months and ends when policy easing combined with depleted inventories drives a recovery. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Industrials, Information Technology, Materials, Real Estate, Communication Services, Energy | (none) |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer Discretionary | Household income funds discretionary spending, and rising payrolls directly increase the income pool. Auto sales, restaurant traffic, hotel occupancy, and big-box retail all rise with employment. | Buy when payrolls are growing above 150,000 per month and unemployment is falling. Avoid when unemployment is rising 0.3 percentage points or more from its recent low. |
| Financials | Bank loan demand depends on employed borrowers who can service mortgages, auto loans, and credit cards. Insurance demand depends on jobs that generate income to insure. Asset managers earn on rising wealth that is supported by job growth. | Buy when employment is expanding and credit conditions are easy. Avoid when unemployment is rising rapidly because loan defaults follow. |
| Industrials | Industrial production tracks consumer demand and business investment, both of which depend on employment. Transportation services, freight volumes, and capital goods orders all scale with the labor market. | Buy when payrolls are growing and capacity utilization is rising. Avoid in recessions when hiring stops and inventories build. |
| Information Technology | Both household tech spending (devices, services) and business IT spending (software, cloud, infrastructure) scale with employment. AI-driven productivity gains can offset some employment weakness for tech specifically. | Buy when employment is stable to growing in mid expansion. Less negative in mild downturns because business IT spending is more resilient than consumer goods. |
| Real Estate | Residential demand depends on household formation, which depends on employment. Commercial demand (office, retail, industrial space) depends on hiring and business activity. | Buy when employment is rising in low-rate environments. Avoid when unemployment is rising because vacancy rates rise. |
| Materials | Materials demand follows industrial production, which follows employment-driven activity. Construction materials and chemicals are particularly cyclical. | Buy in late expansion with full employment driving capacity tightness. Avoid in recessions when industrial output contracts. |
| Communication Services | Advertising revenue depends on corporate marketing budgets that scale with consumer spending power, which depends on employment. Subscription revenue is more stable. | Buy when employment supports advertising growth. Less negative in recessions because subscriptions are sticky. |
| Consumer Staples | Demand for food, household products, and personal care holds steady through employment cycles because consumption is inelastic. The sector wins relative leadership during job-loss recessions. | Buy when unemployment is rising or in recession. Underperforms in tight labor markets when cyclicals dominate. |
| Health Care | Health care utilization is largely independent of employment because medical needs do not pause for layoffs, though insurance coverage matters at the margin. Demographic-driven demand provides structural growth. | Defensive default that performs across employment phases. Particularly favored when employment is deteriorating. |
| Utilities | Utility demand is essential and largely independent of employment. The sector behaves defensively and also responds to interest rate changes that interact with the cycle. | Buy when unemployment is rising and rates are falling. Avoid in strong labor markets when rates rise and cyclicals dominate. |
| Energy | Energy demand depends on overall economic activity, which is tied to employment. Industrial energy use and transportation fuel demand both scale with employment-driven activity. | Buy in late expansion when full employment supports peak energy demand. Avoid in recessions when energy demand falls with activity. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside Employment | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| GDP Growth | Employment is the labor input to the same economic activity that GDP measures as output. Reading the two together confirms whether labor market signals reflect genuine real-economy strength or just statistical noise, because in real expansions both rise together. The combination also reveals whether growth is being driven by adding workers (extensive margin) or by productivity gains per worker (intensive margin). | When employment is rising AND GDP growth is strong, the expansion is broad and healthy and cyclical sectors deserve overweight positions. When employment is rising but GDP growth is weak (low productivity), the favored sectors shift toward consumer-facing names because the labor market is supporting household income. When GDP growth is strong but employment is flat (high productivity), Information Technology and capital-light businesses lead because they capture productivity gains. When both are falling, defensive sectors lead because the contraction is real. |
| CPI Inflation | Employment and inflation are linked through wage pressure. A tight labor market generates wage growth that eventually feeds into prices, while a loose labor market suppresses wages and inflation. Reading the two together tells investors whether the current employment level is sustainable or is creating inflationary pressure that will force policy tightening. | When unemployment is falling AND CPI is rising, the economy is in a wage-price feedback that favors Energy and Materials and hurts long-duration sectors like Information Technology. When unemployment is falling AND CPI is stable or falling, productivity is offsetting wage pressure and the favored sectors expand to include Information Technology and Consumer Discretionary. When unemployment is rising AND CPI is rising, defensives like Consumer Staples and Health Care lead because they can pass through prices without depending on labor market strength. |
| M2 Money Supply | Money supply growth funds the credit that supports business hiring and household spending. Reading employment alongside M2 reveals whether the labor market expansion has monetary support behind it or is running on past stimulus. The combination also signals whether the central bank is providing enough liquidity to maintain the expansion. | When employment is expanding AND M2 is growing, the expansion has monetary support and cyclical sectors can run further. When employment is expanding but M2 is contracting, the labor market is running on momentum without policy support and a slowdown becomes more likely within 12 months, which argues for adding Health Care and Consumer Staples positions. When employment is contracting and M2 is expanding, policy easing is in the pipeline that should support a cyclical recovery, which favors early-cycle sectors. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Did the most recent monthly change in nonfarm payrolls come from broad-based hiring across many industries, or was it concentrated in just one or two industries (such as health care and government, which often dominate the headline)? Concentrated gains in just a few industries suggest the labor market is not as healthy as the headline implies, and the implications for which sectors benefit differ because broad hiring lifts consumer income across the economy while concentrated hiring lifts only specific sub-segments.

2. How does the official monthly payroll number compare to the household survey count of total employed people, and what does any gap between the two surveys say about the reliability of the headline reading? When the household survey shows weaker job growth than the payroll survey, the labor market is likely weaker than the headline suggests, and defensive positioning becomes more appropriate.

3. Of the change in the unemployment rate, what proportion came from people losing jobs (a true sign of weakness) versus people entering the labor force to look for work (a sign of strength), versus people leaving the labor force (which masks weakness in the headline)? Each driver carries a different signal for sector positioning, and a rising rate driven by labor force entry is much less alarming than one driven by job losses.

4. Are wages growing faster than the rate of consumer price inflation, generating real income growth for workers, or is wage growth being eroded by rising prices? Real wage growth supports Consumer Discretionary spending, while falling real wages force households to trade down to Consumer Staples and reduce big-ticket purchases.

5. What is the rate at which workers are voluntarily quitting their jobs, and what does this rate say about worker confidence in finding new jobs? A high quits rate signals labor market strength because workers are confident in finding better positions, while a falling quits rate signals deteriorating conditions and supports defensive sector positioning.

6. Has the unemployment rate's 3-month average risen by 0.5 percentage points or more above its 12-month low, which has historically identified the start of every postwar US recession in real time? If this trigger has fired, defensive sector positioning becomes mandatory even if other economic data still looks strong because the labor market signal has been remarkably reliable.

7. Are job openings rising or falling relative to the number of unemployed people looking for work? When openings exceed unemployed by a wide margin, the labor market is tight and supports wage growth, while a falling ratio signals labor market loosening and weaker household income growth ahead.

8. Is hiring concentrated in cyclical sectors (manufacturing, construction, leisure and hospitality) or in non-cyclical sectors (health care, education, government)? Cyclical sector hiring confirms a real-economy expansion and supports cyclical stock positioning, while non-cyclical hiring may mask underlying weakness in the productive economy.

9. Is the labor force participation rate (the share of the working-age population that is either working or looking for work) rising or falling, and how does this affect the interpretation of the unemployment rate? A rising rate accompanied by falling unemployment is the healthiest combination, while a falling participation rate that drops unemployment mathematically (without job gains) is a hidden weakness signal.

10. Are temporary help and staffing services employment numbers rising or falling, and what do these movements signal about the broader labor market 3 to 6 months out? Temporary employment is one of the most cyclical labor indicators and often turns before the headline payroll numbers, so falling temporary employment is an early warning that supports rotation toward defensive sectors.
