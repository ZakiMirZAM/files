# VIX

## Part 1: Definition

The VIX measures the level of volatility that options markets expect in the US stock market over the next thirty days, often called the market's fear gauge. It is expressed as an annualized percentage, where higher numbers mean the market expects larger price swings and lower numbers mean it expects calm. The index updates continuously through the trading day. It is derived from the prices investors pay for options on a broad stock market index, since those prices rise when investors expect turbulence and demand protection, and fall when investors are calm.

The VIX is constructed from the prices of options on a broad stock market index across a range of strike prices. Options prices are included because they reflect what investors are willing to pay to protect against or bet on future price moves, and that price embeds the market's expectation of how much the index will swing. Options across many strike prices, both above and below the current market level, are included because combining them captures the full range of expected outcomes rather than just one scenario. The prices are combined using a formula that translates them into a single expected volatility figure for the next thirty days. The index is included as an indicator because the price of options protection rises when investors grow fearful about potential declines and falls when they are complacent, making the VIX a real-time gauge of market fear and expected turbulence. A low VIX signals calm and complacency, a high VIX signals fear and expected large swings, and sudden spikes signal acute stress as investors rush to buy protection.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The VIX matters for sector investing because it gauges the level of fear and expected turbulence in the market, which signals the risk environment and, at extremes, serves as a contrarian indicator for the rotation between aggressive and defensive sectors. When the VIX is low, the market is calm and complacent, which supports a risk-on environment where aggressive cyclical and growth sectors can perform, though very low levels can signal complacency that precedes turbulence. When the VIX spikes, fear is high and investors are demanding protection, which pressures aggressive sectors in the moment but, at extremes, often marks a bottom that precedes a rebound favoring cyclical and growth sectors. The metric matters because volatility regimes affect which sectors perform, with defensive sectors holding up better during high-volatility periods and aggressive sectors leading during calm periods. The VIX also provides a contrarian signal at extremes, since peak fear often coincides with market bottoms. The combination of a risk-environment read and a contrarian signal at extremes makes the VIX a useful input for judging when to favor defensive versus aggressive sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the VIX at a genuine extreme that has historically marked a turning point, or merely elevated or low within its normal range where it provides less of a contrarian signal? The contrarian value of the VIX comes at true extremes, so a reading in the normal range should not drive a contrarian sector shift.

2. Is the level of expected volatility matched by actual realized volatility in the market, or is the VIX pricing in fear that has not materialized in actual price swings? When implied volatility far exceeds realized volatility, the market may be overpricing fear, which can present a contrarian opportunity.

3. Is the volatility spike being driven by a genuine economic or financial threat, or by a temporary market shock that is likely to fade without lasting damage? A spike driven by a real threat justifies defensive positioning, while a temporary shock often presents a contrarian buying opportunity.

4. Is the term structure of volatility showing that near-term fear exceeds longer-term expectations, which signals acute current stress, or the reverse, which signals calm? The shape of the volatility term structure reveals whether the fear is concentrated in the near term or expected to persist.

5. Is the VIX spike confirmed by stress in other markets such as credit spreads and funding markets, or is the equity fear isolated? Confirmation across stress measures signals genuine broad-based risk, while isolated equity fear may be a temporary shock.

6. Is very low volatility signaling genuine calm or dangerous complacency that often precedes a turbulence spike, given that extended periods of low volatility have historically preceded sharp corrections? Sustained low volatility can mask building risk, so complacency is itself a warning.

7. Are volatility-targeting and systematic strategies amplifying the volatility move by mechanically buying or selling in response to volatility changes, which can exaggerate spikes? When systematic strategies amplify volatility, the spike can overshoot, which affects how to read the extreme.

8. Is the fear driven by a specific identifiable event that will resolve, or by a general unease that could persist? Event-driven fear fades when the event resolves, while general unease can persist and justify continued caution.

9. How does the current VIX level compare to past episodes, and does it match the levels seen in genuine crises or merely in routine pullbacks? The VIX level relative to historical episodes reveals whether the fear is at crisis levels that demand defense or routine levels that may present opportunity.

10. Is the volatility move large enough and sustained enough to warrant a shift in sector positioning, or is it a brief spike that will reverse before any rotation pays off? Only a sustained shift in the volatility regime warrants repositioning, since brief spikes often reverse quickly.
