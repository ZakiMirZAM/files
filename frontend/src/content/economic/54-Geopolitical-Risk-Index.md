# Geopolitical Risk Index (GPR)

## Part 1: Definition

The Geopolitical Risk index measures the level of geopolitical tension and conflict in the world as reflected in the news. It is built by Dario Caldara and Matteo Iacoviello, who count how often major newspapers carry articles about adverse geopolitical events and risks, including wars, military build-ups, terrorism, and tensions between nations, and turn that frequency into a monthly index relative to its own history. Higher readings mean greater geopolitical risk. The index distinguishes between threats, which capture rising tensions and risks, and acts, which capture realized events, and it extends back over a century, so current readings can be judged against a long historical record. Because it is drawn from actual news coverage rather than forecasts, it captures the climate of geopolitical risk as it is being experienced.

The index is constructed by measuring the share of newspaper coverage devoted to geopolitical tension and conflict and normalizing it into a monthly series. A rising level is interpreted as a more dangerous geopolitical climate, the kind that disrupts trade and supply chains, raises energy and commodity prices, and prompts investors to seek safety. Spikes typically cluster around wars, major terrorist attacks, and acute international confrontations. Unlike the economic-policy-uncertainty indices, which capture uncertainty about the rules of economic policy, the GPR captures the risk of conflict itself, so the two measures describe different sources of risk and are most informative when read together.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The GPR matters for sector investing because geopolitical conflict disrupts trade, supply chains, and energy markets, and prompts flights to safety, which favors particular sectors and pressures others. When geopolitical risk is low, the climate is calm and supports a risk-on environment in which globally exposed cyclical and growth sectors can lead, because trade and supply chains operate smoothly. When geopolitical risk is high, the climate is dangerous and pressures globally exposed cyclicals and trade-dependent sectors first, while benefiting sectors that gain from conflict-driven supply disruptions and safe-haven demand. The metric matters with particular force for Energy, which often rises when conflict threatens oil and gas supply, for Aerospace and Defense within Industrials, which benefits from elevated military spending, and for globally exposed cyclicals such as Materials and Information Technology, whose supply chains and demand are most disrupted by conflict. Because the index captures conflict rather than economic policy, it provides a distinct lens that complements the policy-uncertainty indices.

|---|---|---|
| Energy | Conflict often threatens oil and gas production or transit, raising prices and benefiting energy producers, so high geopolitical risk supports the sector while a calm climate removes that premium. | Buy when GPR is high or spiking. Less favored when GPR is low. |
| Industrials (Aerospace & Defense) | Elevated geopolitical risk drives higher military spending and defense procurement, so high GPR benefits the defense components of Industrials while the broader cyclical components face supply-chain pressure. | Buy defense components when GPR is high or spiking. Broader Industrials are pressured. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples sell essential goods with stable demand that holds up when conflict disrupts the broader economy, so the sector gains relative appeal as a defensive destination when geopolitical risk is high. | Buy when GPR is high or spiking. Underperforms when low GPR favors risk-on sectors. |
| Utilities | Utilities provide stable, regulated, domestically focused cash flows insulated from global conflict, so they serve as a safe-haven destination when geopolitical risk rises. | Buy when GPR is high or spiking. Avoid when low GPR favors risk-on sectors. |
| Health Care | Health care demand is non-discretionary and insulated from conflict, so the sector serves as a defensive destination when high geopolitical risk pressures cyclicals. | Buy when GPR is high or spiking. Defensive default in dangerous climates. |
| Information Technology | Tech depends on global supply chains and cross-border demand, so high geopolitical risk that disrupts supply and chills risk appetite pressures the sector, while a calm climate supports it. | Buy when GPR is low. Avoid when it is high or spiking. |
| Materials | Materials depend on global trade and construction, so conflict that disrupts supply chains and demand pressures the sector, though commodity-linked names can benefit if conflict raises specific commodity prices. | Buy when GPR is low. Commodity-linked names can offset during supply shocks. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Discretionary demand depends on confident households, so high geopolitical risk that dents confidence and raises energy costs pressures the sector, while a calm climate supports spending. | Buy when GPR is low. Avoid when it is high or spiking. |
| Financials | Financials benefit from the risk-on environment a low GPR supports, while conflict-driven flights to safety and market disruption can curb activity, giving the sector exposure to the geopolitical climate. | Buy when GPR is low. Avoid when it is high or spiking. |
| Real Estate | Real estate is largely domestic and income-driven, so it is relatively insulated from geopolitical risk, though conflict-driven moves in interest rates and energy costs can affect it indirectly. | Less sensitive to GPR directly. Read alongside interest-rate and energy signals. |
| Communication Services | The mix of stable telecom and globally exposed media and platform companies gives the sector mixed exposure, with the growth components more sensitive to conflict-driven risk aversion than the utility-like telecoms. | Buy growth components when GPR is low. Telecom components are more insulated. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the Geopolitical Risk Index | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Oil (WTI Crude) | Geopolitical conflict often transmits to markets through energy prices, so reading the GPR alongside oil shows whether rising risk is actually disrupting energy supply or is contained. | When high GPR coincides with rising oil prices, the conflict is hitting energy markets, strongly favoring Energy and pressuring energy-consuming sectors. When high GPR leaves oil prices calm, the risk is not transmitting through energy, which tempers the case for an energy overweight. |
| Global Economic Policy Uncertainty | The EPU captures economic-policy uncertainty while the GPR captures conflict risk, so reading them together separates policy-driven from conflict-driven risk. | When both are elevated, the risk environment is severe and broad defensive positioning is warranted. When the GPR is high but the EPU is calm, the issue is conflict rather than policy, which sharpens the case for conflict beneficiaries like Energy and Defense over a broad defensive rotation. |
| VIX | The GPR measures geopolitical risk while the VIX measures current market fear, so reading them together shows whether rising geopolitical risk has been priced into market volatility yet. | When the GPR is high but the VIX is low, geopolitical risk is building during market complacency, a setup that favors adding conflict beneficiaries and defensives before volatility rises. When both are elevated, the case for safe-haven and conflict-beneficiary sectors is strongest. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the elevated reading driven by realized conflict (acts) or by rising tensions and threats, since realized events tend to have more durable market effects?

2. Is the geopolitical risk concentrated in a region that controls critical energy or commodity supply, which would amplify its effect on Energy and Materials?

3. Is the rise in the index actually transmitting to markets through energy prices, supply chains, or safe-haven flows, or is it contained to news coverage?

4. How does the current level compare with past geopolitical shocks, and did those episodes produce lasting moves in Energy and Defense or prove short-lived?

5. Is the conflict likely to be brief and contained or prolonged and escalating, since the duration determines whether sector rotations should be tactical or sustained?

6. Is the geopolitical risk accompanied by elevated economic-policy uncertainty, indicating a compounded risk environment, or is it a standalone conflict event?

7. Are conflict-beneficiary sectors like Energy and Defense already pricing in the elevated risk, or is there still room for them to appreciate?

8. Is the risk prompting a flight to safety in Treasuries and the dollar that would compound pressure on globally exposed cyclicals while supporting defensives?

9. Could the conflict disrupt specific supply chains in a way that pressures particular industries within the broad cyclical sectors more than others?

10. Is the spike sustained over several months or a short-lived reaction that markets are likely to look through, given that the index is monthly and can be volatile around events?
