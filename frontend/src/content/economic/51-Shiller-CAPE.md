# Shiller CAPE Ratio (PE10)

## Part 1: Definition

The Shiller CAPE ratio measures how expensive the US stock market is relative to its long-run earning power. The name stands for cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings, and it is also called PE10. A plain price-to-earnings ratio divides the market's price by a single year of profit, which can be misleading when that year was unusually strong or weak. CAPE instead divides the real (inflation-adjusted) price of the S&P 500 by the average of inflation-adjusted earnings over the trailing ten years. Averaging a full decade of profit smooths out the booms and busts of the business cycle and shows what the market earns through a complete cycle rather than at one moment. The measure was popularized by Robert Shiller and John Campbell, building on Benjamin Graham and David Dodd's idea that valuation should rest on smoothed earnings. It updates monthly as new price and earnings data arrive. A CAPE of 30 means investors are paying thirty times the market's average inflation-adjusted profit of the past ten years.

The ratio is constructed by taking the real price level of the index and dividing it by ten-year average real earnings. Real earnings are used so that profits from a decade ago are stated in today's money and are comparable to recent profits, and the ten-year window is used because it captures earning power across an entire cycle rather than a single distorted year. The level is interpreted as a long-horizon valuation and expected-return gauge: a high CAPE means the market is richly priced and has historically been followed by lower real returns over the next decade, while a low CAPE means the market is cheaply priced and has historically been followed by stronger long-run returns.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Shiller CAPE matters for sector investing because it gauges how richly the overall market is priced against its through-cycle earnings, which signals how much optimism is already built into prices and which kinds of stocks carry the most valuation risk. When CAPE is low, the market is cheaply valued and a lot of pessimism is priced in, which supports a risk-on environment where cyclical and growth sectors can lead because investors are paying modest prices for future earnings. When CAPE is high, the market is expensive and a great deal of optimism is already embedded in prices, which pressures the most expensive long-duration sectors first because their valuations depend on that optimism being sustained. The metric matters most for long-duration growth sectors like Information Technology and Communication Services, whose high valuations are most exposed when the market's overall valuation is stretched, and it provides useful context for defensive, lower-valuation sectors that tend to hold up better when the market is expensive. Because CAPE speaks to returns over years rather than months, it is a longer-horizon input that frames how much room sectors have to appreciate from current prices.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

|---|---|---|
| Equity Risk Premium (implied) | The implied ERP measures the market's expected return over bonds, while CAPE measures its price against long-run earnings, so reading them together cross-checks the valuation signal from two angles. | S&P 500 Earnings Yield | The earnings yield is the inverse of valuation and the basis for the Fed-model comparison to bond yields, so reading it alongside CAPE confirms whether the market is cheap or expensive from a complementary angle. | When a high CAPE pairs with a low earnings yield that sits below the Treasury yield, the market is clearly expensive relative to bonds, favoring defensives. ## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the current CAPE high or low relative to its own long-run history, rather than simply in absolute terms, since the level that matters is the one judged against past norms?

2. Are the ten-year average earnings distorted by an unusual period within the window, such as a deep recession or a one-time write-down spike, that will roll off and change the figure?

3. How much of the current CAPE level reflects low interest rates rather than genuine optimism about earning power, given that low rates lift the valuation the whole market can support?

4. Is the market's CAPE being pulled up by a small number of very large companies whose high valuations dominate the index, masking cheaper value in the rest of the market?

5. Has the structure of the market changed in a way that justifies a permanently higher CAPE, such as a shift toward higher-margin, capital-light businesses, or is the elevated level purely speculative?

6. Is CAPE near a historical extreme that has preceded weak long-run returns in the past, and does that argue for caution on the most expensive sectors?

7. Are accounting standards consistent across the ten-year earnings window, or have changes made older and newer profit figures less comparable?

8. Does the current CAPE align with where the economy sits in the business cycle, given that valuations tend to be richest late in expansions and cheapest near troughs?

9. Is a high CAPE confirmed by other valuation gauges such as the implied equity risk premium and the earnings yield, or do the measures disagree in a way that warrants investigation?

10. Is the change in CAPE large and sustained enough to warrant a meaningful, longer-horizon sector rotation, or is it a short-term move in prices that a long-run valuation gauge should largely look through?
