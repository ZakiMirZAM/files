# Import Price Index

## Part 1: Definition

The Import Price Index measures the change in the prices that US buyers pay for goods and services purchased from foreign sellers, comparing the current period to a prior period. The index is expressed as a level relative to a base period and is most usefully tracked as a percentage change over a month or a year. The data is released monthly and covers the prior month's import transactions. The index measures the prices of goods at the point they enter the country, before any US tariffs, transportation, or retail markups are added.

The index is constructed by collecting the prices of a wide range of imported products and services and weighting each by its share of total US import value. Imported goods categories are included because physical products such as crude oil, consumer electronics, automobiles, apparel, and industrial supplies make up the bulk of what US companies and households buy from abroad, and these prices directly affect the input costs of US producers and the shelf prices of imported consumer goods. Imported services are included to a lesser degree because foreign services such as transportation and travel also represent costs that US buyers pay. Each product's price is measured at the foreign port of export or as it enters the US, before domestic costs are added, because the goal is to isolate the price set by foreign sellers and the exchange rate rather than domestic markups. The weighting by import value is included so that high-volume imports such as petroleum and electronics have more impact on the index than minor imports, reflecting their true importance to the US cost structure. A rising index means imported goods are getting more expensive, which can come from foreign price increases, a weaker dollar, or rising global commodity prices, while a falling index means imports are getting cheaper.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Import Price Index matters for sector investing because it directly measures the cost of imported inputs and goods, which determines the margins of US companies that depend on foreign sourcing and signals inflationary pressure entering the economy from abroad. When import prices are falling, US retailers and manufacturers that buy from abroad enjoy lower input costs and wider margins, which benefits Consumer Discretionary retailers and Consumer Staples companies that source globally. When import prices are rising, those same companies face margin compression unless they can pass the higher costs to customers, and the rising prices feed into domestic inflation that eventually forces a monetary response affecting all sectors. The index matters with particular force for import-dependent retailers because a large share of the goods on US store shelves are manufactured abroad, so import price changes flow directly into their cost of goods sold. Import prices also serve as an early signal of imported inflation, since a sustained rise in import prices tends to push consumer prices higher within a few months. The combination of direct margin impact on importers and an early read on inflation pressure makes the Import Price Index a useful input for distinguishing between sectors that benefit from cheap imports and sectors exposed to imported inflation.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Falling (negative year over year) | Import prices are declining, usually because the dollar has strengthened, global commodity prices have fallen, or foreign producers are cutting prices to compete. US retailers and manufacturers that source from abroad enjoy lower input costs and expanding margins, and the falling prices ease overall inflation pressure. Import-dependent consumer sectors benefit most while domestic commodity producers face competition from cheaper imports. | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Industrials | Energy, Materials | Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Real Estate, Information Technology, Communication Services |
| Stable (near flat) | Import prices are steady, which reflects balanced exchange rate and global price conditions. Import-dependent companies face neither a cost tailwind nor a headwind, and import-driven inflation pressure is neutral. Sector selection should rely on other factors when import prices are flat. | Consumer Discretionary, Information Technology, Industrials | Energy | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Materials, Real Estate, Communication Services |
| Rising moderately (1 to 4% year over year) | Import prices are rising at a moderate pace, usually because the dollar has weakened, global commodity prices have firmed, or foreign producers are raising prices. Import-dependent companies face gradually rising input costs that pressure margins unless passed through, and the rise adds modestly to domestic inflation. Domestic commodity producers gain some pricing room as imported alternatives get more expensive. | Energy, Materials | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples | Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Real Estate, Information Technology, Communication Services, Industrials |
| Rising sharply (above 4% year over year) | Import prices are climbing rapidly, usually from a sharply weaker dollar, a global commodity spike, or broad foreign inflation. Import-dependent retailers and manufacturers face significant margin compression, and the surge feeds quickly into domestic consumer inflation that forces a monetary response. Domestic commodity producers benefit as imports become expensive, but import-dependent consumer sectors are squeezed. | Energy, Materials | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Industrials, Information Technology | Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Real Estate, Communication Services |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer Discretionary | A large share of discretionary goods such as apparel, electronics, and furniture is manufactured abroad, so falling import prices lower the cost of goods sold and expand retailer margins, while rising import prices squeeze those margins. Retailers with thin margins are especially sensitive to import cost changes. The import-cost channel is a primary driver of retail profitability. | Buy when import prices are falling. Avoid when import prices are rising sharply. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples companies source ingredients, packaging, and finished products from abroad, so falling import prices reduce input costs and support margins, while rising import prices compress them. The sector can pass through some cost increases due to inelastic demand but not all. Import costs affect the margin on globally sourced products. | Buy when import prices are falling. Avoid when import prices rise faster than the sector can pass through. |
| Industrials | Manufacturers import components and raw materials, so falling import prices lower their input costs and support margins, while rising import prices raise costs. The effect depends on how much of the supply chain is foreign-sourced. Import costs are one input among several for industrial margins. | Buy when import prices are falling. Avoid when import prices rise sharply and squeeze manufacturing margins. |
| Energy | Domestic energy producers compete with imported energy, so rising import prices make foreign energy more expensive and give domestic producers pricing room, while falling import prices increase competition from cheaper imports. The relationship runs through the competitive position rather than input costs. The sector benefits from rising import prices. | Buy when import prices are rising on energy components. Avoid when falling import prices increase competition. |
| Materials | Domestic materials producers compete with imported materials, so rising import prices improve their competitive position and pricing power, while falling import prices increase competition from cheaper foreign supply. The sector benefits when imports become expensive. The competitive channel drives the linkage. | Buy when import prices are rising. Avoid when import prices are falling and increasing import competition. |
| Information Technology | Technology hardware relies heavily on imported components and finished devices, so rising import prices raise costs for hardware makers while falling prices lower them. Software is less affected because it has minimal physical import content. The hardware portion of the sector is import-sensitive. | Buy when import prices are falling for hardware makers. Avoid when import prices rise sharply on electronics. |
| Health Care | Health care is largely a domestic service business with limited import exposure, though some medical devices and pharmaceutical ingredients are imported. The sector is mostly insulated from import price swings. Import costs are a minor consideration for health care positioning. | Low sensitivity. Mostly insulated from import price trends. |
| Utilities | Utilities serve domestic customers using mostly domestic inputs, though imported fuel can matter for some generators. The sector is largely insulated from import prices except through fuel costs. Import prices are a secondary consideration. | Low sensitivity. Modest exposure through imported fuel only. |
| Financials | Banks have no direct import cost exposure since they sell financial services rather than physical goods. The sector is insulated from import price changes except through the broader inflation signal that import prices provide. Import prices do not directly affect financial earnings. | Not meaningfully affected by import prices directly. |
| Real Estate | Real estate is a domestic asset with no import cost exposure in its core operations, though construction materials can be imported. The sector is largely insulated except for the construction input channel. Import prices do not drive real estate positioning. | Low sensitivity. Modest exposure through imported construction materials. |
| Communication Services | The sector sells mostly domestic advertising and subscription services with minimal physical import content. It is largely insulated from import price changes. Import prices are not a meaningful driver. | Not meaningfully affected by import prices. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the Import Price Index | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Trade Weighted Index | The dollar's strength is one of the main drivers of import prices, since a stronger dollar makes foreign goods cheaper in dollar terms. Reading the exchange rate alongside import prices shows whether import price changes are being driven by the currency or by foreign price changes. This separates the currency effect from genuine foreign inflation. | When the dollar is strengthening AND import prices are falling, the currency is delivering the expected import cost relief and import-dependent retailers in Consumer Discretionary deserve overweight positions. When the dollar is strengthening but import prices are not falling, foreign producers are raising prices to offset the currency move, which removes the expected margin benefit and argues for caution on import-dependent names. |
| CPI Inflation | Import prices feed into consumer prices, so reading the two together shows whether imported inflation is contributing to overall consumer inflation or whether domestic factors dominate. The import price index often leads CPI for imported goods categories. This connects the imported cost signal to the broader inflation picture that drives policy. | When import prices are rising AND CPI is rising, imported inflation is contributing to the overall problem and the central bank response will pressure rate-sensitive sectors, favoring Energy and Materials over long-duration sectors. When import prices are falling but CPI is still rising, the inflation is domestically generated in services rather than imported goods, which points to different sector implications focused on domestic wage-driven inflation. |
| Oil (WTI Crude) | Petroleum is one of the largest single imported commodities, so oil prices are a major driver of the import price index. Reading them together shows how much of an import price move is simply energy versus broad-based import inflation. This isolates the energy contribution from other imported goods. | When import prices are rising AND oil prices are rising, much of the import inflation is energy-driven and Energy producers benefit while energy-consuming sectors face cost pressure. When import prices are rising but oil prices are stable, the import inflation is broad-based across manufactured goods, which signals a weaker dollar or foreign inflation affecting import-dependent retailers across Consumer Discretionary and Consumer Staples. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in import prices being driven by movements in the exchange rate (which makes foreign goods cheaper or more expensive in dollar terms), by changes in the prices foreign producers charge (genuine foreign inflation or deflation), or by global commodity price swings? Each driver carries a different signal, with currency-driven changes tending to reverse when the dollar moves back and foreign inflation tending to persist.

2. How much of the import price change is concentrated in petroleum and energy products versus spread across manufactured consumer and industrial goods? Energy-driven import price changes benefit domestic energy producers and hurt energy consumers, while broad-based manufactured-goods import inflation affects import-dependent retailers across many sectors.

3. Are foreign producers absorbing exchange rate changes in their own margins to keep their dollar prices stable, or are they passing the full currency move through to the prices US buyers pay? When foreign producers absorb currency moves, the expected import price relief from a strong dollar does not materialize, which changes the outlook for import-dependent sectors.

4. Are the import price changes concentrated in goods from one country or region, such as China, and what does that say about whether the change reflects that country's specific conditions or a broad global trend? Country-specific import price changes affect companies sourcing from that country, while broad changes affect all importers.

5. Is the change in import prices likely to be passed through to US consumer prices, or will US retailers and manufacturers absorb it in their margins because competition prevents pass-through? Whether the cost is passed through determines whether the margin hit lands on the importer (bad for the importing sector) or on the consumer (which feeds inflation).

6. Are import prices for capital goods and industrial supplies (which affect business costs) moving differently from import prices for consumer goods (which affect retail costs)? The distinction matters because rising industrial import prices hurt manufacturers while rising consumer import prices hurt retailers, pointing to different sector implications.

7. How large is the imported content in the cost structure of the sectors under consideration, and would a meaningful import price change actually move their margins? A retailer sourcing most goods abroad is highly exposed to import prices, while a domestic service business is not, which determines how much weight to place on the signal.

8. Is the import price trend consistent with the trade-weighted dollar's movement as the historical relationship would predict, or has the relationship broken down in a way that suggests foreign producers are changing their pricing behavior? A breakdown in the dollar-import-price link signals a change in foreign producer behavior that affects the durability of the trend.

9. Are tariffs or trade policy changes affecting the final cost of imports to US buyers in ways that the import price index does not capture, since the index measures prices before tariffs are applied? Because the index excludes tariffs, the actual landed cost to importers can rise even when the index is flat, which affects the true margin impact on import-dependent sectors.

10. Is the change in import prices an early signal of broader inflation pressure that will show up in consumer prices within a few months, or is it isolated to imported goods that make up a small share of overall consumption? When import price increases are broad and large, they tend to lead consumer inflation and warrant rotation toward inflation-benefiting sectors, while isolated increases do not.
