# Iron Ore Price

## Part 1: Definition

The Iron Ore Price measures the cost of iron ore, the primary raw material used to make steel, quoted in US dollars per metric ton for a standard grade delivered to China. The price updates continuously in futures markets and is reported daily. Because iron ore is the main input to steel and China is the dominant buyer, the price reflects the balance between global iron ore supply, which comes from a small number of large mining companies, and Chinese steel demand. The price is a direct driver of the revenue of iron ore miners and a read on the health of the steel-consuming economy.

The price is determined by the balance between iron ore supply and demand in the global market. Supply is reflected because iron ore comes from a small number of large mining companies concentrated in a few countries, so their output decisions and any disruptions strongly affect available supply. Demand is reflected because steel mills, overwhelmingly in China, buy iron ore to produce steel, so the pace of steel production drives demand. The standard grade delivered to China is included as the benchmark because China is the largest importer and consumer, making its delivered price the global reference. The price is included as an indicator because it sits at the intersection of concentrated global supply and China-dominated demand, so it reflects both the supply discipline of the major miners and the strength of Chinese steel demand. The price is interpreted as a gauge of the steel-input market, with a high price signaling strong demand or tight supply that benefits miners, and a low price signaling weak demand or abundant supply that pressures them.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Iron Ore Price matters for sector investing because it directly drives the revenue of iron ore mining companies and serves as a read on the health of the steel-consuming economy, which affects the materials sector and the broader industrial cycle. When the iron ore price is high, the large mining companies that produce it earn strong revenue, which benefits the Materials sector, and the high price usually signals strong steel demand from a healthy industrial and construction economy. When the price is low, miners face revenue pressure, and the low price usually signals weak steel demand from a slowing economy. The metric matters with particular force for the Materials sector because the major iron ore producers are among the largest mining companies, so the price flows directly into their profits. Because iron ore demand is dominated by Chinese steel production, the price also serves as a read on Chinese industrial activity that affects global commodity markets. The combination of a direct revenue link to major miners and a read on steel-consuming industrial demand makes the iron ore price a useful input for positioning the materials sector and reading global industrial conditions.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the iron ore price move being driven by changes in Chinese steel demand or by changes in supply from the major mining companies, given that demand-driven and supply-driven moves carry different signals? A demand-driven price rise signals a healthy steel-consuming economy, while a supply-driven rise reflects a production constraint that does not carry the same growth signal.

2. Are the major iron ore mining companies, which dominate global supply, increasing or restraining their output, since their supply decisions strongly affect the price independent of demand? Because supply is concentrated among a few large producers, their output discipline is a key driver of the price.

3. Are weather events or disruptions at major mining regions affecting iron ore supply, which can move the price for reasons unrelated to demand? Supply disruptions from cyclones or other events can spike prices temporarily without reflecting underlying demand strength.

4. Are Chinese port inventories of iron ore high or low, since high inventories can suppress new buying even when steel production is strong, while low inventories can amplify price rises? Port inventory levels affect how steel production translates into iron ore buying and price moves.

5. Are Chinese steel mill margins healthy enough to support buying iron ore at current prices, since squeezed mill margins reduce their willingness to pay up for ore? When mills face poor margins, they resist high ore prices, which can cap the price even when production is solid.

6. Is the iron ore price move consistent with what Chinese steel production and other industrial commodities are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests supply factors or inventory effects? Confirmation across the steel and commodity complex strengthens the signal, while a divergence points to supply or inventory effects.

7. Is the grade premium, the extra price for higher-quality iron ore, widening or narrowing, since it reflects steel mill preferences and environmental pressures that affect specific producers? The grade premium affects which iron ore producers benefit most and reflects shifts in steel mill behavior.

8. Is the long-term outlook for iron ore demand being affected by the maturing of the Chinese construction cycle or by the shift toward steel recycling, which reduces demand for newly mined ore? Structural shifts in steelmaking and Chinese construction affect the long-run demand outlook independent of the short-term cycle.

9. How concentrated is the exposure of the materials companies under consideration to iron ore specifically, since a diversified miner is less sensitive to iron ore than a pure iron ore producer? The company's iron ore exposure determines how much the price affects its earnings.

10. Is the change in the iron ore price large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect miner revenue and signal a shift in steel-consuming demand, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary supply or inventory factors? Only a sustained and meaningful price move warrants a sector rotation, since iron ore can swing on temporary supply and inventory factors.
