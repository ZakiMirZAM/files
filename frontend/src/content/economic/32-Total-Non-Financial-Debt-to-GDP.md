# Total Non-Financial Debt to GDP

## Part 1: Definition

Total Non-Financial Debt to GDP measures the combined debt of households, businesses, and the government, excluding financial institutions, scaled to the size of the economy. It is expressed as a percentage or ratio, where a reading of 250% means total debt is two and a half times the size of annual economic output. The data is compiled quarterly. The measure reveals how much debt the economy carries relative to its ability to generate the income needed to service that debt, which signals the economy's financial leverage and vulnerability.

The measure is constructed by adding together the debt of three borrowing sectors and dividing by GDP. Household debt is included because mortgages, auto loans, credit cards, and student loans represent the borrowing of consumers, whose ability to service that debt affects their spending capacity. Business debt, also called non-financial corporate debt, is included because companies borrow to fund operations and investment, and their debt burden affects their financial stability and investment capacity. Government debt is included because federal, state, and local borrowing represents obligations that must be serviced from tax revenue. Financial sector debt is excluded because much of it represents intermediation between other borrowers and lenders rather than genuine net borrowing, so including it would double-count. The total is divided by GDP because scaling to the economy's output reveals whether the debt is large relative to the income available to service it, which is what matters for sustainability. The measure is included as an indicator because high and rising debt relative to output signals financial vulnerability, since servicing the debt consumes a growing share of income and leaves the economy exposed to rising interest rates or falling income.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Total Non-Financial Debt to GDP matters for sector investing because it reveals the economy's overall financial leverage, which signals the vulnerability to interest rate increases and economic shocks and the stage of the long-term debt cycle that shapes the investment environment. When debt is high relative to output, the economy is financially fragile, meaning rising interest rates or falling income can trigger debt-servicing problems that spread through the economy, which favors defensive sectors and warns of potential financial stress. When debt is being used productively to fund investment that grows future income, it can support expansion, but when it merely funds consumption or speculation, it builds vulnerability without growing the capacity to repay. The metric matters because the level and trajectory of debt determine how sensitive the economy is to interest rates, which affects rate-sensitive sectors, and because the unwinding of excessive debt has historically driven severe and prolonged downturns. For sector investing this is a slow-moving structural indicator that informs the broader risk environment and the positioning between cyclical and defensive sectors over longer horizons rather than a fast trading signal.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the rising debt being driven by households, businesses, or the government, given that each sector's debt carries different risks and affects different parts of the economy? Household debt affects consumer spending capacity, business debt affects corporate stability, and government debt affects fiscal sustainability, so the source determines which risks are building.

2. Is the debt being used to fund productive investment that grows future income and the capacity to repay, or to fund consumption and speculation that builds vulnerability without growing repayment capacity? Productive debt is far less dangerous than consumption-driven debt, which affects how much risk the rising debt represents.

3. Is the economy's ability to service the debt being supported by low interest rates, and how vulnerable would the debt burden become if rates rose? When low rates are the main thing making high debt manageable, the economy is vulnerable to rate increases that could trigger debt-servicing stress.

4. Where is the economy in the long-term debt cycle, given that the buildup and unwinding of debt over many years has historically driven major economic swings beyond the normal business cycle? Identifying the debt cycle stage helps judge whether the economy is in a sustainable buildup or approaching a dangerous unwinding.

5. Is the debt denominated in the country's own currency, which gives more flexibility to manage it through monetary policy, or in foreign currency, which creates greater vulnerability? Domestic-currency debt can be managed through inflation and monetary policy, while foreign-currency debt creates harder constraints.

6. Is the rising debt concentrated in particular sectors or borrower types that could become points of failure, or spread broadly across the economy? Concentrated debt creates specific points of vulnerability, while broadly distributed debt spreads the risk.

7. Is the debt ratio rising because debt is growing or because output is shrinking, since a ratio that rises in a recession because output fell carries a different signal than one that rises because of a borrowing binge? The reason the ratio changes determines whether it reflects a borrowing problem or an output problem.

8. Are debt-servicing costs as a share of income rising or falling, given that the burden of the debt matters more than its absolute level? The debt-service burden reveals how much strain the debt is actually putting on borrowers, which matters more than the raw debt level.

9. Is the debt level consistent with what credit spreads and interest rates are signaling about financial stress, or is high debt being carried without apparent stress? When high debt coincides with calm credit markets, the vulnerability is latent, while stress in credit markets signals the vulnerability is materializing.

10. Is the change in the debt ratio large enough and sustained enough to signal a meaningful shift in the economy's financial leverage and vulnerability, or is it a gradual structural trend that does not call for immediate sector repositioning? Because debt levels move slowly, only a meaningful shift in the trajectory or the emergence of servicing stress warrants adjusting longer-term sector positioning.
