# US Crude Oil Inventory

## Part 1: Definition

US Crude Oil Inventory measures the amount of crude oil held in storage across the country, including commercial storage tanks and pipelines, expressed in millions of barrels. The data is released weekly, making it one of the most frequent reads on the oil market. The level of inventory reveals the balance between how much oil is being produced and imported versus how much is being consumed by refineries, since inventory builds when supply exceeds demand and draws down when demand exceeds supply. A separate government emergency stockpile is tracked apart from commercial inventory.

The inventory figure is constructed by measuring the crude oil held in storage at refineries, tank farms, and pipeline systems across the country. Commercial crude inventory is included because it represents the working supply of oil that refiners draw on to make gasoline, diesel, and other products, so its level reflects the real-time balance of the oil market. Inventory at the main delivery hub where the benchmark crude is priced is included with particular attention because that location sets the reference price and its inventory level directly affects pricing. The change in inventory from week to week is included as the key signal because a build indicates that production and imports are outpacing refinery demand, while a draw indicates that demand is outpacing supply. The emergency government stockpile is tracked separately because it is held for national security rather than commercial use, so including it would obscure the commercial supply-demand balance. The inventory level is interpreted as a gauge of whether the oil market is oversupplied or tight, which drives oil prices and the energy sector.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

US Crude Oil Inventory matters for sector investing because it is a frequent and direct read on the supply-demand balance in the oil market, which drives oil prices and therefore the earnings of the energy sector and the costs of energy-consuming sectors. When inventories are drawing down, demand is outpacing supply, which signals a tightening oil market that supports higher oil prices and benefits energy producers while raising costs for fuel-consuming sectors. When inventories are building, supply is outpacing demand, which signals an oversupplied market that pressures oil prices, hurting energy producers but providing cost relief to fuel consumers. The metric matters with particular force for the Energy sector because oil producer revenue depends on the oil price that inventory levels help drive. Inventory data also confirms whether oil price moves are backed by genuine supply-demand changes or are driven by speculation, which makes it a useful check on the oil price signal. The combination of a direct link to energy producer revenue, a read on fuel costs for consuming sectors, and a confirmation of oil price moves makes inventory a useful input for positioning energy and energy-sensitive sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the inventory change being driven by changes in crude oil demand from refineries, by changes in domestic production, or by changes in imports and exports, given that each reflects a different part of the supply-demand balance? Identifying the driver reveals whether the inventory move signals demand strength, production changes, or trade flows.

2. Is the inventory change consistent with normal seasonal patterns, since oil inventories follow predictable seasonal swings with refinery maintenance and demand cycles, or is it deviating from the seasonal norm in a meaningful way? A change that simply follows the seasonal pattern carries less signal than one that deviates from it.

3. Is the inventory at the main delivery hub where the benchmark crude is priced building or drawing, since inventory at that specific location directly affects the benchmark price beyond the national total? Because the delivery hub sets the reference price, its inventory level can move prices independent of the national picture.

4. Is the inventory draw being driven by strong demand from a healthy economy, or by supply constraints such as production cuts or disruptions that reduce available oil? A demand-driven draw signals economic strength, while a supply-driven draw reflects a production problem that carries a different signal.

5. Are refineries running at high or low rates, since refinery activity determines how much crude is being consumed and converted into products, which drives the inventory balance? Refinery utilization is a key driver of crude demand, so its level helps explain inventory changes.

6. Is the government emergency stockpile being drawn down or refilled, since changes to that stockpile affect the overall oil supply picture even though it is tracked separately from commercial inventory? Releases from or additions to the emergency stockpile can affect prices and the supply picture beyond commercial inventory.

7. Is the inventory change consistent with what the oil price is doing, or is there a divergence that suggests the price is being driven by speculation or geopolitics rather than the physical supply balance? When inventory and price align, the signal is reliable, while a divergence suggests non-fundamental factors are driving the price.

8. Are oil exports rising or falling, since growing US oil exports draw down domestic inventory and connect the domestic balance to global demand? Because the US now exports significant oil, export flows affect domestic inventory and tie it to global conditions.

9. Is the inventory trend confirmed by inventory data for refined products such as gasoline and diesel, which reflect end-demand for fuel? Product inventories provide a check on end-demand that complements the crude inventory signal.

10. Is the inventory change large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect oil prices and the energy sector, or is it a single weekly fluctuation that could reverse, given that weekly inventory data is volatile? Only a sustained and meaningful inventory trend warrants a sector rotation, since weekly figures can swing for temporary reasons.
