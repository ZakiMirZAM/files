# US Dollar Index

## Part 1: Definition

The US Dollar Index measures the value of the US dollar against a basket of major foreign currencies, focused on a small set of the most heavily traded developed-market currencies rather than all trading partners. It is expressed as an index level relative to a base period, and the most useful figure for analysis is the percentage change over a chosen period. The index updates continuously through the trading day with daily values reported. It differs from the broader trade-weighted dollar measure by covering fewer currencies, with the largest weight on the euro.

The index is constructed by combining the dollar's exchange rate against six major currencies, with each weighted by its importance in the basket. The euro is included with the heaviest weight because the euro area is one of the largest economies and trading blocs, so the dollar's value against the euro dominates the index. The Japanese yen, British pound, Canadian dollar, Swedish krona, and Swiss franc are included because they represent other major developed economies with heavily traded currencies and deep financial markets. These currencies are chosen because they are the most liquid and most widely held in global finance, making the index a clean read on the dollar's standing among the world's major reserve and trading currencies. The index excludes the currencies of emerging markets and many smaller trading partners, which means it captures the dollar's strength against the developed world rather than against the full range of countries the US trades with. A rising index means the dollar is strengthening against these major currencies, and a falling index means it is weakening.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The US Dollar Index matters for sector investing because the dollar's value against major currencies drives the translated earnings of US multinationals and the dollar price of globally traded commodities, creating clear sector winners and losers. When the dollar strengthens against major currencies, US companies with large sales in Europe and Japan see that revenue translate into fewer dollars, which pressures the earnings of multinationals concentrated in Information Technology, Industrials, and Materials. A strong dollar also lowers the dollar price of commodities, which hurts Energy and Materials producers. When the dollar weakens, the reverse occurs and multinationals and commodity producers benefit. The index matters with particular force for commodity sectors because most globally traded commodities are priced in dollars, so a weaker dollar mechanically raises their prices and producer revenue. The index also reflects the relative attractiveness of US assets, which affects capital flows into US markets. The combination of the earnings-translation effect on multinationals and the pricing effect on commodities makes the US Dollar Index a key input for choosing between domestically focused and globally exposed sectors.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rising sharply (dollar strengthening rapidly against majors) | The dollar is strengthening quickly against major currencies, usually because US interest rates are rising relative to Europe and Japan, US growth is outpacing other developed economies, or global stress is driving investors into dollar safety. A strong dollar lowers import costs, helping domestic retailers, but it compresses the translated earnings of multinationals and lowers dollar-priced commodity prices. Globally exposed and commodity sectors face headwinds while domestic-focused sectors benefit. | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Financials, Real Estate | Information Technology, Industrials, Materials, Energy, Communication Services | Health Care, Utilities |

Domestic-focused sectors enjoy a modest input-cost benefit, while multinationals face a manageable earnings headwind. The effect is present but not strong enough to dominate sector selection alone. | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Financials | Materials, Energy, Information Technology | Health Care, Utilities, Real Estate, Industrials, Communication Services |
| Stable (trading in a narrow range) | The dollar is neither gaining nor losing meaningful ground against major currencies, reflecting balanced relative growth and interest rate conditions. Neither multinationals nor domestic-focused companies face a strong currency tailwind or headwind. A weak dollar raises the translated value of multinationals' foreign earnings and lifts dollar-priced commodity prices, benefiting globally exposed and commodity sectors. Domestic retailers face higher import costs. | Materials, Energy, Information Technology, Industrials | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Real Estate | Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Communication Services |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | Most globally traded materials are priced in dollars, so a weaker dollar mechanically raises their prices and producer revenue, while a stronger dollar lowers them. Materials companies also earn foreign revenue that translates more favorably when the dollar is weak. This dual exposure makes Materials highly dollar-sensitive. | Buy when the dollar is falling. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Energy | Oil and gas are priced in dollars globally, so a weaker dollar raises their dollar price and supports producer revenue, while a stronger dollar reduces foreign demand for dollar-priced energy. Energy companies also have large international operations affected by translation. The sector is closely tied to the dollar through both channels. | Buy when the dollar is falling. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Information Technology | Large technology companies earn a substantial share of revenue in Europe and Japan, so a stronger dollar reduces the dollar value of that revenue and compresses earnings, while a weaker dollar amplifies it. Because tech valuations respond to earnings momentum, currency swings move the sector. The translation effect is the primary dollar linkage. | Buy when the dollar is falling or stable. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Industrials | Industrial companies export equipment and earn significant foreign revenue, so a stronger dollar makes US exports more expensive abroad and reduces translated earnings, while a weaker dollar improves competitiveness and translation. The sector's global sales make it directly exposed. The competitiveness and translation effects compound. | Buy when the dollar is falling or stable. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Domestic-focused retailers benefit from a strong dollar because it lowers the cost of imported goods they sell, expanding margins, while a weak dollar raises import costs. The sector is one of the few that prefers a stronger dollar. The import-cost channel is the dominant linkage for domestic retail. | Buy when the dollar is rising. Avoid when the dollar is falling sharply and raising import costs. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples that source from abroad benefit from a strong dollar through lower input costs, though large multinational staples face an offsetting translation headwind on foreign sales. The net effect depends on the company's mix of domestic sourcing versus foreign sales. Domestically focused staples lean toward benefiting from a strong dollar. | Mixed signal. Domestic-sourcing staples buy on dollar strength, multinational staples prefer weakness. |
| Financials | Most banks earn primarily domestic revenue, so they are relatively insulated from translation, but a strong dollar that reflects strong US fundamentals often coincides with conditions favorable to domestic lending. The linkage is indirect through the macro conditions driving the dollar. The sector is less dollar-sensitive than multinationals. | Mostly indirect. Modestly favored when a strong dollar reflects strong US growth. |
| Real Estate | US real estate is a domestic asset, so REIT earnings are largely insulated from translation, but a strong dollar can attract foreign capital into US property as a safe asset. The linkage is indirect through capital flows. The sector has low direct sensitivity. | Mostly indirect. Modestly favored when a strong dollar attracts foreign property investment. |
| Communication Services | The global digital platforms in this sector earn meaningful foreign revenue, so a stronger dollar compresses their translated earnings while a weaker dollar amplifies them. The domestic telecom portion is insulated. The sector has moderate exposure through its global platforms. | Buy when the dollar is falling. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Health Care | Large pharmaceutical companies earn substantial foreign revenue subject to translation, but domestic health services are insulated, so the net sector sensitivity is moderate. The defensive nature usually dominates currency effects. The dollar is a secondary consideration. | Low sensitivity. Pharma leans toward preferring dollar weakness, services are insulated. |
| Utilities | Utilities are almost entirely domestic businesses serving local customers, so their revenue has essentially no currency translation exposure. The sector is the closest to genuinely unaffected by the dollar. Currency movements should not drive utility positioning. | Not meaningfully affected by the dollar. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the US Dollar Index | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Trade Weighted Index | The trade-weighted index covers a broader set of trading-partner currencies while the dollar index focuses on major developed currencies, so reading them together reveals whether dollar strength is broad-based or concentrated against the majors. This distinction affects which multinationals are most exposed. This separates broad dollar strength from major-currency-specific strength. | When both indexes are rising together, dollar strength is broad and all multinationals and commodity producers face headwinds, which favors domestic-focused sectors across the board. When the dollar index is rising but the trade-weighted index is flatter, the dollar is strong only against major currencies and companies with emerging-market exposure may be less affected, allowing more selective positioning within global sectors. |
| 10-year, 2-year, 3-month rate | Interest rate differentials between the US and other developed economies are a primary driver of the dollar, so reading the dollar alongside US rates confirms whether the currency move is rate-driven. Rate-driven dollar moves tend to persist while sentiment-driven moves can reverse. This connects the currency to its main fundamental driver. | When the dollar is rising AND US rates are rising relative to abroad, the dollar strength is fundamentally supported and likely to persist, which sustains the headwind on multinationals and commodities and favors domestic sectors. When the dollar is rising but US rates are stable, the move may be driven by safe-haven flows that could reverse, which argues for caution before fully committing to domestic-focused positioning. |
| Oil (WTI Crude) | Oil is priced in dollars, so the dollar's strength directly affects the dollar oil price, and reading them together separates a genuine oil supply-demand move from a currency effect. A weaker dollar lifts oil prices independent of oil fundamentals. This isolates the currency effect on oil. | When the dollar is falling AND oil is rising, part of the oil strength is currency-driven, which supports Energy producers but means the oil rise may not reflect genuine demand tightness. When the dollar is rising AND oil is still rising, the oil strength is genuine demand or supply tightness overcoming a currency headwind, which is a stronger signal for the Energy sector. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the dollar's movement against major currencies being driven by the difference between US interest rates and rates in Europe and Japan, by the difference in economic growth between the US and other developed economies, or by global investors seeking dollar safety during stress? Each driver carries different durability, with rate and growth differentials tending to persist while safe-haven moves can reverse when stress fades.

2. Is the dollar's strength or weakness concentrated against one major currency such as the euro, which dominates the index, or spread evenly across all the major currencies in the basket? Because the euro carries the heaviest weight, a move driven mainly by the euro may not reflect broad dollar strength against the rest of the world.

3. Is the dollar index move consistent with the broader trade-weighted dollar that includes emerging markets, or is the dollar strong only against developed-market currencies? A divergence between the two measures tells you whether companies with emerging-market exposure face the same currency pressure as those focused on developed markets.

4. How much of the revenue of the multinational sectors under consideration comes from the specific regions whose currencies are in this index, namely Europe and Japan, versus emerging markets not captured here? A company with heavy European exposure is directly affected by this index, while one focused on emerging markets is better gauged by the broader measure.

5. Is the dollar move large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect the translated earnings of multinationals, or is it a short-term fluctuation that companies hedge through and that will not show up in reported results? Currency moves must be both large and durable to warrant a sector tilt, since companies routinely hedge short-term swings.

6. Are the central banks of the major economies in the basket moving their interest rates in a direction that will widen or narrow the gap with US rates, which would extend or reverse the dollar move? The relative path of central bank policy across major economies is the key forward-looking driver of the dollar.

7. Is the dollar's level consistent with the difference in real interest rates between the US and the other major economies, which is the most fundamental long-run driver of exchange rates? A dollar move in line with real rate differentials is on sound footing, while one that diverges may be driven by sentiment that can reverse.

8. For commodity sectors, is the dollar move actually moving the dollar price of the commodity as the historical inverse relationship would predict, or are commodity-specific supply and demand factors overwhelming the currency effect? The dollar-commodity link is usually strong but can be overridden by commodity-specific shocks.

9. Are global capital flows moving into or out of US assets in a way that is driving the dollar, and what does that say about the relative attractiveness of US markets versus foreign markets? Capital flows into US stocks and bonds support the dollar and reflect investor preference for US assets, which has implications beyond the currency itself.

10. Does the current level of the dollar look expensive or cheap relative to its long-run average and relative to estimates of fair value based on relative prices across countries, and what does that say about its likely future direction? A dollar far above its long-run fair value is more likely to weaken eventually, which would reverse the current sector implications.
