# Henry Hub Natural Gas

## Part 1: Definition

Henry Hub Natural Gas is the price of natural gas at the Henry Hub delivery point in Louisiana, quoted in US dollars per million British thermal units (MMBtu), where a British thermal unit is a standard measure of the heat energy contained in the gas. The figure is reported as a monthly average price, and it serves as the benchmark reference price for natural gas across the United States. Most US natural gas contracts are priced at a spread to the Henry Hub price, so this single number anchors the cost of gas for power plants, factories, and households throughout the country. Natural gas is used to generate electricity, to heat homes and buildings, and as a raw material in the production of chemicals and fertilizer.

The price is determined by the balance of supply and demand for the fuel. Supply factors are reflected because US gas production, mostly from shale drilling, and the amount of gas held in underground storage determine how much fuel is available. Demand factors are reflected because gas consumption rises and falls with the weather, since cold winters increase heating demand and hot summers increase electricity demand for air conditioning, and because industrial activity and power generation set a steady baseline of demand. Storage levels are reflected because gas is injected into storage in mild months and withdrawn in peak months, so the amount in storage relative to seasonal norms signals whether the market is comfortable or tight. Export demand is reflected because the United States now ships large volumes of gas abroad as liquefied natural gas, which links the domestic price to global energy markets and can tighten domestic supply when export demand is strong. The price is included as an economic indicator because natural gas is both a major revenue source for energy producers and a key cost input for utilities, chemical makers, and fertilizer producers, so its level affects electricity bills, industrial costs, and the profitability of the energy sector.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Henry Hub Natural Gas matters for sector investing because it is simultaneously a direct revenue driver for gas producers and a major cost input for power generation and heavy industry, which creates clear winners and losers when the price moves. When gas prices rise, gas producers in the Energy sector capture the higher prices directly as revenue, while utilities that burn gas to generate electricity and chemical makers that use gas as a raw material face rising costs. When gas prices fall, producers lose revenue but power generators and gas-consuming manufacturers gain margin relief. The metric matters with particular force for Utilities, because gas is a primary fuel for electricity generation, though many regulated utilities can pass fuel costs through to customers with a lag. It also matters for Materials, because natural gas is a feedstock and energy source for chemical and fertilizer production, so the gas price sits directly inside those companies' cost of goods. The combination of a direct revenue link to gas producers and a cost link to utilities and chemical makers makes natural gas a central input for choosing between energy producers and energy-consuming sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the natural-gas price move being driven by a change in demand (weather, power generation, or industrial activity) or by a change in supply (production levels, storage, or export volumes)? Demand-driven moves from weather can reverse quickly when the season changes, while supply-driven moves from low production or strong export demand tend to last longer and act as a more durable cost shock for gas-consuming sectors.

2. Where do natural-gas storage levels sit relative to the seasonal average for this time of year, and does the storage trend point toward a comfortable cushion or a tight market? Storage is the key physical-market gauge for gas, and low storage going into a peak season leaves the price vulnerable to a sharp spike.

3. What does the weather outlook point toward for the coming weeks, since cold winters raise heating demand and hot summers raise cooling demand? Because gas demand is highly seasonal and weather-driven, the near-term weather forecast is one of the most important inputs into the direction of the price.

4. How strong is liquefied-natural-gas export demand, and is it draining domestic supply in a way that keeps the US price elevated even when domestic demand is soft? Export demand links the domestic gas price to global energy markets, and strong exports can tighten the home market and support the price independent of US weather.

5. Is US gas production rising or falling in response to the current price, and is drilling activity adding supply that would cap the price or holding back in a way that would let it run higher? US shale production responds to price over time, so the supply response determines how sustainable a price move is.

6. For the gas-burning utilities exposed to the price, can they pass higher fuel costs through to customers, and how long is the lag between when the cost rises and when the rate recovery takes effect? The pass-through mechanism and its lag determine whether rising gas compresses utility cash flow temporarily or flows through to customers, which is central to utility earnings.

7. How large a share of the cost of goods does natural gas represent for the chemical and fertilizer makers in Materials, and can those companies pass higher feedstock costs on to their customers? Because gas is a feedstock for chemicals and fertilizer, the size of that cost share and the ability to pass it on determine how hard a gas spike hits those margins.

8. Are utilities and large gas consumers hedged against the current price move or holding storage positions that smooth short-term spikes, or are they fully exposed to the spot price? Hedging and storage can blunt the earnings impact of a price spike, so the hedge position determines how much a move actually flows through to results.

9. Is the gas price move part of a broad energy rally that includes oil, or is it specific to the gas market from weather and storage factors? A gas-specific move favors a narrower set of producers and pressures gas consumers, while a broad energy move shifts the call toward the whole Energy sector and away from all energy consumers.

10. Is the natural-gas price move large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully change the revenue of gas producers and the cost structure of utilities and chemical makers, or is it a short-term seasonal swing that companies can manage through? Only sustained and meaningful moves warrant a sector rotation, since seasonal swings are routine and companies plan around them with hedges and storage.
