# Wages vs CPI

## Part 1: Definition

Wages vs CPI measures whether worker pay is growing faster or slower than the prices workers pay for goods and services. It is calculated by taking the year-over-year growth in average hourly earnings and subtracting the year-over-year change in the Consumer Price Index, with the result expressed in percentage points. A positive number means wages are rising faster than prices, so workers can buy more than before. A negative number means prices are rising faster than wages, so workers can buy less. The underlying wage and price data are both released monthly, so this comparison updates every month.

The measure is constructed by combining two separate monthly readings. The wage side comes from average hourly earnings for workers across private industries, which is included because it captures the actual pay that households receive and convert into spending, making it the most direct gauge of household purchasing power on the income side. The price side comes from the Consumer Price Index, which is included because it measures the cost of the basket of goods and services households actually buy, making it the correct yardstick for what those wages can purchase. Subtracting price growth from wage growth produces real wage growth, which is included as the key figure because the level of wages alone does not tell you whether households are getting ahead or falling behind once the rising cost of living is accounted for. The comparison matters because a worker earning a 5% raise is worse off if prices rose 6%, and only by netting the two can the true change in living standards and spending power be measured.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Wages vs CPI matters for sector investing because it measures the real purchasing power of households, which determines how much consumers can spend on goods and services beyond the basic necessities, and that spending power directly drives the revenue of consumer-facing sectors. When real wages are positive and rising, households have surplus income that flows into discretionary purchases such as travel, dining, vehicles, and electronics, which lifts Consumer Discretionary and Communication Services. When real wages are negative, households are forced to cut back to essentials and trade down to cheaper options, which shifts spending toward Consumer Staples and discount retailers while pressuring discretionary sectors. The measure matters with particular force for Consumer Discretionary because that sector sells the postponable, income-elastic goods that households buy only when they have money left after covering necessities. Real wage trends also feed back into inflation, since rising wages that outpace productivity can push prices higher and force a policy response. The combination of a direct link to discretionary spending power and a signal about inflation pressure makes Wages vs CPI a focused input for deciding between discretionary and defensive consumer sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the wage growth in this comparison being driven by genuine productivity gains (where workers produce more and can be paid more without raising prices) or by labor market tightness alone (where employers bid up pay to compete for scarce workers, which can feed into inflation)? Productivity-driven wage growth supports a healthy environment for Consumer Discretionary, while tightness-driven wage growth may force the price increases that erode the real wage gain.

2. Are real wage gains spread broadly across income levels, or are they concentrated among higher earners while lower-income workers fall behind on purchasing power? Because lower-income households spend a larger share of income on necessities and discretionary trade-down behavior, the distribution of real wage gains affects whether Consumer Staples or Consumer Discretionary benefits more.

3. Is the price growth used in this comparison being driven mostly by necessities such as food, energy, and housing (which hit household budgets hardest and force the deepest cutbacks) or by discretionary categories that households can simply avoid? When necessity inflation drives the squeeze, the damage to discretionary spending is more severe because households cannot escape the rising essential costs.

4. Does the wage measure account for the full cost of employment including benefits such as health insurance and retirement contributions, or only cash pay, and would including benefits change the picture of real compensation growth? Cash wages alone can understate or overstate the true change in worker compensation, which affects the read on household financial health.

5. Is the real wage trend being distorted by changes in the mix of jobs, such as a shift toward higher-paying or lower-paying industries, rather than reflecting genuine raises for existing workers? Composition shifts can make average wages look stronger or weaker than the actual experience of individual workers, which affects the reliability of the purchasing power signal.

6. Are households drawing on savings or credit to maintain spending when real wages are negative, and how long can that continue before they are forced to cut back? When households bridge a real wage squeeze with savings or borrowing, discretionary spending can hold up temporarily, but this cannot last, which affects the timing of the rotation toward defensives.

7. Is the gap between wage growth and price growth widening or narrowing in the most recent months, and what does the direction of change say about where real purchasing power is heading? The trend matters more than the level, since improving real wages signal recovering discretionary demand while deteriorating real wages signal a coming squeeze.

8. Are wage gains likely to be sustained based on the current tightness of the labor market, or are they likely to fade as hiring slows and workers lose bargaining power? The durability of wage growth determines whether the support for discretionary spending will persist or reverse.

9. How does the current real wage trend compare to past cycles at the same stage, and is the relationship between real wages and consumer spending behaving the way it historically has? Checking the historical pattern guards against assuming a relationship holds when consumer behavior may have shifted.

10. Is the real wage squeeze, if present, severe enough to force genuine trade-down behavior where households switch from premium to value brands, or merely enough to slow the growth of discretionary spending? The severity determines whether Consumer Staples and discount retailers will see an actual share gain or whether the effect is limited to slower discretionary growth.
