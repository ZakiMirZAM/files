# Residential Housing Investment

## Part 1: Definition

Residential Housing Investment measures the inflation-adjusted dollar value of spending on building new homes, improving existing homes, and the fees associated with buying and selling homes during a period. It is reported as a component of GDP in inflation-adjusted dollars and is most usefully tracked as a percentage change from the prior period or prior year. The data is released quarterly with the GDP report, and related monthly data on housing starts, building permits, and home sales provides more frequent signals about the same activity.

Residential Housing Investment is constructed by adding together the main categories of housing-related spending that count as investment rather than consumption. New single-family and multifamily construction is included because building a new home creates a long-lived asset that produces housing services for decades, which is the definition of investment rather than current consumption. Improvements and renovations to existing homes are included because they extend the life and value of the housing stock, adding to the nation's productive assets. Brokerage commissions and other transaction costs on home sales are included because they represent the value of services rendered in transferring housing assets. Each category is included because it represents spending that creates or maintains the housing stock, and the total is adjusted for inflation so the figure reflects real building activity rather than just rising construction prices. The metric is counted as part of business investment within GDP rather than as consumer spending because a house is treated as a capital asset that delivers housing services over time, which is why it sits alongside business equipment and structures in the national accounts.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Residential Housing Investment matters for sector investing because housing is one of the most interest-rate-sensitive and forward-looking parts of the economy, so it often turns up at the start of a recovery and turns down ahead of a slowdown, giving an early read on the cycle. When housing investment is rising, it directly drives revenue for homebuilders within Consumer Discretionary, building products and materials companies within Materials, home improvement retailers within Consumer Discretionary, and mortgage lenders within Financials. When housing investment is falling, those same sectors face shrinking demand and the decline often warns of a broader slowdown because housing weakness historically leads the overall economy. The metric matters with particular force for homebuilders and building products companies because their entire revenue base depends on the pace of construction, so changes in housing investment flow straight to their earnings. Housing investment is also highly sensitive to mortgage rates, which makes it a transmission channel for monetary policy into the real economy. The combination of early-cycle signaling, direct revenue linkage to housing-related companies, and interest-rate sensitivity makes Residential Housing Investment a valuable input for timing the rotation into and out of housing-exposed sectors.

## Part 3: What it tells you

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rising strongly (above 5% year over year) | Housing investment is growing strongly, usually because mortgage rates are low or falling, household formation is robust, and confidence supports big purchases. | Rising moderately (1 to 5% year over year) | Housing investment is growing at a steady pace, reflecting a normal housing market with balanced demand and supply. | Flat (near zero growth) | Housing investment is stagnant, often because mortgage rates have risen enough to slow buying or because home prices have outrun affordability. Investors begin to question the durability of the cycle. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Consumer Discretionary, Materials, Financials, Real Estate | Energy, Information Technology, Communication Services, Industrials |

| Real Estate | Residential REITs and real estate services benefit from a healthy housing market that supports property values, transaction volumes, and rental demand. It is insulated from the housing channel. Housing investment does not drive positioning. | Not meaningfully affected by housing investment. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

|---|---|---|
| 30 year mortgage rate trend | Mortgage rates are the single most important driver of housing affordability and therefore of housing investment, so reading them together shows whether the housing trend is being driven by financing conditions. The mortgage rate often leads housing investment because buyers respond to rate changes before construction adjusts. This connects the cost of financing to the housing outcome. | When mortgage rates are falling AND housing investment is rising, the rate-driven recovery is confirmed and homebuilders in Consumer Discretionary along with Materials deserve overweight positions. When mortgage rates are rising but housing investment has not yet fallen, the decline is likely coming as higher rates work through the market, which argues for trimming housing-exposed sectors ahead of the turn. |
| GDP Growth | Residential housing investment is a component of GDP and historically leads the overall economy, so reading the two together shows whether housing is confirming or leading the broader growth trend. Housing weakness often precedes GDP weakness, giving an early signal. This positions housing as a leading indicator for the cycle. | When housing investment is rising AND GDP growth is accelerating, the early-cycle recovery is broad and housing-exposed sectors plus cyclicals deserve full weight. When housing investment is falling but GDP growth is still positive, housing is warning of a coming slowdown before GDP confirms it, which gives the investor a window to rotate from housing sectors into defensives. |
| Employment | Household formation and the ability to qualify for mortgages both depend on employment, so reading housing investment alongside employment shows whether the housing trend has a solid income foundation. ## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in housing investment being driven by new construction (which directly creates demand for builders and materials) or by renovation and improvement spending (which benefits home improvement retailers more than builders)? The two categories benefit different companies within the housing complex, so identifying which is driving the trend sharpens the sector implications.

2. Is the housing demand coming from first-time buyers forming new households (a sustainable demographic-driven source) or from investors and speculators buying for price appreciation (a more fragile and reversible source)? Demographic-driven demand is durable, while speculative demand can reverse quickly and lead to a sharp downturn that hurts housing-exposed sectors.

3. How affordable is housing relative to household income at current home prices and mortgage rates, and is affordability improving or deteriorating? When affordability deteriorates badly, housing investment cannot be sustained regardless of other factors, which limits the upside for housing-exposed sectors.

4. Are existing homeowners locked into low mortgage rates that discourage them from selling and moving, which restricts the supply of existing homes for sale and shifts demand toward new construction? This lock-in effect can support new homebuilders even when overall housing activity is constrained, which has specific implications for which housing sectors benefit.

5. Is mortgage credit flowing freely to qualified buyers, or have lenders tightened their standards in a way that restricts housing demand independent of the level of mortgage rates? Tight credit can choke off housing investment even when rates are reasonable, which affects the outlook for both housing builders and mortgage lenders.

6. Is the inventory of unsold new and existing homes high or low relative to the pace of sales, and what does this say about whether builders will increase or decrease construction in the coming quarters? High inventory leads builders to cut construction, which hurts the sector, while low inventory encourages building.

7. Is housing construction concentrated in single-family homes (tied to ownership and household formation) or in multifamily rental buildings (tied to rental demand and investor activity)? The two segments respond to different forces and benefit different companies, so the construction mix affects the sector implications.

8. Are construction labor and building materials available and affordable, or are shortages and high input costs constraining the pace of building regardless of demand? Supply constraints can limit housing investment even when demand is strong, which affects both the volume of building and the margins of builders and materials companies.

9. Is the housing investment trend consistent with the level and direction of mortgage rates as the historical relationship would predict, or has the relationship shifted because of changes in household balance sheets or housing supply? A breakdown in the rate-housing relationship signals a structural change that affects how to interpret the current trend.

10. Is housing investment leading the broader economic cycle the way it historically has, turning up before recoveries and down before slowdowns, and where does the current housing trend place the economy in that leading-indicator framework? Because housing has historically been one of the earliest cycle indicators, its current direction provides an early read on where the overall economy and cyclical sectors are heading.
