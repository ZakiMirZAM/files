# Commercial and Industrial Loans YoY Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Commercial and Industrial Loans growth measures the year-over-year percentage change in the total amount of loans that banks have made to businesses for operating and investment purposes. These are loans to companies rather than to households or for real estate, used to fund inventory, equipment, payroll, expansion, and general operations. The data is released weekly and monthly based on bank reporting. The growth rate reveals whether businesses are borrowing more or less, which signals their confidence and investment plans, and whether banks are willing to lend.

The figure is constructed by totaling the commercial and industrial loans on bank balance sheets and comparing the total to the level one year earlier. Loans for business operations are included because they fund the day-to-day needs of companies such as inventory and payroll, reflecting current business activity. Loans for business investment are included because they fund expansion, equipment purchases, and capacity additions, reflecting business confidence in future growth. Loans to companies of all sizes are included because both large corporations and small businesses borrow to operate and grow, and the total captures the full picture of business credit demand. Loans for real estate and to households are excluded because they reflect different drivers than business activity. The growth rate is included as an indicator because business borrowing reflects two forces at once: the willingness of businesses to take on debt to fund activity and investment, which signals their confidence, and the willingness of banks to extend credit, which signals lending conditions. Rising loan growth indicates expanding business activity and available credit, while falling loan growth indicates business caution or tightening credit.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Commercial and Industrial Loans growth matters for sector investing because it reveals the demand for business credit and the willingness of banks to lend, which together signal business confidence, investment plans, and the health of the credit-driven economy. When loan growth is strong, businesses are confident enough to borrow for expansion and investment, and banks are willing to lend, which signals a healthy economy that supports cyclical sectors and especially Financials that earn on the lending. When loan growth slows or contracts, businesses are pulling back on investment or banks are tightening credit, which signals caution that often precedes a broader slowdown. The metric matters with particular force for Financials because business lending is a core source of bank revenue, so loan growth directly drives bank earnings. It also signals the business investment that drives Industrials and the broader capital goods economy. The combination of a read on business confidence, a direct link to bank lending revenue, and a signal about business investment makes loan growth a useful input for positioning Financials and cyclical sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in loan growth being driven by business demand for credit (reflecting their confidence and investment plans) or by banks changing their willingness to lend (reflecting credit conditions)? A demand-driven change signals shifting business confidence, while a supply-driven change signals shifting bank willingness, and the two carry different implications.

2. Are businesses borrowing to fund productive investment such as equipment and expansion, or to fund defensive purposes such as covering operating shortfalls or building precautionary cash? Productive borrowing signals confidence and supports cyclical sectors, while defensive borrowing signals stress.

3. Is the loan growth concentrated among large corporations or small businesses, and what does the distribution say about the breadth of business confidence across the economy? Broad-based borrowing signals widespread confidence, while borrowing concentrated among large firms may not reflect the health of the broader business economy.

4. Are banks tightening or loosening their lending standards, and how does that change in willingness to lend affect the loan growth independent of business demand? When banks tighten standards, loan growth can fall even when businesses want to borrow, which signals deteriorating credit conditions.

5. Is the loan growth being affected by businesses drawing down existing credit lines defensively during stress, which would inflate loan growth for a negative reason rather than a positive one? Defensive drawdowns can make loan growth look strong even as businesses are bracing for trouble, which must be distinguished from genuine expansion borrowing.

6. Is business borrowing shifting away from banks toward non-bank lenders such as private credit funds, which would make bank loan growth understate the true level of business credit? When credit shifts to non-bank lenders, bank loan growth alone misses part of the picture.

7. Is the loan growth consistent with what credit spreads and other credit indicators are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure may be misleading? Confirmation across credit indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence warrants caution.

8. Are interest rates affecting business borrowing decisions, since higher rates raise the cost of loans and can discourage borrowing even when businesses have investment opportunities? Rising rates can suppress loan growth for reasons unrelated to business confidence, which affects interpretation.

9. Is the loan growth leading or following the broader business cycle, and where does the current trend place the economy in that framework? Business borrowing often moves with the cycle, so its direction helps confirm where the economy stands and how to position cyclical sectors.

10. Is the change in loan growth large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in business confidence and credit conditions, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary factors? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants a sector rotation, since loan growth can fluctuate for temporary reasons.
