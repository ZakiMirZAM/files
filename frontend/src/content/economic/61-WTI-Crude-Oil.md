# WTI Crude Oil

## Part 1: Definition

WTI Crude Oil is the price of West Texas Intermediate, the benchmark grade of US crude oil, quoted in US dollars per barrel. The price updates continuously through the trading day in the futures market traded on the New York exchange, with closing values reported, and it serves as the reference price for much of the crude oil produced and traded inside North America. West Texas Intermediate is a light sweet crude, meaning it has low density and low sulphur content, which makes it well suited to refining into gasoline and diesel. WTI is priced for delivery at an inland hub in the United States, so it reflects the supply and demand conditions of the landlocked US market connected by pipelines.

The price is determined in the futures market by the balance of physical supply and demand for US crude plus the positioning of financial traders. Supply factors are reflected because US shale drilling activity, pipeline capacity that moves crude from production regions to the delivery hub, and storage at that hub all affect how much crude is available. Demand factors are reflected because the consumption of US refineries that turn crude into fuels rises and falls with economic activity, travel, and industrial production. Inventory levels are reflected because the amount of crude held in US storage signals whether the domestic market is oversupplied or tight, and a buildup at the delivery hub can push the WTI price down on its own. The price is included as an economic indicator because oil is both a major input cost across the economy and the key revenue source for energy producers, so the WTI level affects inflation, consumer spending power, and energy-sector profitability. Because WTI is landlocked, transport and pipeline bottlenecks can pull it away from international crude prices, which is why it is read alongside the global benchmark rather than alone.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

WTI matters for sector investing because it is simultaneously a direct revenue driver for US energy producers and a major cost input for the rest of the economy, which creates clear winners and losers when the price moves. When WTI rises, energy producers in the Energy sector capture the higher price directly as revenue, while fuel-intensive sectors such as Industrials transportation and Consumer Discretionary travel companies face rising costs that compress margins. When WTI falls, energy producers lose revenue but fuel-consuming sectors gain margin relief and consumers gain spending power as gasoline prices drop. The metric matters with particular force for the Energy sector because the revenue of US oil and gas producers scales almost directly with the WTI price above their cost of production, and US shale economics are tied to WTI specifically. WTI also feeds into inflation because domestic fuel costs flow through to transportation and production costs across the economy. The combination of a direct revenue link to US energy producers and a cost impact on fuel-consuming sectors makes WTI a central input for choosing between energy and energy-consuming sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the WTI move being driven by changes in demand (which reflects the health of the US and global economy) or by changes in supply (which reflects US shale output, pipeline capacity, or geopolitical events)? Demand-driven moves signal economic strength or weakness that affects all cyclical sectors, while supply-driven moves act as a cost shock that helps energy producers but hurts the broader economy.

2. Are US crude inventories at the delivery hub rising or falling relative to seasonal norms, and does the inventory trend confirm whether the domestic market is genuinely tight or oversupplied? Inventory data at the US storage hub provides a physical-market check on whether the WTI move reflects real supply-demand conditions or a local storage effect.

3. Is the WTI move tracking the global benchmark or diverging from it, and what does the Brent-WTI spread say about whether the cause is US-specific or global? A widening spread signals a US landlocked or pipeline issue, while a stable spread signals a global supply-demand move that affects energy producers everywhere.

4. Is US shale production responding to the current WTI price by ramping up (which would add supply and cap the price) or holding back (which would let the price run higher)? Because US shale economics are tied to WTI, shale activity determines how sustainable a WTI move is.

5. Is there a pipeline or transport bottleneck between US production regions and the delivery hub that is pushing WTI away from the price of comparable crude elsewhere? Transport constraints are a WTI-specific factor that can move the price for reasons unrelated to broad supply and demand.

6. Is the WTI futures curve showing near-term prices above longer-dated prices (signaling current tightness) or below them (signaling expected future oversupply), and what does the curve shape say about the durability of the move? The futures curve reveals the market's view on whether current conditions will persist, which affects how long an energy-sector tilt should last.

7. How does US and global fuel demand look across the major consuming regions, and is oil demand rising with economic growth or falling with a slowdown? Because oil demand tracks economic activity, the demand trend is central to interpreting whether a WTI rise is healthy or a coming drag.

8. Is the US refining margin, the difference between the price of crude and the price of the gasoline and diesel made from it, wide or narrow, and what does it say about end-demand for fuel products? Refining margins reveal whether demand for finished fuels is strong, which is a more direct read on consumption than the crude price alone.

9. How does the current WTI price compare to the cost of production for US shale producers, and does the price leave room for them to profit or pressure them toward cutting output? The relationship between WTI and shale production cost determines US energy-sector profitability and whether domestic supply will adjust.

10. Is the WTI move large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect both energy-producer revenue and the cost structure of fuel-consuming sectors, or is it a short-term fluctuation that companies can manage through? Only sustained and meaningful moves warrant a sector rotation, since both producers and consumers can absorb minor temporary price swings.
