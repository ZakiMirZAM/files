# Inverse PE Yields vs Current Interest Rate Yields

## Part 1: Definition

This metric compares the earnings yield of the stock market to the yield available on government bonds. The earnings yield is calculated by inverting the price-to-earnings ratio, meaning it divides company earnings by the stock price to show how much earnings an investor gets for each dollar invested, expressed as a percentage. This is compared to the yield on government bonds, typically the 10-year Treasury, also expressed as a percentage. The comparison shows whether stocks or bonds offer more income per dollar invested, which is a gauge of the relative attractiveness of the two asset classes. The data updates as stock prices and bond yields move.

The comparison is constructed from two figures. The earnings yield is included because it expresses corporate earnings as a percentage of the stock price, making it directly comparable to a bond yield, and because it represents the return investors receive from company profits relative to what they pay for the stock. The bond yield is included because it represents the guaranteed return available from lending to the government, which serves as the baseline alternative to holding stocks. Subtracting the bond yield from the earnings yield, or comparing their levels, is included as the key calculation because it reveals the extra return that stocks offer over bonds, which is a measure of how much investors are being compensated for taking stock-market risk. When the earnings yield is well above the bond yield, stocks offer a large premium and appear attractive relative to bonds, while when the earnings yield falls near or below the bond yield, stocks offer little premium and bonds become more competitive. The comparison is interpreted as a relative-value signal between the two main asset classes.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

This comparison matters for sector investing because it reveals the relative attractiveness of stocks versus bonds, which affects whether capital flows into equities at all and which types of stocks benefit most from the prevailing rate environment. When the earnings yield is well above bond yields, stocks are cheap relative to bonds and the environment supports equities broadly, with higher-risk cyclical and growth sectors often benefiting most as investors reach for return. When bond yields rise toward the earnings yield, bonds become more competitive, which pressures the most expensive long-duration sectors first because their high valuations depend on low bond yields to justify them. The metric matters with particular force for long-duration growth sectors like Information Technology and for bond-substitute sectors like Utilities and Real Estate, because both compete directly with bonds for investor capital. The comparison also signals when the overall market is expensive or cheap, which affects the risk of holding equities. The combination of a relative-value signal between asset classes and a read on which rate-sensitive sectors face the most pressure makes this comparison a useful input for valuation-aware sector positioning.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the earnings yield based on trailing earnings that have already been reported, or on forward earnings estimates that may prove too optimistic, and how does the choice affect the reliability of the comparison? Forward earnings estimates can be too high near cycle peaks, which would make the equity premium look larger than it really is and mask overvaluation.

2. Are the corporate earnings used in the earnings yield sustainable, or are they inflated by peak-cycle profit margins that are likely to revert, which would shrink the real equity premium? When earnings reflect unsustainable peak margins, the apparent premium overstates the true attractiveness of stocks.

3. Is the equity premium thinning because stock prices have risen (making stocks expensive) or because bond yields have risen (making bonds more competitive), given that the two have different implications for which sectors are most at risk? Stock-driven thinning signals broad overvaluation, while bond-driven thinning pressures the most rate-sensitive sectors first.

4. Is the bond yield used in the comparison being held artificially low by central bank buying or other technical factors, which would make the equity premium look larger than it would be under normal conditions? When bond yields are artificially suppressed, the equity premium is flattered, and the comparison may overstate how attractive stocks are.

5. How does the current equity premium compare to its historical range, and is the premium wide or thin by historical standards in a way that signals stocks are cheap or expensive relative to bonds? The premium relative to history is what matters, since a premium that looks adequate in absolute terms may be thin compared to past norms.

6. Is the comparison being distorted by a small number of very large companies whose high valuations pull down the overall market earnings yield, masking better value in the rest of the market? When mega-cap valuations dominate the index, the headline earnings yield may misrepresent the value available in most sectors.

7. Does the level of inflation make the nominal comparison misleading, since high inflation raises bond yields for inflation compensation rather than real return, which affects how to interpret the premium? In high-inflation environments, the nominal comparison must be adjusted for inflation to reveal the true relative value.

8. Is the thin or wide premium consistent with where the economy sits in the business cycle, given that premiums tend to be wide near cycle troughs and thin near cycle peaks? The premium's relationship to the cycle stage helps confirm whether the valuation signal aligns with the broader economic picture.

9. Are investors likely to shift capital between stocks and bonds in response to the changing premium, and which sectors would see the most capital flow out if bonds become more attractive? The most expensive long-duration and bond-substitute sectors are most exposed to capital flowing toward bonds when the premium thins.

10. Is the change in the premium large enough and sustained enough to warrant a meaningful sector rotation, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary moves in stock prices or bond yields? Only a sustained and meaningful change in the relative-value relationship warrants repositioning, since both stock prices and bond yields fluctuate daily.
