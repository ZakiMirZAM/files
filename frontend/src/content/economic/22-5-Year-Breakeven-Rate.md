# 5-Year Breakeven Rate

## Part 1: Definition

The 5-Year Breakeven Rate measures the rate of inflation that financial markets expect on average over the next five years. It is expressed as an annual percentage and is derived by taking the yield on a regular 5-year Treasury note and subtracting the yield on a 5-year Treasury note that is protected against inflation. The result is the inflation rate at which an investor would earn the same return from either bond, which is the market's collective expectation of average inflation over the period. The data updates continuously through the trading day with daily values reported.

The breakeven rate is constructed from the difference between two types of government bonds. The regular Treasury note is included because its yield reflects investors' required compensation for expected inflation plus a real return, so it embeds the market's inflation expectation. The inflation-protected note is included because its principal and interest adjust with actual inflation, so its yield reflects only the real return investors require without the inflation component. Subtracting the inflation-protected yield from the regular yield isolates the inflation expectation, which is included as the key figure because it reveals what the market collectively believes inflation will average rather than what any single forecaster predicts. The five-year horizon is chosen because it captures medium-term inflation expectations that are relevant to both the business cycle and central bank policy without being dominated by short-term volatility. The breakeven rate is interpreted as a real-time gauge of inflation expectations because it reflects the actual money that investors are willing to put behind their inflation views, making it a market-based forecast that updates continuously.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The 5-Year Breakeven Rate matters for sector investing because it reveals the market's inflation expectations, which determine whether the environment favors inflation-benefiting sectors or disinflation-benefiting sectors and signals how the central bank is likely to respond. When breakeven inflation expectations are rising, the market expects more inflation ahead, which favors Energy, Materials, and Real Estate because these sectors benefit from rising prices and rents, while it pressures long-duration sectors whose distant earnings are discounted more heavily. When breakeven expectations are falling, the market expects disinflation, which favors Information Technology and other long-duration growth sectors because lower expected inflation supports lower rates and higher valuations on distant earnings. The metric matters because it is forward-looking, capturing what the market expects rather than what inflation has already done, which gives an early read on the inflation regime that drives sector rotation. Breakeven expectations also guide central bank policy, since rising expectations push toward tightening. The combination of a forward-looking inflation signal and a guide to policy direction makes the breakeven rate a useful input for positioning between inflation-sensitive sector groups.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in breakeven inflation expectations being driven by a genuine shift in the market's view of future inflation, or by technical factors in the inflation-protected bond market such as changes in liquidity or demand for inflation protection? Because the breakeven is derived from two bond markets, technical distortions in the smaller inflation-protected market can move the breakeven without reflecting a true change in inflation expectations.

2. Are the breakeven expectations being driven mainly by energy and commodity prices, which can reverse quickly, or by broad-based expectations of sustained inflation across the economy? Energy-driven expectation moves are less durable than broad-based ones, which affects how committed the inflation-sector rotation should be.

3. Are inflation expectations staying anchored near the central bank's target, or are they drifting away in a way that suggests the market is losing confidence in the central bank's ability to control inflation? Unanchored expectations signal a more serious inflation problem that requires a stronger policy response and favors a more committed rotation toward inflation-benefiting sectors.

4. How do the five-year expectations compare to longer-term expectations such as the five-year period beginning five years from now, and does the comparison show that the inflation concern is near-term or long-lasting? When near-term expectations rise but long-term expectations stay anchored, the inflation is seen as temporary, while a rise in long-term expectations signals a structural concern.

5. Are the breakeven expectations consistent with what consumer and business surveys show about inflation expectations, or is there a divergence between market-based and survey-based measures? When market and survey expectations agree, the inflation signal is more reliable than when they diverge.

6. Is the breakeven move consistent with what is happening to actual inflation, or is the market anticipating a change in inflation that has not yet appeared in the data? When breakevens lead actual inflation, they provide an early signal, but the investor must judge whether the market's anticipation will prove correct.

7. Is the central bank likely to respond to the change in inflation expectations by tightening or easing policy, and how would that response affect rate-sensitive and inflation-benefiting sectors? Because the central bank watches inflation expectations closely, anticipating its response helps position sectors ahead of the policy move.

8. Are global inflation expectations moving in the same direction as US expectations, and what does a synchronized move versus a US-specific move say about whether the inflation driver is global or domestic? Global inflation expectation moves point to common factors like commodity prices, while US-specific moves point to domestic policy or demand.

9. How large is the move in breakeven expectations relative to its historical range, and is the current level high or low enough to signal a meaningful shift in the inflation regime? A move within the normal range may not warrant a sector rotation, while a move to historical extremes signals a regime shift.

10. Is the change in inflation expectations large enough and durable enough to meaningfully affect the valuations of long-duration sectors and the revenue of commodity sectors, or is it a short-term fluctuation that will not change the regime? Only a sustained and meaningful shift in expectations warrants a sector rotation, since expectations can fluctuate on temporary news.
