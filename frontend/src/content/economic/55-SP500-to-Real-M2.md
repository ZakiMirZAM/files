# S&P 500 to Real M2

## Part 1: Definition

S&P 500 to Real M2 measures the level of the US stock market relative to the inflation-adjusted money supply. It is built by taking the daily S&P 500 index level and dividing it by Real M2 (the M2 money supply deflated by the Consumer Price Index), then rebasing the result to 100 at the start of the series so the line reads as an index of relative value rather than an awkward raw ratio. The unit is an index number, and because the S&P 500 updates every trading day while Real M2 updates monthly, the latest money-supply reading is carried forward across the days within each month.

The metric exists to separate two very different sources of rising stock prices. Nominal stock gains can come from genuine growth in corporate earnings and real wealth, or they can come from nothing more than a larger pile of money chasing the same assets. Dividing the index by the money supply removes the second effect. Using Real M2 rather than nominal M2 in the denominator removes a further layer of illusion: nominal M2 itself grows partly because of inflation, so a ratio against nominal M2 still leaves some monetary inflation embedded in the picture. Real M2 strips inflation out of the money stock first, so the resulting line answers a cleaner question, which is how the stock market has moved relative to the real purchasing power held in money. When this index rises, equities are outpacing the real money base and the gains reflect something beyond liquidity. When it falls, the market is being lifted by money creation faster than it is creating real value. The Nominal M2 toggle shows the same calculation against the unadjusted money stock, and the gap between the two views is itself the contribution of inflation.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

S&P 500 to Real M2 matters because it identifies the regime the whole market is operating in, and the regime determines which sectors are about to lead. A market that is rising on real value creation rewards a different set of sectors than a market that is rising on liquidity, even when the index level looks identical in both cases. When the ratio is climbing, real earnings and productivity are doing the heavy lifting, capital is being allocated to genuinely productive uses, and the sectors that compound real cash flows (Information Technology, Industrials, and quality cyclicals) tend to lead because their advance is backed by fundamentals rather than by the discount rate alone. When the ratio is falling while the nominal index still rises, the advance is liquidity-driven and inflationary, and leadership rotates toward real assets and inflation beneficiaries (Energy, Materials, and Real Estate) while the purchasing power of fixed nominal cash flows erodes. The metric also functions as a long-horizon valuation gauge for the market as a whole: extended readings far above the historical range have historically preceded weak forward real returns, because a market priced richly relative to the real money base has borrowed its gains from the future. The student should treat this ratio as a regime switch, using its direction to decide whether to favor real-compounding sectors or inflation-hedging sectors, and using its level to judge how much forward return has already been pulled forward.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is a change in the ratio being driven by the numerator (the S&P 500 level) or the denominator (Real M2), and what does the source say about whether the move reflects real value creation or a change in the monetary backdrop? A rising ratio from climbing stock prices is a fundamentals signal, while a rising ratio from a shrinking real money supply is a tightening signal with very different sector implications.

2. How far does the Real M2 view diverge from the Nominal M2 view, and what does the size of that gap reveal about how much inflation is embedded in the current market regime? A wide gap means inflation is doing much of the work behind nominal gains, which favors inflation-hedged sectors over long-duration growth.

3. Is the ratio stretched relative to its own multi-year history, and if so, how much forward real return has effectively been pulled forward into the present? An extended reading has historically preceded weaker forward real returns, which argues for rotating toward durable-cash-flow defensives before the regime turns.

4. Is Real M2 expanding or contracting, and is the change being driven by the nominal money supply or by the inflation rate used to deflate it? Contracting Real M2 from high inflation has different consequences than contracting Real M2 from outright monetary tightening, and the distinction changes which sectors are at risk.

5. Does the direction of this ratio agree with the direction of corporate earnings, or is the market rising on liquidity while real earnings stagnate? Agreement confirms a fundamentally driven advance that supports cyclicals and growth, while disagreement warns that the rally rests on money creation.

6. How does the ratio behave around turning points in the business cycle, and is the current move consistent with an early-cycle real recovery or a late-cycle liquidity-fueled blow-off? The same nominal index level can sit in either regime, and only the ratio reveals which one, with opposite implications for cyclical versus defensive positioning.

7. Is the money-supply growth feeding this ratio concentrated in a way that reaches the real economy and corporate earnings, or is it being absorbed into financial assets without productive use? Liquidity that inflates asset prices without funding real activity tends to push the ratio down and reward real assets over operating businesses.

8. When the ratio last reverted from a stretched level, which sectors led the unwind and which preserved capital, and are those same conditions present now? History of prior reversions helps distinguish a durable regime from one that is borrowing returns from the future.

9. Is the dollar strengthening or weakening alongside moves in this ratio, and does the currency confirm or contradict the liquidity story the ratio is telling? A weak dollar accompanying a falling ratio reinforces the inflationary, real-asset regime, while a strong dollar complicates it.

10. Does the Real M2 ratio confirm or contradict other long-horizon valuation gauges such as Shiller CAPE and the equity risk premium, and where the signals diverge, which input (earnings multiples or monetary excess) explains the disagreement? Convergent signals strengthen the case for a defensive tilt, while divergence isolates the specific source of the market's richness.
