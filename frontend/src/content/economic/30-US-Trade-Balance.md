# US Trade Balance

## Part 1: Definition

The US Trade Balance measures the difference between the value of goods and services the United States sells to other countries and the value of goods and services it buys from them, expressed in dollars over a period, usually a month. When exports exceed imports, the balance is a surplus, and when imports exceed exports, it is a deficit. The United States typically runs a deficit, so the figure is usually negative, and the most useful read is whether the deficit is widening or narrowing. The data is released monthly with a roughly six-week lag.

The trade balance is constructed by subtracting the value of imports from the value of exports across both goods and services. Goods exports and imports are included because physical products such as machinery, vehicles, electronics, agricultural products, and energy make up the largest part of trade and are the most sensitive to the exchange rate and global demand. Services exports and imports are included because the United States trades large amounts of services such as travel, financial services, and intellectual property licensing, which would be missed if only goods were counted. Exports are counted as a positive because they represent foreign demand for US production that brings money into the country, while imports are counted as a negative because they represent US demand for foreign production that sends money out. The balance is included as an economic indicator because it reflects the competitiveness of US production, the relative strength of domestic versus foreign demand, and the flow of money between the US and the rest of the world. A narrowing deficit can reflect stronger exports or weaker imports, while a widening deficit can reflect stronger domestic demand pulling in imports or weaker export competitiveness.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The US Trade Balance matters for sector investing because it reveals the balance between foreign demand for US production and domestic demand for foreign goods, which signals the relative health of export-producing sectors versus domestic-focused sectors and the broader competitive position of US industry. When the trade deficit narrows because exports are rising, it signals strong foreign demand that benefits export-heavy sectors such as Industrials, Materials, and Energy. When the deficit widens because imports are rising, it can signal strong domestic demand that benefits domestic-focused retailers, or weakening export competitiveness that hurts exporters. The metric matters because it ties together the dollar, global demand, and domestic demand into a read on which sectors are positioned to benefit from trade flows. The trade balance also reflects the savings and investment balance in the economy and affects the value of the dollar. The combination of a signal about export-sector health, a read on domestic versus foreign demand, and a link to the dollar makes the trade balance a useful supporting input for positioning between globally exposed and domestically focused sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is a change in the trade balance being driven by the export side (reflecting foreign demand and US competitiveness) or by the import side (reflecting domestic demand and the dollar)? The same change in the balance can carry opposite signals depending on which side is moving, so identifying the driver is essential for reading which sectors benefit.

2. Is a narrowing deficit a positive sign of rising exports and strong foreign demand, or a negative sign of falling imports and weakening domestic demand? A narrowing deficit can reflect either strength or weakness, so understanding the cause determines whether to favor export sectors or worry about domestic demand.

3. Is the trade balance change being driven by goods trade (sensitive to manufacturing and the dollar) or services trade (sensitive to global business activity and travel), and what does the mix say about which sectors are affected? Goods-driven changes affect Industrials and Materials, while services-driven changes affect different sectors.

4. Is the energy trade balance, now a significant factor as the US exports more energy, driving the overall trade balance, and what does that say about the energy sector specifically? Because energy has become a major export, the energy portion of the balance carries direct implications for energy producers.

5. Is the dollar's strength or weakness consistent with the trade balance trend, since a strong dollar typically widens the deficit by making imports cheap and exports expensive? When the dollar and trade balance align as expected, the signal is reliable, while a divergence suggests other forces at work.

6. Are tariffs or trade policy changes affecting the trade balance in ways that reflect policy rather than underlying competitiveness or demand? Policy-driven changes in trade flows carry different implications than demand-driven or currency-driven changes and may reverse with policy.

7. Is the United States gaining or losing competitiveness in global markets, or is the trade balance simply tracking the relative strength of domestic versus foreign demand? Genuine competitiveness changes affect US producers' long-run position, while demand-driven changes reflect cyclical conditions.

8. Is the trade balance change consistent with what real exports and the dollar are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests measurement timing or other distortions? Confirmation across trade indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence warrants caution.

9. Is the trade deficit being financed sustainably by foreign investment in US assets, and what does the financing picture say about the durability of the current trade pattern? The way the deficit is financed affects whether the trade pattern can continue and what it means for the dollar and capital flows.

10. Is the change in the trade balance large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect the revenue of export-producing or import-dependent sectors, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary factors such as inventory timing or one-time shipments? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants a sector rotation, since the monthly trade balance can be volatile.
