# 10-Year Real Yield (TIPS)

## Part 1: Definition

The 10-Year Real Yield measures the interest rate an investor earns on a 10-year US government bond after expected inflation is removed, so it represents the real, inflation-adjusted return on a safe long-term asset. It is expressed as a percentage and the data is reported daily. The yield is read directly from Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, which are government bonds whose principal is adjusted with the Consumer Price Index, so their quoted yield already strips out inflation and leaves the real return. The same number can be reconstructed as the nominal 10-year Treasury yield minus the 10-year breakeven inflation rate, which is why it is often called the real yield.

The yield is constructed by tracking the market price of the 10-year inflation-protected bond and solving for the rate that price implies, which moves inversely to the price. The inflation-protection feature is reflected because the bond's principal rises with the Consumer Price Index, so the investor is compensated for inflation separately and the quoted yield is left as the pure real return. The nominal yield is reflected because the real yield plus the market's expected inflation equals the nominal 10-year yield, so the real yield is the piece of the nominal rate that is not compensation for inflation. The demand for safe real returns is reflected because when investors bid heavily for inflation-protected bonds the price rises and the real yield falls, while heavy selling pushes the real yield up. The metric is included as an indicator because the real yield is the true cost of money and the discount rate that determines the present value of long-dated cash flows, which makes it the single most important rate for valuing long-duration assets. A deeply negative real yield means safe assets are guaranteed to lose purchasing power, which pushes investors toward riskier assets and inflates valuations, while a high positive real yield rewards safety and compresses valuations across the market.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The 10-Year Real Yield matters for sector investing because it is the discount rate applied to future cash flows, and sectors differ sharply in how far into the future their earnings sit. Long-duration sectors like Information Technology and Communication Services derive most of their value from earnings expected years ahead, so a rise in the real yield discounts those distant cash flows more heavily and compresses their valuations, while a fall in the real yield lifts them. Rate-sensitive sectors like Real Estate and Utilities carry heavy debt and are valued for their yield, so a rising real yield raises their financing costs and makes their fixed payouts less attractive against safe bonds, while a falling real yield supports them. Shorter-duration and cyclical sectors whose earnings are near-term and tied to the economic cycle are less sensitive to the discount rate and more sensitive to growth. The metric matters because it separates the two forces that move the market, the real cost of money and expected inflation, and isolates the one that most directly drives valuation multiples. For sector investing the real yield is the cleanest read on whether the rate environment is a tailwind or a headwind for the highest-multiple, longest-duration parts of the market.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is a move in the real yield being driven by a change in the nominal Treasury yield or by a change in inflation expectations, given that the real yield is the nominal yield minus breakeven inflation and each source has different implications? A rise driven by a falling breakeven is a disinflation signal, while a rise driven by a climbing nominal yield with steady inflation expectations is a pure increase in the real cost of money.

2. Is the level of the real yield negative, low positive, or high positive, and how far is it from its typical range, since a deeply negative real yield distorts valuations far more than a modestly positive one? The regime matters because the effect on long-duration sectors is nonlinear and most extreme when real yields are deeply negative.

3. Is the real yield rising because the market expects stronger real growth, or because the central bank is holding policy restrictive to fight inflation, given that each has different implications for cyclical versus defensive sectors? A growth-driven rise supports cyclicals and Financials, while a policy-driven rise into a slowdown favors defensives.

4. Is the speed of the move in the real yield gradual or abrupt, since a rapid repricing compresses long-duration valuations faster than the market can absorb and creates sharper sector rotation? The pace matters because abrupt moves force disorderly repositioning out of the highest-multiple sectors.

5. Is the central bank actively suppressing the real yield through asset purchases, or is the real yield free to reflect market forces, given that policy suppression props up long-duration valuations artificially? The policy influence matters because a real yield held down by purchases can snap higher when that support is withdrawn.

6. Is the real yield move consistent with what the nominal curve and its slope are showing, or is it diverging in a way that signals a specific repricing of real returns? Confirmation across the curve strengthens the read, while a divergence isolates a change in real returns specifically.

7. How sensitive are the leading sectors in the current market to the discount rate, given that a market led by long-duration growth names is far more exposed to a rising real yield than one led by cyclicals? The market's composition matters because the same real yield move has a larger index-level effect when the leaders are long-duration.

8. Is the real yield high enough that safe inflation-protected bonds now compete with equities for capital, which would cap valuation multiples across the market? The competition from safe real returns matters because it sets a ceiling on how high multiples can go.

9. Is the change in the real yield being anticipated by the market, or is it a surprise that could force a repricing of long-duration valuations? Anticipated moves are already reflected in prices, while surprises drive the sharpest rotations.

10. Is the real yield move large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully change the discount rate applied to long-duration cash flows, or is it a routine fluctuation that will not alter sector leadership? Only a large and sustained move warrants rotating between long-duration and short-duration sectors, since the real yield fluctuates daily with market conditions.
