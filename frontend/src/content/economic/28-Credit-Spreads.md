# Credit Spreads

## Part 1: Definition

Credit Spreads measure the extra interest that companies must pay to borrow compared to the US government, expressed in percentage points or basis points. A spread is calculated by taking the yield on a corporate bond and subtracting the yield on a government bond of the same maturity. The difference is the extra compensation investors demand for the risk that the company might not repay. Spreads are tracked separately for investment-grade bonds, issued by financially strong companies, and high-yield bonds, issued by riskier companies. The data updates continuously through the trading day with daily values reported.

Credit Spreads are constructed by comparing corporate bond yields to government bond yields across the range of corporate borrowers. The corporate bond yield is included because it reflects the total return investors require to lend to a company, which embeds compensation for the risk of default, the risk of the bond becoming less liquid, and a return premium. The government bond yield is subtracted because it represents the return on lending with virtually no default risk, so removing it isolates the extra compensation specifically related to corporate credit risk. Investment-grade spreads are tracked because they reflect the borrowing conditions for financially strong companies and signal stress in the core of the corporate economy. High-yield spreads are tracked because they reflect borrowing conditions for riskier companies and are more sensitive to economic stress, making them an early warning signal. The spread is included as an indicator because it reveals how much risk investors perceive in lending to companies, which widens when investors grow fearful about defaults and economic weakness and narrows when investors are confident, making spreads a real-time gauge of financial conditions and credit risk.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Credit Spreads matter for sector investing because they reveal the market's perception of corporate credit risk and the cost of corporate borrowing, which signals financial conditions and economic stress that drive sector leadership. When spreads are narrow, investors are confident, credit is cheap and available, and companies can borrow easily to fund expansion, which supports cyclical sectors and especially Financials that profit from healthy credit markets. When spreads widen, investors are growing fearful about defaults and economic weakness, credit becomes expensive and scarce, and the widening often precedes or accompanies economic downturns that favor defensive sectors. The metric matters with particular force for Financials because banks and lenders are directly exposed to credit conditions, and for highly leveraged companies across sectors whose borrowing costs rise as spreads widen. Credit spreads are also one of the most reliable early warning signals of financial stress, since they widen before many other indicators turn down. The combination of a read on financial conditions, a direct link to credit-sensitive sectors, and an early warning of stress makes credit spreads a high-priority input for the cyclical-versus-defensive decision.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the spread move concentrated in high-yield bonds, which signal stress among riskier borrowers, or has it spread to investment-grade bonds, which would signal stress reaching the core of the corporate economy? Stress that spreads from high-yield to investment-grade signals a broadening problem that affects the whole corporate sector, not just the riskiest borrowers.

2. Is the widening in spreads being driven by genuine concern about rising defaults, or by a broader flight from risk where investors demand more compensation even without a specific default threat? A default-driven widening reflects real credit deterioration, while a flight-from-risk widening may reverse when sentiment improves.

3. Are the spreads widening because corporate fundamentals such as leverage and cash flow are deteriorating, or because of external shocks unrelated to corporate health? Fundamental-driven widening signals genuine corporate stress, while shock-driven widening may not reflect the underlying health of borrowers.

4. Is the level of spreads consistent with the actual default rate, or are spreads pricing in far more defaults than are actually occurring, which could signal either excessive fear or an early warning? When spreads price in more defaults than are happening, the market may be anticipating deterioration or overreacting, which affects how to read the signal.

5. Are companies still able to refinance their maturing debt at reasonable costs, or are widening spreads creating a refinancing problem for borrowers with debt coming due? A refinancing problem can turn manageable debt into a crisis, which is especially dangerous for highly leveraged sectors.

6. Are credit covenants, the protective terms in lending agreements, tightening or loosening, which signals whether lenders are growing cautious or remaining willing to extend easy terms? Tightening covenants signal growing lender caution that often precedes wider spreads and credit stress.

7. Is the spread move consistent with what equity market volatility and other risk indicators are showing, or is credit stress diverging from equity calm in a way that provides an early warning? When credit markets show stress that equity markets have not yet priced, it provides an early signal of trouble ahead.

8. How does the current spread level compare to its historical range, and is it at levels associated with normal conditions, building stress, or crisis? The spread level relative to history reveals whether conditions are normal, deteriorating, or at crisis levels that warrant a full defensive rotation.

9. Are non-bank lenders such as private credit funds showing the same stress as the public bond market, or is stress concentrated in one part of the credit system? Divergence between public and private credit can reveal where stress is building and whether it is broad-based.

10. Is the change in spreads large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in financial conditions, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary market moves? Only a sustained and meaningful widening or narrowing warrants a sector rotation, since spreads can move on temporary sentiment shifts.
