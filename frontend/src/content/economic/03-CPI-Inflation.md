# CPI Inflation

## Part 1: Definition

CPI Inflation measures the percentage change in the average price of a fixed basket of consumer goods and services bought by a typical urban household, comparing the current month to the same month one year earlier. The unit is a percentage rate. Data is released every month, around the second or third week, and covers the prior month's prices. Two main versions are reported: the headline rate which includes everything, and the core rate which excludes food and energy because those two categories swing too much month to month and obscure the underlying trend.

The basket is constructed by tracking the prices of hundreds of specific items across eight major categories: food and beverages, housing, apparel, transportation, medical care, recreation, education and communication, and other goods and services. Each category is included because together they cover where the average household actually spends its money, so the resulting index reflects the real cost of maintaining the household's standard of living. Each item is weighted by its share of typical household spending, which means that housing (about 35% of the index) gets the largest weight because households spend more on rent and mortgage equivalents than on any other category, while items like apparel (about 3%) get small weights because they represent a small share of total spending. The weights are included this way so that a 10% rise in housing costs has a much larger impact on the index than a 10% rise in clothing prices, reflecting the real impact on household budgets. Substitution effects are partially accounted for because households shift toward cheaper alternatives when prices rise, and the basket is updated every two years to reflect changing spending patterns. The shelter component is built from observed rents because owner-occupied housing is treated as a service that homeowners pay themselves rather than an asset, which causes shelter inflation to lag the actual rental market by 6 to 12 months.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

CPI Inflation is critical for sector investing because it determines which type of company can maintain or grow margins in the current environment and which type faces margin compression. Inflation also shapes the central bank's policy response, which in turn affects the cost of capital that drives sector valuations. When inflation is rising, companies that produce the inputs causing the inflation (Energy, Materials, parts of Real Estate through rents) capture price gains directly through revenue, while companies that consume those inputs (Consumer Discretionary, Industrials, Utilities) face margin pressure. When inflation is falling, the relationship reverses: long-duration sectors like Information Technology and Communication Services benefit from falling discount rates that lift the present value of their distant earnings, while commodity producers face declining revenue. The combination of margin impact and discount-rate impact makes CPI the single most important price-side variable for sector rotation, and historical sector return data confirms that the inflation regime explains more cross-sectional variation in sector performance than almost any other factor besides the growth phase itself. The student should think of CPI as the second cycle variable alongside GDP growth, with the two together defining a four-quadrant map (growth up or down by inflation up or down) that the historical sector rotation playbook follows closely.

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Energy producers sell the oil and gas that are major inputs to rising inflation. As inflation rises, energy prices rise, and energy revenue scales directly with the commodity prices. | Buy when CPI is above 3% and rising. Avoid when CPI is falling toward target or below. |
| Materials | Materials companies sell the metals, chemicals, and basic inputs whose prices rise with inflation. Pricing power passes through to revenue. | Buy when CPI is rising on commodity-driven inflation. Avoid in disinflation when commodity prices fall. |
| Real Estate | Rents adjust upward with inflation, particularly on lease renewals, supporting REIT revenue and property values. Real estate is also a hedge against monetary debasement. | Buy when CPI is rising moderately between 3 and 4%. Avoid when CPI is above 5% because central bank tightening compresses cap rates. |
| Information Technology | Tech companies have long-duration cash flows whose present value is highly sensitive to discount rates. Falling inflation allows rates to fall, which lifts tech valuations. | Buy when CPI is 2 to 3% and falling. Avoid when CPI is above 4% and rising. |
| Communication Services | Similar duration sensitivity to tech. Streaming and digital advertising are also tied to consumer spending power, which is eroded by high inflation. | Buy when CPI is stable or falling. Avoid in high inflation environments. |
| Consumer Discretionary | High inflation erodes real consumer purchasing power, reducing demand for discretionary goods. Low inflation supports real wage growth and discretionary spending. | Buy when CPI is stable at target with strong employment. Avoid when CPI is above 4% because real wages compress. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples companies can pass through moderate price increases because demand is inelastic. The sector outperforms in high-inflation and deflation environments due to defensive characteristics. | Buy in extreme inflation environments above 4% or in deflation. Underperforms in moderate inflation when cyclicals lead. |
| Health Care | Health care pricing is largely set by reimbursement contracts that adjust slowly. The sector is defensive in deflation but can struggle in high inflation when costs rise faster than reimbursement. | Buy in disinflation or deflation. Defensive default in most environments. |
| Industrials | Input cost inflation compresses industrial margins when companies cannot pass through prices fast enough. Falling inflation supports margin expansion. | Buy when CPI is stable or falling and growth is strong. Avoid in high inflation that squeezes margins. |
| Financials | Inflation interacts with financials through interest rates. Banks earn wider net interest margins when rates rise to fight inflation, but loan growth slows. | Buy in early stages of rising inflation when banks expand margins. Avoid in late tightening cycles when loan growth collapses. |
| Utilities | Utility revenue depends on regulated rate structures that adjust slowly to inflation, causing margin pressure when input costs rise. The sector also faces rate-sensitivity risk when central banks tighten. | Buy in low-inflation, falling-rate environments. Avoid in high-inflation, rising-rate environments. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside CPI Inflation | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| PPI Inflation | PPI captures inflation pressures at the producer level before they reach the consumer. Reading the two together tells investors whether the consumer inflation is being driven by upstream cost pass-through (sustained) or by domestic demand pressures or one-time effects (potentially transient). | When PPI is rising sharply AND CPI is also rising, the inflation is being driven by upstream cost pressure that will likely continue, and Energy and Materials sectors deserve overweight positions because they sell the inputs causing the inflation. When PPI is falling but CPI is still rising, the inflation is concentrated in services and demand-side pressures, which argues for shorter-duration cyclicals over commodity producers. When PPI is falling and CPI is also falling, disinflation is broad and Information Technology and Consumer Discretionary should lead. |
| M2 Money Supply | The long-run quantity theory of money says sustained inflation requires sustained money supply growth above output growth. Reading CPI alongside M2 reveals whether the current inflation has monetary fuel that will sustain it or whether it is a one-time level shift from supply shocks. | When CPI is rising AND M2 is also growing rapidly, inflation has monetary support and will likely persist, which favors Energy, Materials, and Real Estate over an extended period. When CPI is rising but M2 is contracting, the inflation is supply-driven and may fade as central bank tightening takes effect, which argues for shorter-term commodity exposure and a rotation back to Information Technology when M2 stabilizes. |
| Employment | Wage growth is one of the key drivers of services inflation, which is the stickier component of CPI. Reading inflation alongside employment tells investors whether wage pressures are feeding inflation or whether labor market loosening is helping disinflation. | When CPI is rising AND employment is tight (unemployment below 4%), wage-driven inflation is in play and likely persistent, which forces continued policy tightening and pressures long-duration sectors. When CPI is rising but employment is loosening, the inflation is concentrated in goods or housing rather than services, and disinflation may follow as the labor market cools, which sets up Information Technology and Consumer Discretionary for a rebound. |
| GDP Growth | The combination of growth and inflation defines the four classic regime quadrants for asset returns. Reading them together produces the cleanest sector rotation map available from public data. | Strong GDP with falling CPI is the Goldilocks regime that favors Information Technology, Consumer Discretionary, and Communication Services. Strong GDP with rising CPI favors Energy and Materials. Weak GDP with rising CPI favors Consumer Staples and Health Care. Weak GDP with falling CPI favors Utilities and long-duration bonds. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the inflation being driven by demand pressures (households and businesses competing for limited goods, pushing prices up) or by cost-push pressures (rising input costs forcing producers to raise prices to protect margins)? The two causes call for different policy responses and favor different sectors, with demand-pull inflation pointing to broad cyclical leadership and cost-push inflation pointing specifically to Energy and Materials.

2. Is the money supply growing faster than the economy's output growth (a precondition for sustained inflation per the long-run quantity theory), and how does this relationship inform whether the current inflation is structural or transitory? Sustained inflation requires sustained money growth, so an inflation reading that is not accompanied by money supply expansion is likely to fade.

3. What share of the headline CPI is coming from volatile categories like food and energy (which can swing temporarily) versus stable core categories (which signal the underlying trend)? Investors should focus on the core inflation trend for sector rotation decisions because the volatile components do not warrant lasting sector tilts.

4. Within core inflation, is the pressure concentrated in goods (more cyclical and tied to supply chains) or in services (stickier and tied to wages)? Services inflation is harder to bring down and signals that the central bank will need to keep policy tight for longer, which extends pressure on long-duration sectors like Information Technology.

5. Is shelter inflation (the largest single component of core CPI at about a third of the index) reflecting current rental market conditions, or is it reflecting rent agreements signed 6 to 12 months ago that no longer match the current market? Shelter has a built-in lag, so falling current rents may not show up in CPI for many months and the true underlying inflation pace may be different from the official number.

6. Are inflation expectations among consumers, businesses, and bond markets staying anchored near the central bank's target, or are they drifting upward in a way that could embed inflation in wage and price-setting behavior? When expectations become unanchored, inflation becomes much harder to bring down without a recession, which forces a more painful policy response that hurts cyclicals.

7. Are firms reporting that they have pricing power and can pass through cost increases to customers, or are they reporting that competition prevents pass-through and is forcing them to absorb costs in margins? Pricing power preserves margins and supports companies that have it (typically branded consumer staples and dominant tech platforms), while the inability to pass through costs hurts companies that cannot.

8. Are wages growing faster than productivity, generating unit labor cost pressure that mathematically forces ongoing inflation, or is productivity growing fast enough to absorb wage gains without raising unit costs? Productivity-absorbed wage growth supports a Goldilocks environment that favors Information Technology, while unit cost pressure forces continued inflation that favors Energy and Materials.

9. Is the current inflation rate a one-time level shift caused by a specific event (a tariff, a tax change, a currency move, a supply chain disruption), or is it a sustained run-rate that will continue at the current pace going forward? One-time level shifts do not require lasting sector tilts, but sustained run-rates warrant durable rotation toward inflation-benefiting sectors.

10. Is the inflation experience in the United States similar to or different from inflation in other developed economies, and what does any divergence say about whether the cause is global (suggesting common factors like commodity prices) or US-specific (suggesting domestic policy or demand conditions)? Global inflation patterns affect different sectors differently, with global commodity-driven inflation favoring US Energy producers because they sell into global markets.
