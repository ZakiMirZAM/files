# How the Economy Works

## What it is

This overview is the working model behind the Understand the Economy First questions. Every metric on this page is a gauge on one machine. Reading a gauge without knowing the machine produces confident answers that mean nothing, so this page asks you to build the machine first: what produces growth, what credit does to it, how policy reaches markets, and how the biggest foreign economies transmit into US sectors.

The output of an economy is hours worked multiplied by output per hour. Long-term growth therefore comes from two sources only: more workers, and more output per worker. The first is set by birth rates and immigration, which move slowly and are visible decades ahead. The second is productivity, which comes from better tools, better training, and better ideas. Everything else, every boom and every recession, is a swing around that slow trend line rather than a change in it.

The swings come mostly from credit. When credit expands, borrowers spend more than they earn, and one person's spending is another person's income, so incomes rise, collateral values rise, and lending gets easier still. When credit contracts, the same loop runs in reverse. Two cycles run at once. The short-term debt cycle is the familiar business cycle of five to ten years: the central bank eases, credit and spending expand, inflation appears, the central bank tightens, spending contracts, and the cycle resets. The long-term debt cycle runs over decades: each short cycle tends to end with a little more debt than the last, until total debt service consumes so much income that lowering interest rates can no longer restart borrowing, and the economy faces a deleveraging rather than an ordinary recession.

## The pieces of the machine

| Piece | What it does | Where this page shows it |
|---|---|---|
| Productivity and labor | Sets the long-run growth trend the cycles swing around | Real GDP Growth, GDP Output Gap, Industrial Production, Labor Force Participation, US Population |
| The short-term debt cycle | Easing and tightening of credit drives the business cycle | Treasury yields, the yield-curve spreads, 30-Year Mortgage Rate, C&I Loans, Bank Loans |
| The long-term debt cycle | Accumulated debt relative to income limits how much more borrowing can lift spending | Total Non-Financial Debt % of GDP, M2, Fed Balance Sheet, Net Liquidity |
| Inflation and its pricing | The gap between demand and capacity moves prices; the market prices the future separately | CPI, PPI, 5-Year Breakeven, Import Prices, the commodity tiles |
| Policy transmission | Rates change discount rates and debt service; liquidity changes the money available to bid for assets | Fed Balance Sheet, Treasury General Account, SOFR-OIS Spread, the Fed model |
| Psychology and pricing | Confidence decides whether the same fundamentals get a high price or a low one | VIX, Consumer Sentiment, CAPE, the implied equity risk premium, AAII allocation |

Policy transmits into markets through three doors. Rates set the discount rate every asset is valued against and the cost of every floating debt. Liquidity, meaning bank reserves and the size of the central bank balance sheet, sets how much money is free to bid for assets; the Net Liquidity metric on this page nets the Treasury's cash account and reverse repo out of the balance sheet for exactly this reason. Expectations move markets before either of the first two arrives, because markets price what policy is expected to do, not what it did.

## The big-player economies

The United States grows on consumption, which is roughly two thirds of GDP, funded by wages and household credit. Its fragilities are whatever the consumer's balance sheet is carrying and whatever the fiscal deficit must refinance. China grows on investment and exports: infrastructure, property, and manufacturing funded by state-directed credit. Its property and local-government debt overhang is the classic late stage of a long-term debt cycle, and a Chinese slowdown reaches US sectors through commodity demand (Basic Materials, Energy), capital-goods orders (Industrials), and revenue exposure (Technology). The euro area depends on exports and imported energy, so it catches both a global slowdown and an energy shock; its central bank must set one policy for many fiscal sovereigns, which slows its response. Japan matters out of proportion to its growth because decades of low rates made the yen the world's funding currency: Japanese institutions hold large stocks of foreign bonds, and when the Bank of Japan tightens, that capital gets called home and global yields rise with it.

## Why it matters

Every judgment this page later asks for depends on this model. Whether an inflation print matters depends on whether it is a demand overshoot or a supply shock, which is a question about the machine. Whether a sector is helped by a reading depends on the channel, earnings or discount rate, which is a question about transmission. Whether the rotation matrix's favorite sector deserves the ranking depends on where the debt cycle sits, which no single metric shows. The analyst who knows the machine reads forty metrics as one story; the analyst who does not reads forty disconnected charts.

## Common traps

The first trap is reading every metric as equally important all the time. The machine decides which gauge matters now: in a tightening cycle the curve and credit spreads lead; in a deleveraging, liquidity and debt service dominate everything else.

The second trap is confusing the level of activity with its sustainability. Spending funded by credit growth above income growth is borrowed from the future, and the metric that looks strongest at the top of a cycle is often the one about to reverse.

The third trap is treating policy as a lever with instant effect. Rate changes reach housing in months, corporate investment in quarters, and inflation in a year or more. The market prices the expectation immediately, which is why prices move on the announcement and the economy moves later.

The fourth trap is analyzing the US in isolation. The dollar, foreign demand, and foreign funding flows sit inside every US sector's earnings. A China construction slowdown is a Basic Materials earnings event; a Bank of Japan tightening is a Treasury yield event; neither appears anywhere in US domestic data first.
