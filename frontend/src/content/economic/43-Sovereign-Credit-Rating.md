# Sovereign Credit Rating

## Part 1: Definition

The Sovereign Credit Rating measures the creditworthiness of a national government, expressed as a letter grade assigned by rating agencies that judges how likely the government is to repay its debt. The grades run from the highest quality at the top down through progressively riskier levels, with a clear line separating investment-grade ratings from speculative or junk ratings. Ratings change infrequently, only when an agency formally reviews and adjusts them, and changes are accompanied by an outlook that signals the likely direction of future changes. The rating affects the government's borrowing costs and serves as a benchmark for the credit quality of the entire country.

The rating is constructed by the agencies assessing several factors that determine a government's ability and willingness to repay its debt. The level and trajectory of government debt relative to the economy is included because a heavy and rising debt burden raises the risk that the government cannot service its obligations. The strength and stability of the economy is included because a strong economy generates the tax revenue needed to service debt, while a weak one strains repayment capacity. The government's fiscal position, including its deficit and revenue base, is included because persistent large deficits add to debt and signal difficulty balancing the budget. Political stability and institutional strength are included because a stable government with sound institutions is more likely to honor its debts than an unstable one. For countries that borrow in their own currency, the ability to print money to repay is also considered. These factors are combined into a single letter grade that is included as a summary judgment of credit quality, which affects the government's borrowing costs and signals the overall risk environment of the country.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Sovereign Credit Rating matters for sector investing because it reflects the creditworthiness and financial stability of the national government, which affects borrowing costs across the economy, the currency, and the overall risk environment that shapes sector positioning. A high and stable rating signals financial stability that supports low borrowing costs, a firm currency, and a healthy environment for risk assets, which broadly supports cyclical sectors. A downgrade or a negative outlook signals deteriorating government finances that can raise borrowing costs across the economy, pressure the currency, and increase risk aversion, which favors defensive positioning. The metric matters with particular force for Financials, because banks hold large amounts of government debt and a sovereign downgrade directly affects the value of those holdings and the banks' own funding costs. For a country like the United States with the dominant reserve currency, the rating is more of a slow-moving structural backdrop than a fast trading signal, but rating actions can still move markets and affect borrowing costs. The combination of a read on government financial stability, an effect on economy-wide borrowing costs, and a direct link to Financials makes the rating a useful structural input.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the rating change or outlook revision being driven by the government's debt trajectory, by the strength of the economy, by the fiscal deficit, or by political instability, given that each factor reflects a different aspect of creditworthiness? Identifying the driver reveals whether the credit concern is structural and lasting or tied to a specific issue that could resolve.

2. Does the country borrow in its own currency, which gives it the ability to print money to repay debt and reduces default risk, or in foreign currency, which creates a harder constraint? Domestic-currency borrowers face far lower default risk than foreign-currency borrowers, which affects how seriously to treat a downgrade.

3. Does the country have reserve-currency status that allows it to sustain a higher debt burden and weaker finances than other countries could, which affects the practical meaning of the rating? The dominant reserve currency provides a buffer that makes a rating closer to a symbolic backdrop than an imminent default risk.

4. Is the rating change likely to trigger forced selling by investors who are restricted to holding only investment-grade debt, which would amplify the market impact? A downgrade across the investment-grade line can force large institutional selling, which amplifies the financial market impact beyond the credit concern itself.

5. Is the outlook accompanying the rating positive, stable, or negative, and what does it signal about the likely direction of future rating changes? The outlook provides forward guidance that often matters more for markets than the current rating level, since it signals where the rating is heading.

6. Are the different rating agencies in agreement, or do they disagree about the country's creditworthiness in a way that creates uncertainty? Agreement across agencies strengthens the signal, while disagreement creates uncertainty about the true credit picture.

7. Is the credit concern reflected in the rating already priced into government bond yields and the currency, or would a rating action come as a surprise that moves markets? When credit concerns are already priced in, a rating action has less impact, while a surprise action can move markets sharply.

8. How exposed are the domestic banks to government debt, and how much would a downgrade affect their balance sheets and funding costs? Because banks hold government debt and are tied to sovereign creditworthiness, the extent of their exposure determines how much a downgrade would harm the Financials sector.

9. Is the rating change consistent with what bond yields, credit spreads, and the currency are signaling, or is it lagging market signals that have already moved? Rating agencies often lag market signals, so checking whether the market has already moved reveals whether the rating action confirms or trails the market.

10. Is the change in the rating or outlook significant enough to meaningfully affect borrowing costs, the currency, and the risk environment, or is it a minor adjustment within a still-strong credit position? Only a meaningful deterioration that affects borrowing costs and the risk environment warrants adjusting sector positioning, since minor adjustments within a strong rating carry little practical impact.
