# Bank Loans and Leases

## Part 1: Definition

Bank Loans and Leases measures the total dollar amount of credit that all US commercial banks have extended, reported in billions of dollars. It is the broad credit aggregate that adds together business loans, commercial real estate loans, consumer loans, and other lending on bank balance sheets. The base series shown is total loans and leases in bank credit at all commercial banks, which stood near 13.86 trillion dollars in mid-2026. The chart also offers a variant series, commercial and industrial loans at large domestically chartered banks, which is the business-lending slice and stood near 1.56 trillion dollars. Both series are reported weekly and can be viewed as a level (the dollar amount outstanding) or as a year-over-year growth rate (the percentage change from one year earlier). The metric reveals how much credit the banking system is supplying, which is one of the main forces that drives the economy through the credit cycle.

The figure is constructed by totaling the loans and leases that banks carry as assets and comparing the total to the level one year earlier when the growth rate is shown. Business loans are included because they fund company operations, inventory, equipment, and expansion. Commercial real estate loans are included because they fund property purchases and construction. Consumer loans are included because they fund household spending such as cars and credit cards. The combined total is the broad credit aggregate because credit from any source feeds spending and asset prices across the economy. The C&I variant is tracked separately because business lending tends to turn before the broad total, so it acts as an earlier signal. The metric is included as an indicator because the supply of bank credit is a primary engine of the credit cycle. Credit growth funds the spending and asset purchases that drive expansions, and a sharp slowdown in credit growth has preceded past economic slowdowns. A high or rising level of credit is not proof of trouble by itself, because a growing economy needs more credit over time. The loan quality and the rate of change matter more than the level alone, and they must be weighed alongside other signals.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Bank Loans and Leases matters for sector investing because the supply of bank credit is one of the main forces in the credit cycle, and the credit cycle drives which sectors lead. When credit is growing strongly, businesses and households can borrow to spend and invest, which fuels expansion and supports cyclical sectors and especially Financials that earn on the lending. When credit growth rolls over, spending and asset purchases lose their fuel, which signals a slowdown that favors defensive sectors. The metric matters with particular force for Financials, because lending is the core business of banks, so credit growth drives bank revenue directly. It matters for Real Estate, because property is bought largely with borrowed money, so the flow of credit supports property values. The credit cycle also carries a bubble warning. Late in a boom, credit often climbs to record highs and grows rapidly, because easy lending funds the very assets that are inflating. A sharp roll-over in the growth rate from a high level has preceded past slowdowns. The combination of a read on the credit cycle, a direct link to bank lending revenue, and a warning about late-cycle excess makes bank credit a useful input for positioning between cyclical and defensive sectors.

|---|---|---|
| High-Yield Credit Spread | Bank credit reflects the quantity of lending while the high-yield spread reflects its price and the risk perceived in riskier borrowers, so reading them together shows whether credit is both flowing and cheap or contracting and feared. The two together give a fuller picture of credit conditions. This combines the quantity and the price of credit. | When credit growth is strong AND the high-yield spread is narrow, credit is both abundant and cheaply priced, which strongly supports Financials and credit-sensitive sectors. | Personal Savings Rate | Bank credit reflects borrowing while the savings rate reflects how much households hold back from spending, so reading them together shows whether households are leaning on credit or on their own funds. | M2 Money Supply | Bank lending creates money, so credit growth and money-supply growth are closely linked, and reading them together shows whether credit is feeding through into the broader money in the economy. ## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in credit growth being driven by borrower demand (reflecting confidence to spend and invest) or by banks changing their willingness to lend (reflecting credit conditions)? A demand-driven change signals shifting confidence, while a supply-driven change signals shifting bank willingness, and the two carry different implications.

2. Is the growth concentrated in the broad total or in the C&I business-lending slice, and what does the difference say about where the credit cycle is turning? The C&I series tends to turn first, so a divergence between it and the broad total can give an early read on the direction of the cycle.

3. Is credit funding productive activity such as business investment and home purchases, or speculative activity such as bidding up assets that are already inflating? Productive credit supports a durable expansion, while speculative credit late in a boom raises the risk of a bubble.

4. Is the level of credit at a record high and still growing rapidly, which can signal late-cycle excess, or is it growing from a normal base in line with the economy? A high and rapidly rising level is not proof of trouble by itself, but it warrants closer attention to loan quality.

5. Is loan quality holding up, or are delinquencies and defaults beginning to rise even as the credit total keeps growing? Rising defaults beneath a still-growing total can be an early warning that the credit cycle is about to turn down.

6. Are banks tightening or loosening their lending standards, and how does that change in willingness to lend affect credit growth independent of borrower demand? When banks tighten standards, credit growth can fall even when borrowers want to borrow, which signals deteriorating conditions.

7. Is borrowing shifting away from banks toward non-bank lenders such as private credit funds, which would make bank credit understate the true level of lending in the economy? When credit shifts to non-bank lenders, the bank series alone misses part of the picture.

8. Is the credit growth consistent with what credit spreads and other credit indicators are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure may be misleading? Confirmation across credit indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence warrants caution.

9. Are interest rates affecting borrowing decisions, since higher rates raise the cost of credit and can slow lending even when the underlying demand for activity is healthy? Rising rates can suppress credit growth for reasons unrelated to confidence, which affects interpretation.

10. Is the change in credit growth large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the credit cycle, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary factors? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants a sector rotation, since credit growth can fluctuate for temporary reasons.
