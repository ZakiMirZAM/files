# PPI Inflation

## Part 1: Definition

PPI Inflation measures the percentage change in the average price that producers receive for their goods and services, comparing the current month to the same month one year earlier. The unit is a percentage rate. Data is released every month, usually a day or two before the consumer-side CPI release, and covers the prior month. The most cited version is the final demand PPI, which captures prices for goods and services sold for final use rather than for further processing.

The index is constructed from a sample of about 25,000 prices collected each month from approximately 4,000 establishments across all major industries. Industries are included to cover the full producer side of the economy: agriculture, mining, manufacturing, construction, transportation, warehousing, wholesale trade, retail trade, health care services, and other services where producers sell to other businesses, consumers, governments, or for export. Each industry is included because together they represent the supply side of the economy where price pressures originate before they reach the consumer, so the index would miss the upstream signal if any major industry were excluded. Within each industry, individual product prices are weighted by their share of total industry revenue, so higher-revenue products carry more weight. The weighting structure is included this way because larger revenue products have more economic impact when their prices change. The index also breaks out into three stages of processing (crude materials, intermediate goods, and final demand) which allows analysts to track price pressures as they move through the supply chain from raw inputs to finished products. The stage-of-processing structure is included specifically so that investors can see whether upstream pressures are pushing downstream and whether they are being absorbed in margins or passed through to end buyers.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

PPI Inflation is one of the most useful leading indicators for sector rotation because it captures price pressure at the producer level before that pressure reaches consumer prices and forces a central bank response. Producers face input costs first, and when those costs rise faster than producers can pass them through, margin pressure shows up immediately in the producer sector's earnings. When producers can pass through, the inflation flows downstream into CPI and forces a monetary response that affects all sectors. The metric matters specifically for identifying sector winners because it directly reveals which industries have pricing power in the current environment (sellers of inputs whose prices are rising, like Energy, Materials, and parts of Industrials) and which industries face margin compression (consumers of those inputs, like Consumer Discretionary manufacturers, food processors, and Utilities). Historical data shows that sharp PPI moves precede CPI moves by 2 to 6 months, giving investors a leading edge on inflation regime shifts and the sector rotation those shifts produce. The student should think of PPI as the upstream signal that tells you what CPI is about to do, which in turn tells you which sectors will lead.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Above 5% and rising | Producer prices are rising sharply, signaling intense cost pressure throughout the supply chain. The cause is typically commodity price spikes, supply chain disruptions, or strong demand outpacing capacity. Producers with pricing power capture the inflation as revenue, while downstream manufacturers face margin compression as they struggle to pass through costs. CPI will likely follow upward within 3 to 6 months. | Energy, Materials, Industrials | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Utilities, Real Estate | Information Technology, Communication Services, Health Care, Financials |
| 1 to 3% (stable) | Producer prices are growing at a moderate, sustainable rate that suggests balanced supply and demand. No major margin pressure is building, and CPI is likely to track at a similar moderate pace. Sector leadership will not come from inflation signals at this level. | Industrials, Information Technology, Materials | Energy | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities, Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Real Estate, Communication Services |
| Near zero or falling | Producer prices are flat or declining, signaling either weak demand or excess supply at the producer level. Producers face revenue pressure, but consumer-side firms gain margin relief as input costs fall. This typically precedes CPI declines and supports a disinflation regime that benefits long-duration sectors. | Consumer Discretionary, Information Technology, Communication Services, Health Care | Energy, Materials | Consumer Staples, Utilities, Financials, Real Estate, Industrials |
| Negative for 3 or more months | Producer prices are falling for an extended period, indicating broad weakness in demand or oversupply. This often precedes consumer price deflation and signals recessionary or near-recessionary conditions. Defensive sectors lead because their pricing power matters most when general price levels are falling. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Energy, Materials, Industrials, Financials | Information Technology, Communication Services, Real Estate, Consumer Discretionary |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Energy producers are the largest single source of upstream price pressure. Rising PPI in the energy components directly drives Energy sector revenue, while falling PPI in energy directly compresses it. | Buy when PPI energy components are rising. Avoid when PPI energy components are falling. |
| Materials | Materials producers sell the metals, chemicals, and basic inputs whose prices rise in PPI strength. Pricing power flows directly to revenue. | Buy when PPI is rising on industrial inputs. Avoid in PPI deflation. |
| Industrials | Capital goods producers sell equipment and machinery at prices captured in PPI. They benefit when they can raise prices and suffer when input costs rise faster than their selling prices. | Buy in moderate PPI inflation when pricing power is intact. Avoid in extreme PPI inflation when input costs squeeze margins. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Discretionary goods manufacturers consume materials and components whose prices are captured in PPI. Rising PPI compresses their margins, while falling PPI expands margins. | Buy when PPI is falling, providing margin relief. Avoid when PPI is rising sharply. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples manufacturers consume agricultural inputs and packaging materials. Rising PPI in food and intermediate goods squeezes margins, particularly for food producers. | Buy when PPI food components are falling. Avoid when PPI food components are rising sharply. |
| Information Technology | Tech companies have light exposure to physical input costs but benefit from the broader disinflation regime when PPI falls. | Buy when PPI is falling, supporting overall disinflation. Less directly affected than other sectors. |
| Communication Services | Similar to tech, mostly insulated from physical input costs but benefits from disinflation regimes. | Buy when PPI is falling. Mostly insulated from PPI moves. |
| Health Care | Health care service costs are partially captured in PPI but mostly set by reimbursement contracts. Pharmaceutical input costs matter for drug manufacturers. | Defensive default. Less directly affected by PPI than cyclical sectors. |
| Real Estate | Construction costs are captured in PPI and affect new development economics. Rising PPI in construction inputs slows new supply, which can support existing REIT pricing. | Mixed signal. Rising construction PPI hurts homebuilders but helps existing REITs by limiting new supply. |
| Financials | Banks are not direct PPI consumers but are affected through their borrowers. Rising PPI signals inflation that forces tightening, which affects loan demand. | Mostly indirect. Buy when PPI is rising moderately in early tightening cycles, avoid in late tightening. |
| Utilities | Utilities consume fuel inputs whose prices are captured in PPI energy. Rising PPI compresses margins when regulators delay rate increases. | Buy when PPI is falling, providing margin relief. Avoid when PPI fuel components are rising sharply. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside PPI Inflation | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| CPI Inflation | PPI captures producer-side prices that typically flow downstream into consumer prices with a 2 to 6 month lag. When GDP is weak AND PPI is falling, the contraction is real and defensives lead. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the producer price pressure being driven by rising raw material costs (crude materials), rising intermediate goods costs, or rising final goods prices, and what does the stage-of-processing breakdown reveal about how far the inflation has already moved through the supply chain? Pressure at the crude stage that has not yet reached final demand signals that more inflation is coming, while pressure already at the final stage signals that consumer prices will likely follow within months.

2. Is the producer price change concentrated in goods (which are more affected by global commodity moves and supply chains) or in services (which are more affected by domestic wages and demand)? Goods PPI inflation favors Materials and Industrials producers, while services PPI inflation has different implications and tends to be stickier.

3. Are producers passing through cost increases to their customers, or are they absorbing the increases in their margins because competition prevents pass-through? Surveys and earnings calls reveal pass-through behavior, and the answer determines whether margin compression hits the producer (bad for the producer) or the downstream buyer (bad for the buyer).

4. Is the producer price move concentrated in a few specific industries (such as oil refining or steel) or broad-based across most industries? Concentrated PPI strength affects only specific sectors and is less likely to flow into consumer prices broadly, while broad-based PPI strength signals systemic inflation that will affect most sectors.

5. Are the input costs that PPI captures rising because of supply disruptions that will eventually resolve (such as port congestion or geopolitical events), or because of demand strength that will persist as long as growth continues? Supply-driven PPI tends to fade, while demand-driven PPI tends to persist, and the duration affects how long sector tilts should last.

6. How does the change in producer prices compare to the change in producer wages over the same period, and what does this say about producer profit margins? When producer prices rise faster than producer wages, margins expand and earnings improve, supporting producer sector outperformance. When wages rise faster, margins compress and the producer sector underperforms.

7. Are global producer prices (in major trading partners) moving in the same direction as US PPI, or is the US move diverging from the global pattern? Synchronized global PPI movement suggests common cause (commodity prices, global demand) and supports global sector rotation themes, while a US-specific PPI move points to domestic factors like the dollar or fiscal policy.

8. Is the producer price strength being supported by capacity utilization at high levels (which limits new supply and supports continued pricing power) or by capacity at low levels (where additional supply could come on quickly and end the pricing power)? High utilization extends the duration of PPI strength and the favored sector tilt.

9. Are inventories at the producer level rising (signaling that demand is failing to keep up with production, which will eventually pressure prices down) or falling (signaling that demand is outpacing supply, which supports continued price strength)? Inventory dynamics provide early warning of when PPI strength will peak.

10. Is the PPI move being driven by US-specific factors (domestic demand, US dollar moves, US fiscal policy) or by global factors (commodity prices, global trade flows, global supply conditions)? US-specific drivers can be addressed by US policy, while global drivers require waiting for global conditions to change, which affects how long sector rotation tilts should be held.
