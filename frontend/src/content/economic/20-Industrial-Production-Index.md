# Industrial Production Index

## Part 1: Definition

The Industrial Production Index measures the actual physical output of the nation's factories, mines, and utilities, expressed as an index level relative to a base period, with the most useful figure being the percentage change over a month or a year. The data is released monthly and covers the prior month. Unlike survey-based measures that capture sentiment, this index measures real output, making it a hard-data confirmation of how much the industrial economy is actually producing.

The index is constructed by measuring the real output of three major groups and combining them into a single figure weighted by their economic importance. Manufacturing output is included with the largest weight because factories producing goods such as vehicles, machinery, electronics, chemicals, and consumer products represent the bulk of industrial activity and are the most sensitive to the business cycle. Mining output is included because extraction of oil, gas, coal, and metals is a significant part of physical production and reflects demand for raw materials and energy. Utility output is included because the generation of electricity and gas reflects overall economic activity, though it is also affected by weather. Each group's output is measured from physical production data and from inputs such as hours worked and materials consumed where direct output is hard to measure. The components are weighted by their share of total industrial value added so that the largest industries have the most influence. The index is built to capture real physical production rather than dollar values, which means it isolates the volume of goods produced from changes in their prices, making it a clean measure of how hard the industrial economy is working.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Industrial Production Index matters for sector investing because it is the hard-data measure of how much the goods-producing economy is actually making, which directly drives the earnings of cyclical sectors tied to manufacturing, mining, and energy. When industrial production is rising, factories are making more goods, mines are extracting more materials, and the strong output supports Industrials that build equipment, Materials that supply inputs, and Energy that powers production. When industrial production is falling, the goods economy is contracting, which pressures those cyclical sectors and often signals a broader slowdown because industrial activity is a core part of the cycle. The metric matters with particular force for Industrials and Materials because their revenue tracks physical production volumes almost directly, so changes in the index flow quickly to their results. Because it is hard data rather than survey sentiment, the index serves as a confirmation of the signals from forward-looking measures like the manufacturing survey. The combination of a direct link to goods-producing sectors and its role as hard-data confirmation makes the Industrial Production Index a valuable input for cyclical sector positioning.

| Information Technology | Technology hardware is sold into industrial production for automation and equipment, and the broader tech cycle correlates with industrial activity. Rising production supports tech hardware demand, while falling production signals weakening business equipment spending. The sector has meaningful exposure through industrial technology. | Buy when industrial production is rising. Less negative than pure cyclicals in declines due to software resilience. |
| Financials | Industrial expansion drives business investment that requires financing, so rising production supports loan demand from industrial borrowers. | Consumer Staples | Staples demand is unrelated to industrial production, so the sector gains relative appeal when falling production signals a slowdown. | Utilities | Utility output is part of the index, but utility earnings are driven by regulated rates and stable demand rather than the industrial cycle, so the sector behaves defensively when industrial production signals a slowdown. | Consumer Discretionary | Discretionary demand is driven by household income rather than industrial production, so the sector has limited direct exposure to the index. | Real Estate | Real estate demand is driven by interest rates and property fundamentals rather than industrial production, so the sector has little direct exposure, aside from industrial property. The connection is weak except for the warehouse and logistics segment. The index does not broadly drive real estate. | Low sensitivity, except industrial property which follows production. |
| Communication Services | The sector earns from advertising and subscriptions unrelated to industrial production, so it has minimal direct exposure to the index. The connection runs only through the broad economy. The index does not meaningfully drive positioning. | Not meaningfully affected by industrial production. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Which of the three components (manufacturing, mining, or utilities) is driving the change in industrial production, and what does the source say about which part of the goods economy is strengthening or weakening? Manufacturing-driven changes reflect the core goods economy, mining-driven changes reflect raw material demand, and utility-driven changes can be distorted by weather.

2. Within manufacturing, is the change concentrated in durable goods (such as vehicles and machinery, which are highly cyclical) or in nondurable goods (such as food and chemicals, which are more stable)? Durable goods strength confirms genuine cyclical expansion, while nondurable strength carries a weaker cyclical signal.

3. Is the utility component distorting the headline because of unusually hot or cold weather that boosted or depressed power generation independent of the underlying economy? Because utility output responds to weather, a weather-driven swing can make the headline misleading about the true industrial trend.

4. Is the change in industrial production being driven by domestic demand or by export demand, and what does that say about whether the strength or weakness is internal or tied to global conditions? Domestic-driven production reflects internal economic health, while export-driven production reflects global demand that affects multinational industrials.

5. Is auto production, which is a large and volatile component, driving the change, and is the move in autos reflecting genuine demand or temporary factors such as supply shortages or plant retooling? Because auto production swings can dominate the index, distinguishing genuine auto demand from temporary disruptions matters for interpreting the signal.

6. Is the industrial production trend consistent with the forward-looking manufacturing survey, or is there a gap between what purchasing managers expect and what factories are actually producing? When the hard data and the survey diverge, the hard production data ultimately determines sector earnings.

7. Are inventories building up as production rises (signaling that goods are not selling and a pullback may be coming) or being drawn down (signaling that demand is outpacing production)? The relationship between production and inventories reveals whether rising output reflects genuine demand or is creating an inventory overhang.

8. Is the change in production large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the industrial cycle, or is it a one-month fluctuation that could reverse? A sustained trend is a more reliable signal for cyclical sector rotation than a single volatile reading.

9. How does the level of industrial production compare to its prior peak, and is the economy still recovering lost ground or pushing into new territory? The position relative to the prior peak indicates whether the industrial economy is in early recovery with room to run or in late expansion near its limits.

10. Is the industrial production trend confirming or contradicting the signals from other cyclical indicators such as freight, capacity utilization, and the manufacturing survey, and what does the balance of evidence say about the cycle stage? When the hard production data confirms other cyclical indicators, the signal for cyclical sectors is strong, while a contradiction requires weighing the indicators by their reliability.
