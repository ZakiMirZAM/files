# S&P 500 Expected Return (Building Blocks)

## Part 1: Definition

The S&P 500 Expected Return (Building Blocks) estimates the yearly return the index offers an investor who buys at today's price and holds for ten years. Instead of one model output, the estimate is the sum of four parts that can each be observed today. The result is a percentage per year, and the figure updates whenever the index price or the earnings data or the bond market inputs change.

`Expected return = dividend yield + real earnings growth + expected inflation + valuation change`

The metric is constructed by adding the four parts. The dividend yield is the latest monthly index dividend divided by the index level, and the term is included because dividends are cash paid to the owner regardless of where prices go. Real earnings growth is the trailing ten-year growth rate of the index's inflation-adjusted earnings per share, and the term is included because owners of the index ultimately earn what its companies earn. Per-share earnings already contain the effect of past share buybacks, meaning companies repurchasing their own shares, so no separate buyback term is added because adding one would count the same cash twice. Expected inflation is the ten-year breakeven rate, which is the yield gap between ordinary ten-year Treasuries and inflation-protected Treasuries. The inflation term is included because the first two parts are measured in inflation-adjusted terms while an investor's actual return is not.

The valuation change term asks what happens if the market's price level normalizes. The CAPE ratio is the index price divided by the average of the last ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. The app computes the yearly price drift that would occur if the CAPE returned to its thirty-year median over ten years, and the term is capped at plus or minus four points a year so an extreme starting valuation cannot dominate the sum. A second, more conservative reading anchors the same calculation on the full-history median CAPE. When any term is missing the app shows the sum as partial rather than substituting a guess.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The expected-return reading matters for sector selection because it states how much the market is paying investors to take equity risk from today's price, and the size of that payment decides whether cyclical or defensive sectors should lead. A high reading usually appears when the market is cheap against its own earnings history, which tends to happen after price declines and near the start of a new cycle. Cheap markets reward risk-taking. The early-cycle groups recover first: Consumer Discretionary, Financials, and Industrials. Smaller companies across every sector share in that recovery. A low reading usually appears when the market is expensive and the valuation term is deeply negative, which means the price itself has become a drag on the next ten years. Expensive markets favor the defensive trio of Consumer Staples, Health Care, and Utilities. Quality companies with steady earnings also hold up, because their returns depend least on the market staying generous. Long-duration growth sectors are the most exposed to the valuation drag, since Information Technology and Communication Services hold their value in distant cash flows that a falling price multiple reprices hardest. The building blocks add something a single price ratio cannot: the composition of the reading. A reading lifted by the expected inflation term points toward Energy and Materials because those sectors pass inflation through to revenue, while a reading lifted by the valuation term points toward the classic early-cycle cyclicals. Because every block is observable, the investor can see why the market is attractive or unattractive rather than trusting a single summary number.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the expected return | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Shiller CAPE | The valuation block is computed from the CAPE's distance to its median, so the CAPE is the raw material behind the most volatile of the four building blocks. Reading the CAPE directly shows how far the index sits from the thirty-year anchor and from the stricter full-history anchor. The gap between the two anchors reveals how much the conclusion depends on which history is trusted. | When the reading is low AND the CAPE sits far above both anchors, the overvaluation is confirmed on both histories. The defensive tilt toward Consumer Staples, Health Care, and Utilities is then strongest. When the reading is high AND the CAPE sits below its anchors, the cheapness is confirmed. Early-cycle sectors (Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Industrials) deserve the added weight. When the two anchors disagree, size sector tilts to the conservative reading rather than the generous one. |
| Equity Risk Premium | The expected return minus the risk-free rate is the reward for holding stocks instead of bonds, and the implied equity risk premium measures that same reward from a discounted cash flow model. Reading the two together cross-checks the building-blocks sum against an independent method. Agreement between the two makes the sector signal far more trustworthy. | When both show generous readings, stocks are cheap against bonds and cyclical sectors plus Financials deserve the overweight. When both are thin, equities compete poorly with bonds. The defensive trio of Consumer Staples, Health Care, and Utilities takes leadership. When the two disagree, inspect the inputs before rotating, because one of the models is being distorted by an unusual input. |
| 10-Year Treasury Yield | The ten-year Treasury yield is the safe alternative the expected return must beat, and the breakeven inflation block is itself derived from the Treasury market. A given expected return means something different when bonds pay little than when bonds pay well. The comparison converts the reading from an absolute number into a choice between assets. | When the reading is high AND the ten-year yield is low, the equity advantage is wide. Risk-on sectors such as Consumer Discretionary, Financials, and Industrials are favored. When the reading is low AND the yield is high, bonds compete directly with stocks and the rate pressure also hits Utilities and Real Estate, which leaves short-duration value and cash-rich quality within Health Care and Consumer Staples as the best equity shelter. When both are low, the whole opportunity set is poor and defensive positioning matters more than sector rotation. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Which of the four building blocks contributes the most to the current reading, and does the sector tilt being considered match that driver? A reading lifted by expected inflation argues for Energy and Materials, while a reading lifted by the valuation term argues for the early-cycle cyclicals, so the same headline number can support different rotations.

2. Is the trailing ten-year real earnings growth being measured from a depressed starting year or an elevated one, and how much does that base change the growth block? A window that starts in a recession inflates the growth rate, while a window that starts at a profit peak suppresses it, and neither says much about the next decade.

3. Are current index profit margins far above their own long-run history, and would a return of margins to normal cut the earnings that both the growth block and the CAPE depend on? Growth built on peak margins is the least durable input in the sum.

4. Does the thirty-year median CAPE reflect a market that is structurally comparable to today's, given changes in interest rates and in the index's sector mix and in accounting standards? An anchor drawn from a different kind of market can make the reversion assumption too harsh or too generous.

5. Is the valuation term sitting at its cap of plus or minus four points a year, and how much larger would the drift be without the cap? A capped term means the underlying valuation gap is more extreme than the headline sum reveals.

6. Is the ten-year breakeven rate a clean measure of expected inflation, or is the gap between ordinary and inflation-protected Treasuries being distorted by liquidity conditions in the bond market? A distorted inflation block changes the reading without any change in the real outlook.

7. How far apart are the baseline reading and the conservative full-history reading, and what does that gap say about how sensitive the conclusion is to the anchor choice? A conclusion that survives both anchors deserves more weight than one that depends on the friendlier anchor.

8. Has the dividend yield block declined because companies shifted their payout from dividends toward buybacks, and is that shift showing up instead as faster per-share earnings growth? The split between the two blocks can change while the total return on offer does not.

9. Is any building block currently missing so that the sum shows as partial, and does the conclusion under consideration survive without the missing term? A partial sum should lower conviction rather than being read as if it were complete.

10. How does the completed reading compare with the ten-year Treasury yield, and is the extra return over the safe asset large enough to justify equity risk at this point in the cycle? A reading only slightly above the bond yield argues for defensive sectors even when the absolute number looks respectable.
