# Personal Savings Rate

## Part 1: Definition

The Personal Savings Rate measures the share of after-tax income that households save rather than spend, expressed as a percentage. It is calculated by taking total household income after taxes, subtracting everything households spend on goods, services, and interest, and expressing what remains as a percentage of after-tax income. The data is released monthly as part of the personal income and spending report. The rate reveals how much of a financial cushion households are building or drawing down, which signals both their financial health and their willingness to spend.

The rate is constructed by subtracting household spending from household after-tax income and dividing the remainder by after-tax income. After-tax income is included as the base because it represents the money households actually have available to either spend or save after the government takes its share. Spending on goods and services is subtracted because it represents the income that is consumed rather than saved. Interest payments on debt are subtracted because servicing debt uses income that cannot be saved. What remains is included as saving because it represents the portion of income that households set aside, whether in bank accounts, investments, or debt paydown. The rate is included as an economic indicator because it reveals the balance households are striking between spending and saving, which signals their confidence and financial cushion. A rising savings rate means households are holding back spending to build a cushion, often out of caution, while a falling rate means households are spending more of their income, either from confidence or from necessity when income is squeezed.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Personal Savings Rate matters for sector investing because it reveals the balance households are striking between spending and saving, which signals both the sustainability of current consumer spending and the direction of future spending that drives consumer-facing sectors. When the savings rate is low, households are spending most of their income, which supports current consumer spending and benefits Consumer Discretionary, but it also means households have little cushion and limited room to increase spending further. When the savings rate is high, households are holding back spending to build a cushion, which constrains current consumer demand but creates pent-up spending power that can be released later. The metric matters because it signals the sustainability of consumer spending, since spending funded by a falling savings rate cannot continue indefinitely once the cushion is depleted. The savings rate also reflects consumer confidence, since cautious households save more while confident households spend more. The combination of a sustainability signal for consumer spending and a read on consumer confidence makes the savings rate a useful supporting input for judging the durability of consumer-facing sector strength.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the savings rate falling because households are confidently spending more out of rising income, or because rising prices are forcing them to save less just to maintain their standard of living? A confidence-driven decline supports discretionary spending, while a squeeze-driven decline signals stress that warns of a coming pullback.

2. Is the savings rate rising because households are growing cautious about the economic outlook, or because of temporary factors such as a one-time income boost that has not yet been spent? Caution-driven saving signals weakening consumer demand, while temporary saving may reverse and support future spending.

3. Are households building up excess savings that create pent-up spending power for the future, or depleting savings that will eventually force them to cut back? The direction of the savings cushion determines whether future spending is likely to accelerate or pull back.

4. Is the savings behavior concentrated among higher-income households, who hold most of the savings, or are lower-income households also changing their behavior in ways that affect necessity and discretionary spending differently? The distribution of saving across income groups affects which consumer sectors are influenced by the change.

5. Is the current savings rate high or low relative to its historical range, and what does that comparison say about whether households have room to spend more or need to rebuild cushions? The rate relative to history reveals whether the spend-versus-save balance is stretched in either direction.

6. Are households servicing rising debt burdens that consume income and constrain both spending and saving, and how does the debt-service burden affect their financial flexibility? When debt service consumes a large share of income, households have less flexibility to spend, which affects consumer sector demand.

7. Is the change in the savings rate consistent with what consumer sentiment and retail sales are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure may be misleading? Confirmation across consumer indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence warrants caution.

8. Is the savings rate being distorted by one-time events such as stimulus payments, tax changes, or timing effects that temporarily boost or depress measured saving? One-time factors can move the rate for reasons unrelated to underlying consumer behavior, so identifying them is essential.

9. How does the savings behavior relate to the stage of the business cycle, given that households tend to save more in uncertain late-cycle and recessionary periods and less in confident expansion phases? The savings rate's relationship to the cycle stage helps confirm the broader economic read.

10. Is the change in the savings rate large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect the sustainability of consumer spending, or is it a short-term fluctuation that does not change the underlying consumer picture? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants adjusting the read on consumer-facing sectors, since the rate can fluctuate month to month for temporary reasons.
