# Earnings Yield vs 1-Year Treasury

## Part 1: Definition

This metric compares the earnings yield of the S&P 500 to the yield available on the 1-year US Treasury bond. The earnings yield is calculated by dividing the trailing earnings of the index by the current index price, which is the inverse of the price-to-earnings ratio, expressed as a percentage. It represents how much in earnings an investor receives for each dollar paid for the index. The 1-year Treasury yield is the return available from lending to the US government for one year, also expressed as a percentage, and it stands in for the safe, cash-like alternative to holding stocks. The comparison between the two is often called a Fed model comparison. The data updates as index prices and Treasury yields move.

The comparison is constructed from two figures and one calculation. The earnings yield is included because it expresses corporate earnings as a percentage of the index price, which makes it directly comparable to a bond yield, and because it stands for the return investors receive from company profits relative to what they pay. The 1-year Treasury yield is included because it represents the near-riskless short-term return that competes with stocks for investor capital. The spread, defined as the earnings yield minus the Treasury yield, is the key calculation because it reveals the extra yield that stocks offer over safe short-term bonds, which is a gauge of how cheap or expensive equities are relative to cash-like bonds. A positive spread means stocks offer a higher earnings yield than the Treasury, which signals that equities are reasonably or attractively priced. A negative spread means the Treasury out-yields stocks, which signals that equities are richly priced relative to the safe alternative. This comparison carries a well-known limitation. It sets a real or earnings yield, which already reflects the eroding effect of inflation on prices and profits, against a nominal bond yield, which includes compensation for expected inflation. Because of this mismatch, the comparison can mislead in high-inflation periods, when the nominal Treasury yield rises for inflation reasons rather than for real-value reasons.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

This comparison matters for sector investing because it reveals the relative attractiveness of stocks versus safe short-term bonds, which affects whether capital flows into equities at all and which types of stocks benefit most from the prevailing rate environment. When the earnings yield sits well above the 1-year Treasury yield, stocks are cheap relative to cash-like bonds and the environment supports equities broadly, with higher-risk cyclical and growth sectors often benefiting most as investors reach for return. When the Treasury yield rises toward or above the earnings yield, safe bonds become competitive, which pressures the most expensive long-duration sectors first because their high valuations depend on low short-term rates to justify them. The metric matters with particular force for long-duration growth sectors like Information Technology and for bond-substitute sectors like Utilities and Real Estate, because both compete most directly with the safe bond alternative. A high spread paired with low short-term rates favors the long-duration growth sectors whose value sits far in the future, while a negative spread paired with high short-term rates favors short-duration value sectors and defensives whose cash flows arrive sooner. The combination of a relative-value signal between asset classes and a read on which rate-sensitive sectors face the most pressure makes this comparison a useful input for valuation-aware sector positioning.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the earnings yield based on trailing earnings that have already been reported, or on forward earnings estimates that may prove too optimistic, and how does the choice affect the reliability of the spread? Forward estimates can be too high near cycle peaks, which would make the earnings yield look larger than it really is and make the spread appear wider than warranted.

2. Are the corporate earnings used in the earnings yield sustainable, or are they inflated by peak-cycle profit margins that are likely to revert, which would shrink the real spread? When earnings reflect unsustainable peak margins, the apparent spread overstates how attractive stocks are relative to the Treasury.

3. Does the level of inflation make this nominal comparison misleading, given that the earnings yield is a real concept while the 1-year Treasury yield is a nominal one that rises with expected inflation? In high-inflation periods, a negative spread can reflect inflation compensation in the Treasury yield rather than genuine equity overvaluation, so the comparison must be read with inflation in mind.

4. Is the spread compressing because stock prices have risen, making the earnings yield fall, or because the 1-year Treasury yield has risen, making safe bonds more competitive, given that the two have different implications for which sectors are most at risk? Price-driven compression signals broad overvaluation, while rate-driven compression pressures the most rate-sensitive long-duration sectors first.

5. How does the current spread compare to its own historical range, and is it wide or narrow by historical standards in a way that signals stocks are cheap or expensive relative to short-term bonds? The spread relative to its history is what matters, since a spread that looks adequate in absolute terms may be narrow compared to past norms.

6. Is the 1-year Treasury yield unusually high or low because of where the central bank has set short-term policy rates, and does that policy stance make the spread a temporary signal rather than a lasting one? When short-term rates are set far above or below normal for policy reasons, the spread can swing sharply without any change in underlying equity value.

7. Is the spread being distorted by a small number of very large companies whose high valuations pull down the overall index earnings yield, masking better value in the rest of the market? When mega-cap valuations dominate the index, the headline earnings yield may misrepresent the value available in most sectors.

8. Is the negative or narrow spread occurring while the yield curve is inverted, with the 1-year Treasury yielding more than longer-dated bonds, and what does that say about the durability of the front-end rate pressure? An inverted curve often signals that short-term rates are expected to fall, which means the spread pressure on long-duration sectors may ease as the front end normalizes.

9. Are investors likely to shift capital between stocks and short-term bonds in response to the changing spread, and which sectors would see the most capital flow out if cash becomes more attractive? The most expensive long-duration and bond-substitute sectors are most exposed to capital flowing toward safe bonds when the spread turns negative.

10. Is the change in the spread large enough and sustained enough to warrant a meaningful sector rotation, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary moves in stock prices or short-term rates? Only a sustained and meaningful change in the relationship warrants repositioning, since both stock prices and the 1-year Treasury yield can move sharply over short periods.
