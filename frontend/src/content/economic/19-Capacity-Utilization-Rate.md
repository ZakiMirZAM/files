# Capacity Utilization Rate

## Part 1: Definition

The Capacity Utilization Rate measures how much of the economy's total production capacity is actually being used, expressed as a percentage of the maximum sustainable output that factories, mines, and utilities could produce. A reading of 80% means the nation's industrial facilities are operating at 80% of their full capacity. The data is released monthly alongside industrial production figures and covers the prior month. The rate is most useful for what it reveals about whether the economy has room to grow without straining its productive capacity or whether it is running hot enough to generate inflation.

The rate is constructed by comparing actual industrial output to an estimate of the maximum output that the existing capital stock could sustainably produce. Manufacturing capacity is included because factories represent the largest part of the nation's industrial production and their utilization reveals how hard the goods-producing economy is working. Mining capacity is included because extraction industries such as oil, gas, and metals are capital-intensive and their utilization reflects demand for raw materials. Utility capacity is included because power generation utilization reflects overall economic activity and energy demand. The actual output for each is measured from production data, and the capacity estimate is built from surveys of what facilities could produce when fully staffed and running normal shifts. The rate is included as an economic indicator because it reveals the gap between current production and potential production, which signals whether there is slack in the economy that allows growth without inflation, or whether the economy is near its limits where further demand pushes up prices rather than output.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Capacity Utilization Rate matters for sector investing because it reveals how close the economy is to its production limits, which signals both the room for further cyclical growth and the risk of rising inflation that affects sector leadership. When utilization is low, there is slack in the economy, meaning factories can produce more without straining capacity, which supports continued expansion and benefits cyclical sectors without triggering inflation. When utilization is high, the economy is near its limits, and further demand tends to push up prices and input costs rather than output, which signals late-cycle conditions that favor inflation-benefiting sectors and warn of an approaching peak. The metric matters with particular force for Industrials and Materials because high utilization signals strong demand for their products and pricing power, while it also prompts businesses to invest in new capacity, which drives capital goods orders. Capacity utilization also feeds into inflation analysis, since a high rate signals that the economy cannot easily meet additional demand. The combination of a read on remaining growth room and an inflation signal makes capacity utilization a useful input for judging the cycle stage and positioning between early-cycle and late-cycle sectors.

| CPI Inflation | High capacity utilization signals that the economy is near its production limits where additional demand pushes up prices, so reading utilization alongside inflation confirms whether capacity constraints are feeding inflation. ## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in capacity utilization being driven by a change in actual production (reflecting demand) or by a change in the estimated capacity itself (reflecting investment in new capacity or retirement of old capacity)? A utilization change driven by demand carries a different signal than one driven by shifting capacity, since the latter can make the economy look tighter or looser without any change in underlying demand.

2. Is utilization high enough that the economy is straining its production limits in a way that will push up prices and input costs, or is there still slack that allows demand to be met without inflation? The level relative to the inflation-triggering threshold determines whether high utilization is a healthy growth signal or an inflation warning.

3. Which industries are running tight and which have slack, given that aggregate utilization can mask wide differences between sectors that are at their limits and sectors with excess capacity? Industry-level differences reveal which specific sectors have pricing power and which face competition from idle capacity.

4. Are businesses responding to high utilization by investing in new capacity, which would support capital goods orders and eventually relieve the tightness, or are they holding back on investment despite the high utilization? The investment response determines whether high utilization translates into capital goods demand and whether the tightness will persist or ease.

5. Is the high or low utilization consistent with what the manufacturing survey and industrial production data are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests the utilization signal may be misleading? Confirmation across industrial indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence warrants caution.

6. Has the estimated level of full capacity shifted because of structural changes such as factory closures, offshoring, or new technology, in a way that changes what a given utilization rate means? A structural change in capacity can make the same utilization rate signal something different than it did historically, which affects interpretation.

7. Is the utilization rate near the level that has historically coincided with the late stage of the business cycle and the onset of inflation pressure, and what does that say about where the economy sits in the cycle? Because high utilization has historically marked late-cycle conditions, its level helps place the economy in the cycle and time the rotation toward late-cycle sectors.

8. Is the change in utilization being driven by the manufacturing component, the mining component, or the utility component, and what does the source say about which part of the industrial economy is strengthening or weakening? The component driving the change reveals whether goods production, resource extraction, or energy demand is the source of the signal.

9. Is utilization rising from a low base in an early recovery (a bullish signal for cyclicals with room to run) or already high and possibly peaking (a late-cycle signal that warns of an approaching slowdown)? The direction combined with the level determines whether high utilization is an opportunity for cyclicals or a warning to rotate defensive.

10. Is the utilization trend large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect producer pricing power and capacity investment decisions, or is it a short-term fluctuation that does not change the underlying picture? Only a sustained and meaningful trend warrants a sector rotation, since utilization can fluctuate month to month for temporary reasons.
