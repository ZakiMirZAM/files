# 30-Year Mortgage Rate Trend

## Part 1: Definition

The 30-Year Mortgage Rate Trend tracks the direction of the interest rate on the most common type of home loan in the United States, a fixed-rate mortgage that the borrower repays over thirty years. The rate is expressed as an annual percentage, and the trend is most useful as the direction and pace of change over weeks and months. The data is published weekly based on surveys of mortgage lenders. The rate determines the monthly cost of buying a home with borrowed money, which makes it the single most important factor in housing affordability and a key driver of the housing market.

The rate is constructed from the yields that lenders charge on thirty-year fixed mortgages, which are built on top of longer-term government bond yields plus an additional spread. The base of long-term government bond yields is included because mortgage rates move with the broader long-term interest rate environment that reflects growth and inflation expectations. The additional spread that lenders add is included because it compensates them for the risk that borrowers may prepay or default and for the cost of servicing the loan, so the mortgage rate sits above the government bond yield by this margin. The thirty-year fixed structure is included because it is the dominant mortgage type in the United States, where borrowers lock in a fixed rate for the life of the loan, making this rate the benchmark for housing affordability. The rate is included as an indicator because it directly determines the monthly payment on a home purchase, which controls how much house buyers can afford and therefore drives housing demand, construction, and the broader housing-related economy.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The 30-Year Mortgage Rate Trend matters for sector investing because it is the single most important driver of housing affordability, which directly determines the health of the housing market and the many sectors tied to it. When mortgage rates are falling, housing becomes more affordable, which boosts home buying and construction and benefits homebuilders within Consumer Discretionary, building products within Materials, home improvement retailers, mortgage lenders within Financials, and real estate. When mortgage rates are rising, housing affordability deteriorates, which slows home buying and construction and pressures those same housing-related sectors. The metric matters with particular force for the housing-linked sectors because their revenue depends directly on the pace of home buying and building that mortgage rates control. The mortgage rate also reflects the broader long-term interest rate environment, connecting it to other rate-sensitive sectors. Because housing is a large and rate-sensitive part of the economy that often leads the broader cycle, the mortgage rate trend serves as both a direct driver of housing sectors and an early signal about the broader economy, making it a valuable input for positioning housing-exposed and rate-sensitive sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the mortgage rate moving because of changes in the underlying long-term government bond yield, or because of changes in the spread that lenders add on top, given that each reflects a different driver? A rate move driven by government yields reflects the broad rate environment, while one driven by the lender spread reflects mortgage-market-specific conditions such as risk perception or lender capacity.

2. Is the change in mortgage rates large enough to meaningfully affect housing affordability and monthly payments, since the impact on home buying depends on how much the monthly cost changes for a typical buyer? A small rate change may not move affordability enough to matter, while a large change can sharply alter what buyers can afford.

3. Are existing homeowners locked into low mortgage rates that discourage them from selling and moving, which restricts the supply of existing homes and shifts demand toward new construction? This lock-in effect can support new homebuilders even when overall housing activity is constrained by high rates.

4. Is the change in mortgage rates being passed through to actual home buying activity, or are buyers and sellers adjusting in ways that offset the rate change such as price negotiations or rate buydowns? When the market adjusts through prices or incentives, the full impact of a rate change on affordability may be muted.

5. How affordable is housing overall at the current combination of mortgage rates and home prices, since the rate matters most in the context of price levels and household income? A given mortgage rate has a very different effect on affordability depending on whether home prices are high or low relative to income.

6. Is mortgage credit flowing freely to qualified buyers, or have lenders tightened their standards in a way that restricts home buying independent of the rate level? Tight credit can choke off home buying even when rates are reasonable, which affects housing sectors beyond the rate itself.

7. Is the mortgage rate trend consistent with what the broader long-term interest rate environment is doing, or is the mortgage spread widening or narrowing in a way that signals stress in mortgage markets? A widening mortgage spread can signal stress in the mortgage market that affects housing beyond the broad rate move.

8. Are mortgage applications for home purchases rising or falling in response to the rate change, since application data provides an early read on how buyers are responding before sales close? Purchase applications lead actual home sales, so they reveal how buyers are reacting to the rate trend in real time.

9. Is the mortgage rate trend leading the broader economic cycle the way housing historically has, turning the housing market before the broader economy turns? Because housing has historically led the cycle, the mortgage rate's effect on housing provides an early read on where the broader economy and cyclical sectors are heading.

10. Is the change in mortgage rates large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in housing affordability and the outlook for housing-related sectors, or is it a short-term fluctuation that buyers and builders can look through? Only a sustained and meaningful rate change warrants a sector rotation, since brief rate fluctuations may not change the housing outlook enough to move housing-related earnings.
