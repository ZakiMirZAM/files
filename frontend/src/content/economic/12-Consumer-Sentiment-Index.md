# Consumer Sentiment Index

## Part 1: Definition

The Consumer Sentiment Index measures how optimistic or pessimistic households feel about their own finances and the broader economy. It is reported as an index number relative to a base period rather than a percentage, with higher numbers meaning more optimism and lower numbers meaning more pessimism. The data comes from monthly surveys of households, with a preliminary reading mid-month and a final reading at month end. Two widely followed versions exist, one focused more on current conditions and one focused more on expectations, and both are reported as index levels.

The index is constructed from survey responses to a set of questions asked to a representative sample of households each month. Questions about current personal finances are included because how households feel about their present financial situation directly shapes their willingness to spend right now rather than save. Questions about expected future finances are included because spending decisions on large purchases depend on whether households expect their situation to improve or worsen. Questions about expected business conditions over the coming year and the coming five years are included because households' views on the broader economy affect their confidence to make commitments such as buying homes and cars. Questions about whether now is a good time to buy major household items are included because they directly capture purchasing intent for big-ticket discretionary goods. The responses are combined into a single index, with the components weighted to reflect their historical relationship to actual spending. The index is built this way because consumer spending is the largest part of the economy, and how households feel often shapes how they spend before the spending actually shows up in hard data.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Consumer Sentiment Index matters for sector investing because it is a forward-looking gauge of household willingness to spend, and since consumer spending drives the majority of economic activity, sentiment often signals shifts in consumer-facing sector revenue before the hard sales data confirms them. When sentiment is high and rising, households are confident enough to make large discretionary purchases such as vehicles, vacations, and home furnishings, which lifts Consumer Discretionary, Communication Services, and parts of Real Estate. When sentiment is low and falling, households delay big purchases and increase precautionary saving, which pressures discretionary sectors while supporting defensive consumer sectors that sell necessities. The index matters with particular force for Consumer Discretionary because the survey directly asks whether now is a good time to buy major items, making it a near-direct read on demand for big-ticket discretionary goods. Sentiment also tends to lead actual spending, which gives investors an early signal for rotating between discretionary and defensive positioning. The combination of a forward-looking signal and a direct link to discretionary purchase intent makes the Consumer Sentiment Index a useful input, though it must be weighed against the fact that sentiment and actual spending sometimes diverge.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

|---|---|---|
| Retail Sales YoY | Sentiment measures how households feel while retail sales measure what they actually spend, so reading them together reveals whether sentiment is translating into real spending or whether the two are diverging. Sentiment and spending sometimes move apart, and the hard sales data confirms or corrects the sentiment signal. This validates the forward-looking sentiment reading against actual behavior. | When sentiment is rising AND retail sales are growing, the optimism is real and translating into spending, which supports Consumer Discretionary with confidence. When sentiment is falling but retail sales are still growing, households are spending despite their stated pessimism, which suggests the discretionary pullback may be delayed and argues against a premature rotation into defensives. |
| Wages vs CPI | Real purchasing power is one of the main drivers of how households feel, so reading sentiment alongside real wages shows whether the confidence has a foundation in actual income gains. Rising real wages support sustainable confidence, while sentiment that rises without real wage support may be fragile. This connects the feeling to the financial reality behind it. | When sentiment is high AND real wages are positive, the confidence is grounded in genuine purchasing power and discretionary spending is sustainable, supporting Consumer Discretionary. When sentiment is high but real wages are negative, the confidence may be driven by other factors such as asset prices and could reverse quickly if those fade, which argues for caution on discretionary positioning. |
| Employment | Job security is one of the strongest drivers of consumer confidence, so reading sentiment alongside employment shows whether the confidence reflects a solid labor market. Strong employment supports durable confidence, while weakening employment undermines it. This connects sentiment to the labor market foundation. | When sentiment is high AND employment is strong, the confidence rests on job security and discretionary demand is well-supported. When sentiment is high but employment is weakening, the confidence is likely to fade as job worries spread, which gives an early signal to begin rotating from discretionary sectors toward defensives. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in sentiment being driven by the current conditions component (how households feel about their finances right now) or by the expectations component (how they feel about the future), and which one has historically been a better predictor of actual spending? The expectations component tends to lead spending changes, so a move there carries a stronger forward signal than a move in current conditions.

2. Is the sentiment change being driven by households' views on their personal finances or by their views on the broader national economy, given that personal finances tend to drive their own spending more directly than abstract views about the country? Personal financial sentiment connects more directly to household spending behavior than general economic pessimism.

3. Is the sentiment reading being distorted by political partisanship, where households' stated views about the economy reflect their political alignment rather than their actual financial situation and spending plans? Partisan distortion has grown over time and can cause the headline sentiment number to misrepresent actual spending intent.

4. Does the sentiment trend align with what households are actually doing in the hard spending data, or is there a divergence where households say one thing but spend differently? Sentiment and behavior diverge often enough that the hard data must be checked before acting on a sentiment signal alone.

5. Is the sentiment change being driven primarily by inflation worries, by job security worries, by asset price movements such as stocks and home values, or by specific events in the news? Each driver has different implications, with inflation-driven pessimism affecting trade-down behavior and job-security pessimism affecting big-ticket purchases most.

6. Are the survey questions about buying conditions for major household items showing the same direction as the overall index, given that those questions most directly capture demand for the big-ticket discretionary goods that matter most for sector rotation? The buying-conditions component is the most actionable part of the survey for discretionary sector decisions.

7. Is the current sentiment level so depressed that it may mark a contrarian bottom where conditions are about to improve, or so elevated that it may mark excessive optimism that precedes a pullback? Sentiment extremes have historically coincided with turning points, so the level relative to history matters for judging whether to fade or follow the signal.

8. Is the sentiment trend consistent across income groups, or are higher-income and lower-income households diverging in how they feel, which would affect whether premium or value-oriented consumer sectors benefit? Divergence across income groups points to different winners within the consumer space.

9. How quickly does the current sentiment reading translate into actual spending based on the historical lead time, and is that relationship still holding in the current cycle? Understanding the lag between sentiment and spending helps time the sector rotation rather than acting too early or too late.

10. Is the sentiment change supported by underlying fundamentals such as employment and real wages, or is it a mood shift driven by news and headlines that may not persist? Sentiment grounded in fundamentals is more durable and actionable than a temporary mood swing that can reverse without affecting actual spending.
