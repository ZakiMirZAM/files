# Net Liquidity

## Part 1: Definition

Net Liquidity measures the amount of central bank liquidity actually reaching the banking system after the two largest drains on reserves are removed. It is expressed in dollars, usually trillions, and the data is reported weekly and read here at a monthly frequency. It is constructed by taking the Federal Reserve's total assets and subtracting the Treasury General Account, which is the government's cash balance held at the central bank, and then subtracting overnight reverse repurchase agreements, which is cash that money market funds and others park back at the central bank overnight. What remains is an estimate of the reserves available to the banking system to support lending and asset prices.

The measure is constructed by combining three official series into a single net figure. The Federal Reserve balance sheet is included because it is the gross supply of liquidity the central bank has created through asset purchases, and it expands under quantitative easing and contracts under quantitative tightening. The Treasury General Account is subtracted because when the government builds up cash in its account it pulls reserves out of the banking system, so a rising balance drains liquidity even when the central bank's balance sheet is unchanged. Overnight reverse repos are subtracted because cash parked there is money removed from circulation and held at the central bank rather than supporting the banking system, so a rising reverse repo balance drains liquidity while a falling balance returns it. The three are combined because the gross balance sheet alone can be misleading, since large swings in the government's cash balance or in reverse repos can add or drain liquidity independent of policy. The metric is included as an indicator because this net figure has tracked risk-asset conditions more closely than the gross balance sheet, which makes it a cleaner read on whether the liquidity backdrop is a tailwind or a headwind for markets.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Net Liquidity matters for sector investing because the liquidity available to the banking system influences asset prices and financial conditions, and the sectors most sensitive to liquidity are the same long-duration and rate-sensitive sectors that lead when conditions ease and lag when they tighten. When net liquidity is rising, abundant reserves support risk appetite, compress risk premia, and lift the valuations of liquidity-sensitive sectors like Information Technology and Real Estate. When net liquidity is falling, draining reserves tighten conditions, widen risk premia, and pressure the same sectors while favoring defensives. The metric matters because it captures liquidity swings that the headline balance sheet misses, since a stable balance sheet can still coincide with a sharp liquidity drain when the government builds cash or when reverse repos rise. For sector investing this is a shorter-horizon, more technical indicator that informs the risk-on or risk-off backdrop, and it is most relevant during periods when large swings in the Treasury account or reverse repos move banking system reserves meaningfully, such as around debt ceiling episodes or shifts in money market flows.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is a change in net liquidity being driven by the central bank balance sheet, by the Treasury General Account, or by reverse repos, given that each component has different implications for how durable the liquidity swing is? A policy-driven change through the balance sheet tends to be more persistent, while a change driven by the Treasury account or reverse repos can reverse quickly.

2. Is net liquidity moving in the same direction as the headline balance sheet, or is it diverging because a drain from the Treasury account or reverse repos is offsetting policy? A divergence matters because the headline balance sheet can look stable while net liquidity is tightening, which misleads a market watching only the gross figure.

3. Is a fall in net liquidity being caused by the Treasury rebuilding its cash balance after a debt ceiling episode, which creates large and temporary drains that reverse once the rebuild completes? Debt ceiling dynamics drive some of the largest swings in net liquidity, and understanding them is essential for judging whether a drain is durable.

4. Is the liquidity added or drained large enough relative to total banking system reserves to meaningfully affect financial conditions, or is it small relative to the overall level of reserves? The impact depends on the size of the swing relative to total reserves, since the same drain matters more when reserves are already scarce.

5. Are banking system reserves abundant enough to absorb a liquidity drain without creating funding stress, or are reserves scarce enough that a drain could cause strain in money markets? When reserves are abundant, the effects of a drain are muted, while scarce reserves make the system vulnerable.

6. Is a fall in reverse repos returning cash to the banking system in a way that is cushioning what would otherwise be a policy-driven drain, or are reverse repos rising and adding to the drain? The reverse repo balance can either offset or amplify a tightening, which changes the net effect on liquidity-sensitive sectors.

7. Is the change in net liquidity reinforcing or offsetting what the central bank is doing through its policy rate and balance sheet? When net liquidity moves in the same direction as policy it amplifies the effect, while an offsetting move can neutralize it.

8. Is the effect of net liquidity consistent with what funding spreads and market volatility are showing, or is liquidity moving in a way not yet reflected in stress measures? Confirmation across measures strengthens the signal, while a divergence may provide an early read on changing conditions.

9. Is the change in net liquidity being anticipated by markets, or is it a surprise that could move financial conditions and asset prices unexpectedly? Anticipated changes are already priced, while surprises can move markets, which affects the trading implications.

10. Is the change in net liquidity large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect banking system reserves and the liquidity-sensitive sectors, or is it a routine fluctuation that will not change financial conditions? Only a large and sustained swing warrants adjusting the positioning of liquidity-sensitive sectors, since net liquidity fluctuates with the government's cash management and money market flows.
