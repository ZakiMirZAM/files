# Oil (WTI Crude)

## Part 1: Definition

Oil measured by West Texas Intermediate crude is the price of the benchmark grade of US crude oil, quoted in US dollars per barrel. The price updates continuously through the trading day in the futures market, with daily closing values reported, and it serves as the reference price for much of the oil produced and traded in North America. West Texas Intermediate is one of two main global oil benchmarks, the other being Brent crude, and the gap between them reflects regional supply and transport conditions.

The price is determined in the futures market by the balance of supply and demand for physical crude oil plus the positioning of financial traders. Supply factors are reflected because the output decisions of major producing groups, US shale drilling activity, and any disruptions from geopolitical events or weather all affect how much crude is available. Demand factors are reflected because the consumption of refineries that turn crude into gasoline, diesel, and other products rises and falls with economic activity, travel, and industrial production. Inventory levels are reflected because the amount of crude held in storage signals whether the market is oversupplied or tight. The price is included as an economic indicator because oil is both a major input cost across the economy and a key revenue source for energy producers, so its level affects inflation, consumer spending power, and the profitability of the energy sector. The futures market structure, where near-term and longer-dated contracts trade at different prices, also reveals whether the market sees current tightness or expects future oversupply.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Oil matters for sector investing because it is simultaneously a direct revenue driver for energy producers and a major cost input for the rest of the economy, which creates clear winners and losers when the price moves. When oil prices rise, energy producers in the Energy sector capture the higher prices directly as revenue, while fuel-intensive sectors such as Industrials transportation, Consumer Discretionary travel companies, and Materials face rising costs that compress margins. When oil prices fall, energy producers lose revenue but fuel-consuming sectors gain margin relief and consumers gain spending power as gasoline costs drop. The metric matters with particular force for the Energy sector because oil and gas producer revenue scales almost directly with the oil price above their cost of production. Oil also feeds into inflation, since energy costs flow through to transportation and production costs across the economy, which can force a monetary response. The combination of a direct revenue link to energy producers and a cost impact on fuel-consuming sectors makes oil a central input for choosing between energy and energy-consuming sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the oil price move being driven by changes in demand (which reflects the health of the global economy) or by changes in supply (which reflects production decisions, geopolitical events, or disruptions)? Demand-driven moves signal economic strength or weakness that affects all cyclical sectors, while supply-driven moves act as a cost shock that helps energy producers but hurts the broader economy.

2. Are oil inventories at major storage hubs rising or falling relative to seasonal norms, and does the inventory trend confirm whether the market is genuinely tight or oversupplied? Inventory data provides a physical-market check on whether the price move reflects real supply-demand conditions or speculative positioning.

3. What are the major oil-producing groups doing with their output and spare capacity, and are they adding or restricting supply in a way that will affect the price going forward? Production decisions by large producers are among the most important drivers of oil prices, and their spare capacity determines how much they can influence the market.

4. Is US shale production responding to the current price by ramping up (which would add supply and cap prices) or holding back (which would let prices run higher)? US shale acts as a swing producer that responds to prices, so its activity affects how sustainable a price move is.

5. Is the oil futures curve showing near-term prices above longer-dated prices (signaling current tightness) or below them (signaling expected future oversupply), and what does the curve shape say about the durability of the price move? The futures curve reveals the market's view on whether current conditions will persist, which affects how long an energy sector tilt should last.

6. How does the demand picture look across the major consuming regions, and is global oil demand rising with economic growth or falling with a slowdown? Because oil demand tracks global activity, the demand trend across major economies is central to interpreting the price.

7. Is the refining margin, the difference between the price of crude and the price of the gasoline and diesel made from it, wide or narrow, and what does it say about end-demand for fuel products? Refining margins reveal whether the demand for finished fuels is strong, which is a more direct read on consumption than crude prices alone.

8. Are geopolitical risks adding a risk premium to the oil price that could reverse if tensions ease, or is the price reflecting fundamental supply-demand conditions? A geopolitical risk premium can deflate quickly when tensions resolve, which makes price moves driven by it less durable than fundamental moves.

9. How does the current oil price compare to the cost of production for the marginal producers, and does the price leave room for producers to profit or pressure them toward cutting output? The relationship between price and production cost determines energy sector profitability and whether supply will adjust.

10. Is the oil price move large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect both energy producer revenue and the cost structure of fuel-consuming sectors, or is it a short-term fluctuation that companies can manage through? Only sustained and meaningful moves warrant a sector rotation, since both producers and consumers can absorb minor temporary price swings.
