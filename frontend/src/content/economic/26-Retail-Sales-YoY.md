# Retail Sales YoY

## Part 1: Definition

Retail Sales measures the total dollar value of goods sold by retail stores and food service establishments, with the year-over-year version comparing the current month to the same month one year earlier, expressed as a percentage change. The data is released monthly and covers the prior month. A closely watched version called the control group strips out the most volatile categories, autos, gasoline, building materials, and food services, to give a cleaner read on underlying consumer spending. The figure is a direct measure of how much households are actually spending on goods.

Retail Sales is constructed by surveying a large sample of retail businesses and totaling their sales receipts across categories. Sales at motor vehicle and parts dealers are included because autos are one of the largest household purchases and a key cyclical signal, though they are volatile enough that the control group excludes them. Sales at general merchandise stores, including department stores and large retailers, are included because they capture broad consumer goods spending. Sales at food and beverage stores are included because grocery spending is a stable necessity category. Sales at gasoline stations are included because fuel is a major household expense, though it is excluded from the control group because it swings with oil prices rather than consumer demand. Sales at restaurants and bars are included because dining out is a discretionary spending category that reflects consumer confidence. Online sales are included because e-commerce has become a major channel for consumer goods. The categories are combined to capture the full picture of consumer goods spending, and the control group version is built by removing the volatile pieces to isolate the underlying trend in discretionary and core consumer demand.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Retail Sales matters for sector investing because it is the most direct monthly measure of consumer spending on goods, and since consumer spending drives the majority of economic activity, it directly signals the revenue trend for consumer-facing sectors. When retail sales are growing strongly, households are spending freely on goods, which lifts Consumer Discretionary retailers, Communication Services through advertising tied to retail, and the broader consumer economy. When retail sales are flat or falling, consumers are pulling back, which pressures discretionary retailers while shifting relative strength toward Consumer Staples that sell necessities. The metric matters with particular force for Consumer Discretionary because the sector is composed largely of the retailers and goods makers whose sales the report measures directly, so the data flows almost immediately into their revenue expectations. Retail sales also confirm or contradict the signal from consumer sentiment, providing hard evidence of whether confidence is translating into spending. The combination of a direct revenue link to consumer retailers and a hard-data read on consumer health makes Retail Sales a high-priority input for positioning consumer-facing sectors.

|---|---|---|
| Consumer Discretionary | The sector is composed largely of the retailers and goods makers whose sales the report measures directly, so retail sales growth flows almost immediately into their revenue. Strong sales mean rising revenue, while falling sales mean shrinking revenue. The sector's earnings track retail sales more directly than any other. | Buy when retail sales are growing strongly. Avoid when they are flat or falling. |
| Communication Services | The advertising portion of this sector depends on retailers spending to promote their goods, so strong retail sales support advertising budgets while weak sales lead retailers to cut promotion. The cyclical components follow retail spending. The sector benefits from strong retail sales through advertising. | Buy when retail sales are growing strongly. Avoid when they are falling. |
| Financials | Consumer borrowing for goods purchases through credit cards and installment loans rises with retail spending, so strong sales support consumer lending revenue and credit quality. Weak sales reduce borrowing and can raise credit risk. The sector benefits indirectly through consumer credit. | Buy when retail sales are growing. Avoid when they are falling and credit risk rises. |
| Information Technology | Consumer technology purchases are part of retail sales, so strong sales support consumer tech demand for devices and electronics. Weak sales signal reduced consumer tech spending. The sector has moderate exposure through its consumer products. | Buy when retail sales are growing. Less negative than pure discretionary when sales fall due to business demand. |
| Real Estate | Strong retail sales support demand for retail property space and the rents that retail landlords charge, while weak sales pressure retail occupancy and rents. The sector's retail property segment follows consumer spending. Retail sales affect the retail real estate channel. | Buy when retail sales are growing and supporting retail property. Avoid when falling sales pressure retail occupancy. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples include the grocery and necessity retailers whose sales hold steady regardless of the cycle, and the sector gains relative appeal when falling discretionary retail sales drive consumers toward necessities. The defensive demand holds when discretionary spending weakens. The sector is a destination when retail sales slow. | Buy when retail sales are flat or falling. Underperforms when strong sales drive discretionary leadership. |
| Health Care | Health care demand is driven by medical need rather than retail spending, so the sector behaves defensively when falling retail sales signal consumer weakness. Its earnings stability becomes valuable when discretionary spending falters. The sector is insulated from the retail channel. | Buy when retail sales are falling. Defensive default in consumer downturns. |
| Utilities | Utility demand for essential services is independent of retail spending, so the sector behaves defensively when falling retail sales signal a weakening consumer. It holds up when discretionary spending declines. The sector is a refuge when retail sales fall. | Buy when retail sales are falling. Avoid when strong sales drive cyclical leadership. |
| Energy | Energy demand is relatively inelastic to retail spending, and while gasoline is part of retail sales, energy producer revenue depends more on oil prices than on retail volumes. The connection is weak and indirect. Retail sales are not a meaningful driver for energy producers. | Low sensitivity. Energy producer revenue follows oil prices more than retail sales. |
| Materials | Materials demand is driven by industrial and construction activity rather than consumer retail spending, so the sector has little direct exposure to retail sales. The connection runs through the broader economy. Retail sales are a secondary consideration. | Not meaningfully affected by retail sales. |
| Industrials | Industrial demand is driven by business investment rather than consumer retail spending, so the sector has limited direct exposure to retail sales. The connection is indirect through the broader consumer economy. Retail sales are a secondary consideration. | Low direct sensitivity. Follows business investment more than retail sales. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside Retail Sales | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer Sentiment Index | Sentiment measures how households feel while retail sales measure what they actually spend, so reading them together confirms whether confidence is translating into spending or whether the two are diverging. Sentiment and spending sometimes move apart, and the hard sales data confirms or corrects the sentiment signal. This validates the sentiment reading against actual behavior. | When sentiment is high AND retail sales are growing, the optimism is translating into spending, which supports Consumer Discretionary with confidence. When sentiment is falling but retail sales are still growing, households are spending despite their stated pessimism, which suggests the discretionary pullback is delayed and argues against a premature defensive rotation. |
| Wages vs CPI | Real purchasing power drives the ability to spend, so reading retail sales alongside real wages shows whether spending growth is funded by genuine income gains or by drawing on savings and credit. Sustainable retail growth needs real income support. This connects the spending to its income foundation. | When retail sales are growing AND real wages are positive, the spending is funded by genuine income gains and is sustainable, supporting Consumer Discretionary. When retail sales are growing but real wages are negative, households are spending by drawing on savings or credit, which cannot last and warns that discretionary spending will eventually pull back. |
| Personal Savings Rate | The savings rate and retail sales together reveal whether households are spending out of income or out of savings, so reading them together shows the sustainability of the spending. A falling savings rate alongside strong sales signals households are dipping into savings. This connects spending to its funding source. | When retail sales are growing AND the savings rate is stable, the spending is funded sustainably from income, supporting Consumer Discretionary. When retail sales are growing but the savings rate is falling sharply, households are funding spending by depleting savings, which is unsustainable and signals a coming pullback that argues for caution on discretionary sectors. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the growth in retail sales real, reflecting more goods being purchased, or is it merely nominal, reflecting the same volume of goods at higher prices because of inflation? When retail sales growth is entirely driven by inflation rather than higher volumes, the underlying consumer demand is weaker than the headline suggests, which affects the read on discretionary sectors.

2. Is the control group version, which strips out volatile autos, gasoline, and building materials, showing the same trend as the headline, or is the headline being distorted by swings in those volatile categories? The control group gives a cleaner read on underlying consumer demand, so a divergence between it and the headline reveals whether the signal is genuine.

3. Is the spending growth concentrated in discretionary categories such as electronics, apparel, and dining out, or in necessity categories such as groceries, given that the mix reveals whether consumers are confident or cautious? Discretionary-led growth signals consumer confidence that supports Consumer Discretionary, while necessity-led growth signals caution that favors Consumer Staples.

4. Is the spending being funded by current income, by drawing down savings, or by taking on credit card and installment debt, and how sustainable is the funding source? Spending funded by income is sustainable, while spending funded by savings depletion or rising debt cannot continue and warns of a coming pullback.

5. Are the retail sales gains broad across income groups, or concentrated among higher-income households while lower-income households pull back, which would affect whether premium or value retailers benefit? The distribution of spending across income groups determines which consumer sectors and which types of retailers benefit most.

6. Is online retail growing at the expense of physical stores, and what does the channel shift say about which retailers and which retail property types will benefit or suffer? The shift between e-commerce and physical retail affects different companies and the retail real estate that serves them.

7. Is the retail sales trend consistent with what consumer sentiment surveys are showing, or is there a divergence where households say one thing but spend differently? When spending and sentiment diverge, the hard sales data is the more reliable guide to actual consumer behavior.

8. Are retail sales being distorted by one-time factors such as stimulus payments, tax refunds, seasonal timing, or weather, which would make the underlying trend different from the reported figure? One-time factors can inflate or depress a month's reading, so identifying them is essential for reading the true trend.

9. Is the retail sales trend accelerating or decelerating over the most recent months, and what does the direction of change say about where consumer spending is heading? The trend matters more than a single month, since accelerating sales signal a strengthening consumer while decelerating sales warn of weakening demand.

10. Is the change in retail sales large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in consumer spending power, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary factors that will not change the underlying consumer trend? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants a sector rotation, since retail sales can be volatile month to month.
