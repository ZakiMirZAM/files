# U-6 Unemployment Rate

## Part 1: Definition

The U-6 Unemployment Rate is the broadest official measure of unemployment and underemployment in the United States. It is expressed as a percentage of the labor force and is released monthly. Unlike the headline unemployment rate, which counts only people without jobs who are actively looking, the U-6 rate also includes people who have given up looking for work and people who are working part-time but want full-time work. This makes it a fuller picture of the slack in the labor market, capturing forms of underemployment that the headline rate misses.

The U-6 rate is constructed by adding several groups together and dividing by an expanded measure of the labor force. The officially unemployed are included because they are without work and actively seeking it, which is the core definition of unemployment. Marginally attached workers are included because they want a job and have looked recently but have stopped actively searching, often because they are discouraged, so excluding them would understate the true pool of people who want work. Discouraged workers, a subset of the marginally attached who have stopped looking specifically because they believe no jobs are available, are included for the same reason. People working part-time for economic reasons are included because they are employed but underutilized, wanting full-time work they cannot find, which represents genuine labor market slack that the headline rate ignores. These groups are combined because together they capture the full extent of unused labor capacity, which is included as the goal of the measure because labor market slack affects wage pressure, consumer income, and the inflation outlook in ways the narrow rate does not fully reveal.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The U-6 rate matters for sector investing because it provides the fullest picture of labor market slack, which determines household income, wage pressure, and the inflation outlook that together shape sector leadership. When the U-6 rate is high, there is substantial labor market slack, meaning many people want more work than they have, which suppresses wage growth and consumer spending power and signals a weak economy that favors defensive sectors. When the U-6 rate is low, the labor market is genuinely tight with little slack, which supports wage growth and consumer spending that benefits cyclical sectors but also generates inflation pressure. The metric matters because it reveals slack that the headline unemployment rate hides, giving a more accurate read on how much room the labor market has before wage-driven inflation emerges. The gap between the U-6 rate and the headline rate is itself informative, since a wide gap signals hidden weakness even when the headline looks healthy. The combination of a fuller slack measure and a more accurate read on wage and inflation pressure makes the U-6 rate a valuable input for judging the strength of the consumer and the cycle stage.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. How wide is the gap between the U-6 rate and the headline unemployment rate, and is the gap widening or narrowing, given that a wide gap signals hidden labor market slack that the headline rate misses? A widening gap reveals deteriorating conditions beneath a healthy-looking headline, which is an early warning for consumer-facing sectors.

2. Is the change in the U-6 rate being driven by the officially unemployed, by discouraged workers giving up the search, or by part-time workers wanting full-time work, given that each component carries a different signal about the labor market? A rise driven by discouraged workers signals deeper weakness than one driven by temporary unemployment.

3. Are part-time workers who want full-time jobs increasing, which would signal that employers are cutting hours before cutting jobs, an early sign of labor market weakening? Because employers often reduce hours before laying off, a rise in involuntary part-time work is an early warning of deteriorating conditions.

4. Is the U-6 rate low enough to signal genuine labor market tightness that will drive wage growth and inflation, or is there still meaningful slack that allows employment to grow without inflation? The level of slack determines whether the labor market is generating inflation pressure or still has room to absorb workers.

5. Are discouraged workers returning to the labor force as conditions improve, which would temporarily raise measured unemployment even as the labor market strengthens? When discouraged workers return, the U-6 rate can rise for a positive reason, which must be distinguished from genuine deterioration.

6. Is the U-6 rate consistent with what other labor market indicators such as job openings, quits, and payrolls are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure may be misleading? Confirmation across labor indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence warrants caution.

7. Are the changes in the U-6 rate concentrated among particular demographic groups or industries, and what does that distribution say about which parts of the consumer economy are strengthening or weakening? Concentrated changes affect specific consumer segments differently, which has implications for which consumer sectors benefit.

8. Is the U-6 rate leading or following the broader economic cycle, and where does the current level and direction place the economy in that framework? The labor market's position in the cycle helps confirm the timing of sector rotations.

9. How does the current U-6 rate compare to its level at the same stage of past cycles, and is the relationship between labor slack and consumer spending behaving the way it has historically? Checking the historical pattern guards against assuming a relationship holds when consumer behavior may have shifted.

10. Is the change in the U-6 rate large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the labor market and consumer spending power, or is it a short-term fluctuation that could reverse? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants a sector rotation, since the rate can fluctuate month to month for temporary reasons.
