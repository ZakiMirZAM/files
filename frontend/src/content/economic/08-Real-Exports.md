# Real Exports

## Part 1: Definition

Real Exports measures the inflation-adjusted dollar value of goods and services that the United States sells to buyers in other countries during a period, reported as a level in inflation-adjusted dollars and most usefully tracked as a percentage change from the prior year. The figure is part of the quarterly national accounts and is released with the GDP report, with monthly trade data providing more frequent updates on the goods portion. Adjusting for inflation matters because it isolates the change in the actual quantity of goods and services sold abroad rather than just changes in their prices.

Real Exports is constructed by adding together the value of all categories of goods and services sold to foreign buyers and then removing the effect of price changes. Goods exports are included because physical products such as aircraft, machinery, agricultural commodities, chemicals, and refined fuels represent the bulk of what US producers sell abroad, and these categories are the most sensitive to foreign demand and the exchange rate. Services exports are included because the United States sells large amounts of intellectual property licensing, financial services, travel and tourism, and business services to foreign buyers, and these have become a growing share of total exports that would be missed if only goods were counted. Each category is measured at the value foreign buyers actually pay, and the total is adjusted using price indexes for exported goods and services so the final number reflects real volume changes. The metric is included in the national accounts as a positive contributor to GDP because goods and services produced inside the country and sold abroad represent domestic production, so rising real exports add directly to measured economic output.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Real Exports matters for sector investing because it directly measures foreign demand for US production, which is the revenue source for the export-heavy sectors and a signal about the health of the global economy that US multinationals depend on. When real exports are rising, the companies that produce exportable goods, concentrated in Industrials (aircraft, machinery, equipment), Materials (chemicals, metals), Energy (refined fuels and crude), and Information Technology (hardware and software), capture rising foreign revenue directly. When real exports are falling, those same sectors face shrinking foreign markets and the broader signal points to weakening global growth that hurts all globally exposed companies. The metric matters with particular force for capital goods producers within Industrials because aircraft and heavy machinery are among the largest US export categories by value, so changes in export demand show up quickly in their order books. Real exports also interacts with the dollar, since a weaker dollar makes US goods cheaper abroad and tends to lift export volumes over time. The combination of direct revenue linkage to export-producing sectors and a window into global demand conditions makes real exports a valuable input for deciding how much to weight globally exposed sectors versus domestically focused ones.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

|---|---|---|
| Trade Weighted Index | The dollar's strength is one of the main drivers of export competitiveness, so reading the exchange rate alongside actual export volumes shows whether currency moves are translating into trade outcomes. This connects the price signal to the volume result for export sectors. | When the dollar is weak AND real exports are rising, the competitiveness channel is working and export-heavy Industrials and Materials deserve overweight positions with confidence. When the dollar is weak but real exports are not rising, foreign demand is too weak to respond to the currency advantage, which signals a global slowdown that argues for defensive positioning despite the favorable currency. |
| GDP Growth | Real exports are a direct component of GDP, so reading them alongside overall GDP growth shows whether exports are adding to or subtracting from domestic output growth. The combination reveals whether the economy is being driven by foreign demand or domestic demand. This distinction affects whether to favor export sectors or domestic sectors. | When GDP growth is strong AND real exports are rising, the expansion is broad-based across foreign and domestic demand, supporting both export sectors and domestic cyclicals. When GDP growth is strong but real exports are falling, the growth is domestically driven and domestic-focused sectors like Consumer Discretionary should be favored over export-heavy Industrials and Materials. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in real exports being driven by goods exports (which are sensitive to global manufacturing demand and the exchange rate) or by services exports (which are tied to global business activity, travel, and intellectual property licensing)? The two categories respond to different forces and benefit different sectors, with goods strength favoring Industrials and Materials while services strength favors Communication Services and Financials.

2. Which specific export categories are driving the change, given that a small number of categories such as aircraft, refined fuels, agricultural products, and semiconductors account for a large share of US exports? Concentration in a few categories means the export trend may benefit only specific sectors rather than the broad export economy.

3. Is the change in real exports being driven by stronger or weaker foreign demand, or by the exchange rate making US goods more or less competitive, or by both at once? Separating the demand driver from the currency driver matters because demand-driven export strength signals a healthy global economy while currency-driven strength reflects a more temporary competitiveness advantage.

4. Are exports to major trading partners moving in the same direction, or is the change concentrated in exports to one region such as Europe, China, or North America? Regional concentration tells you which foreign economies are driving the trend and which US companies with exposure to those regions will benefit most.

5. Is the export strength or weakness consistent with what other countries are reporting for their imports from the United States, and does the trade data from both sides of the transaction agree? When export data is confirmed by trading partners' import data, the signal is more reliable than when the two diverge due to measurement timing.

6. Are trade barriers, tariffs, or trade agreements changing in ways that affect the volume of US exports independent of underlying demand and currency conditions? Policy-driven changes in export access can move the data for reasons that will reverse or persist depending on the policy, which affects how durable the sector implications are.

7. Is the real export trend leading or lagging the broader global manufacturing cycle, and what does the relationship say about whether export demand will continue in the coming quarters? Exports often move with the global manufacturing cycle, so understanding the cycle position helps forecast whether the current export trend will continue.

8. Are US exporters gaining or losing market share in global markets, or is the export change simply tracking the overall growth or contraction of global trade? Gaining share signals genuine competitiveness improvement that benefits US producers specifically, while merely tracking global trade reflects external conditions.

9. How large is the export exposure of the specific sectors under consideration as a share of their total revenue, and would a meaningful change in exports actually move their earnings? A sector with large export exposure will see real earnings impact from export trends, while a mostly domestic sector will not, which determines how much weight to place on the export signal.

10. Is the change in real exports being supported by rising production capacity and investment in export industries, or is it straining existing capacity in a way that cannot continue without new investment? Capacity-constrained export growth cannot persist without capital investment, which affects both the durability of the trend and the outlook for the capital goods producers that would supply that investment.
