# Global Central Bank Balance Sheet

## Part 1: Definition

The Global Central Bank Balance Sheet measures the combined size of the assets held by the world's major central banks, which reflects the total amount of liquidity those central banks have injected into the global financial system. It is expressed as a total dollar value or as a change over time, and the data is compiled from the individual balance sheets of the major central banks, which report weekly or monthly. The measure reveals whether the world's central banks are collectively adding liquidity to the financial system or withdrawing it, which affects asset prices globally.

The measure is constructed by adding together the balance sheets of the largest central banks, converted to a common currency. The assets of the major central banks are included because when a central bank buys bonds or other assets, it creates new money that enters the financial system, so the size of its balance sheet reflects how much liquidity it has injected. The balance sheets of the largest economies are included because their central banks control the bulk of global liquidity, and their actions dominate the global financial environment. The balances are converted to a common currency so they can be added together into a single global measure. Changes in the combined balance sheet are included as the key signal because an expanding total means central banks are collectively injecting liquidity that tends to lift asset prices, while a shrinking total means they are withdrawing liquidity that tends to pressure asset prices. The measure is included as an indicator because global liquidity conditions affect risk appetite, asset valuations, and the cost of capital across all markets, making the combined balance sheet a gauge of the monetary tide that lifts or lowers asset prices worldwide.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Global Central Bank Balance Sheet matters for sector investing because it measures the global liquidity that flows into asset prices, which affects valuations across all sectors and especially the long-duration and risk-sensitive sectors that depend most on abundant liquidity. When the combined balance sheet is expanding, central banks are injecting liquidity that lowers the cost of capital and lifts asset prices broadly, which benefits long-duration growth sectors like Information Technology and rate-sensitive sectors like Real Estate that gain most from abundant cheap money. When the balance sheet is contracting, liquidity is being withdrawn, which raises the cost of capital and pressures the same liquidity-sensitive sectors while favoring defensive sectors whose earnings do not depend on cheap money. The metric matters because global liquidity has become a powerful driver of asset valuations, often overwhelming individual-country signals, so the global tide affects all markets at once. The combination of a read on the global cost of capital and a direct effect on liquidity-sensitive sectors makes the global balance sheet a useful input for judging the overall risk environment and positioning the most liquidity-dependent sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Are the major central banks acting in the same direction, all expanding or all contracting their balance sheets, or are they diverging in ways that partly offset each other in the global total? Synchronized action by the major central banks creates a powerful global liquidity tide, while divergence produces a more muted and mixed effect.

2. Is the balance sheet expansion or contraction being driven by deliberate policy decisions to ease or tighten, or by mechanical factors such as bonds maturing without being replaced? Deliberate policy changes carry a stronger signal about central bank intentions than mechanical changes in the balance sheet.

3. Is the liquidity from an expanding balance sheet actually flowing into the real economy and broad money supply, or is it staying within the financial system and lifting asset prices without supporting economic activity? When liquidity stays in financial markets, it supports asset prices but not the real activity that cyclical sectors depend on.

4. Which central banks are driving the change in the global total, and do their economies and currencies carry enough weight to move global conditions? The largest central banks dominate the global liquidity picture, so a change driven by a major central bank matters more than one driven by a smaller one.

5. Is the currency conversion used to combine the balance sheets distorting the global total because of exchange rate movements rather than genuine changes in liquidity? When exchange rates move sharply, the combined balance sheet measured in a common currency can change without any real change in liquidity injection.

6. Is the change in the balance sheet large relative to its history, and does the current trajectory represent a meaningful shift in global liquidity conditions or a minor adjustment? The size of the change relative to history reveals whether it represents a significant liquidity shift that warrants repositioning.

7. Are central banks expanding the balance sheet to fight a genuine crisis, which signals economic stress alongside the liquidity support, or to provide ongoing stimulus in calmer conditions? Crisis-driven expansion comes with economic stress that affects sectors differently than stimulus provided in stable conditions.

8. Is the liquidity environment consistent with what interest rates and credit spreads are signaling, or is there a divergence between the quantity of liquidity and its price? Confirmation across monetary indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence requires understanding which channel is dominating.

9. How sensitive are the specific sectors under consideration to global liquidity, given that long-duration and rate-sensitive sectors respond far more than defensive sectors with stable earnings? The sectors' liquidity sensitivity determines how much the balance sheet trend should affect their positioning.

10. Is the change in the global balance sheet large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect the global cost of capital and asset valuations, or is it a short-term fluctuation that will not change the liquidity environment? Only a sustained and meaningful change in global liquidity warrants repositioning the most liquidity-sensitive sectors.
