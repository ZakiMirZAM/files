# Trade Weighted Index

## Part 1: Definition

The Trade Weighted Index measures the value of the US dollar against a basket of foreign currencies, with each currency weighted by how much the United States trades with that country. The index is expressed as a level relative to a base period rather than a percentage, and the most useful figure for analysis is the percentage change in the index over a chosen period such as three months or one year. The data is published daily and in summary form weekly and monthly.

The index is constructed by combining the dollar's exchange rate against many individual foreign currencies into one number. Each currency is included because it belongs to a country that the United States actually trades with, and the trade-weighting matters because a currency from a major trading partner has more economic impact on US companies than a currency from a minor one. The currencies of large trading partners such as the euro area, China, Canada, Mexico, and Japan receive the heaviest weights because changes in those exchange rates affect the largest share of US imports and exports, while currencies of smaller trading partners receive smaller weights because they touch a smaller portion of US trade. The trade weighting is included this way so the index reflects the dollar's true impact on the competitiveness of US goods abroad and the cost of imported goods at home, rather than treating every currency as equally important. A broad version of the index covers a wide set of currencies to capture the full trade picture, while a narrower version covers only the most heavily traded major currencies. A rising index means the dollar is strengthening against trading-partner currencies, and a falling index means the dollar is weakening.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Trade Weighted Index matters for sector investing because the strength of the dollar directly changes the reported earnings of companies that sell abroad and the input costs of companies that import, which creates clear winners and losers across sectors. When the dollar strengthens, US companies with large foreign sales see their overseas revenue translate into fewer dollars, which compresses the reported earnings of multinationals concentrated in Information Technology, Industrials, Materials, and Energy. At the same time a strong dollar lowers the cost of imported inputs and imported finished goods, which helps domestic-focused retailers and companies that buy from abroad. When the dollar weakens, the relationship reverses and multinationals and commodity producers benefit because foreign revenue translates into more dollars and dollar-priced commodities rise in value. The index matters with particular force for Materials and Energy because most commodities are priced in dollars globally, so a weaker dollar mechanically raises commodity prices and producer revenue. The combination of the earnings-translation effect on multinationals and the pricing effect on commodities makes the Trade Weighted Index a key input for choosing between domestically focused sectors and globally exposed sectors.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rising sharply (dollar strengthening more than 5% over six months) | The dollar is strengthening rapidly, usually because US interest rates are rising faster than those abroad, US growth is outpacing other economies, or global investors are seeking the safety of dollar assets during stress. A strong dollar lowers the cost of imported goods, which helps domestic retailers and import-dependent companies. It hurts US multinationals because their foreign sales convert into fewer dollars, and it pressures commodity producers because dollar-priced commodities become more expensive for foreign buyers, reducing demand. | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Real Estate, Financials | Information Technology, Industrials, Materials, Energy, Communication Services | Health Care, Utilities |
| Rising moderately (dollar gradually appreciating) | The dollar is gaining ground at a measured pace, reflecting relatively stronger US fundamentals without a disruptive surge. Domestic-focused sectors enjoy a modest input-cost benefit, while multinationals face a manageable headwind to translated earnings. The effect is present but not large enough to dominate sector selection on its own. | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Financials | Materials, Energy, Information Technology | Health Care, Utilities, Real Estate, Industrials, Communication Services |
| Stable (dollar trading in a narrow range) | The dollar is neither gaining nor losing meaningful ground, which usually reflects balanced relative growth and interest rate conditions between the US and its trading partners. Neither multinationals nor domestic-focused companies face a currency tailwind or headwind. Sector selection should be driven by other factors when the dollar is range-bound. | Information Technology, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary | Energy | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Materials, Real Estate, Communication Services |
| Falling (dollar weakening) | The dollar is losing value against trading-partner currencies, usually because US rates are falling relative to abroad, US growth is lagging, or risk appetite is drawing capital into foreign and emerging markets. A weak dollar raises the translated value of multinationals' foreign earnings and lifts dollar-priced commodity prices, benefiting globally exposed sectors. Domestic retailers face higher import costs that pressure margins. | Materials, Energy, Information Technology, Industrials | Consumer Discretionary, Consumer Staples, Real Estate | Health Care, Utilities, Financials, Communication Services |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | Most industrial metals and raw materials are priced in dollars on global markets, so a weaker dollar mechanically raises their prices and lifts producer revenue, while a stronger dollar lowers commodity prices and compresses revenue. Materials companies also earn substantial foreign revenue that translates more favorably when the dollar is weak. This dual exposure makes Materials one of the most dollar-sensitive sectors. | Buy when the dollar is falling. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Energy | Oil and gas are priced in dollars globally, so a weaker dollar raises the dollar price of energy and supports producer revenue, while a stronger dollar reduces foreign demand for dollar-priced energy. Energy companies also have large international operations whose earnings translate more favorably in a weak-dollar environment. The sector's revenue is closely tied to the dollar through both channels. | Buy when the dollar is falling. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Information Technology | Large technology companies generate a substantial share of revenue abroad, so a stronger dollar reduces the dollar value of that foreign revenue and compresses reported earnings, while a weaker dollar amplifies it. Because tech valuations are sensitive to earnings momentum, currency swings can meaningfully move the sector. The translation effect is the primary dollar linkage for tech. | Buy when the dollar is falling or stable. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Industrials | Industrial companies export heavy equipment and earn significant foreign revenue, so a stronger dollar makes US exports more expensive abroad and reduces translated earnings, while a weaker dollar improves export competitiveness and translation. The sector's global sales make it directly exposed to the index. The competitiveness effect compounds the translation effect. | Buy when the dollar is falling or stable. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Domestic-focused retailers benefit from a strong dollar because it lowers the cost of imported goods they sell, expanding margins, while a weak dollar raises import costs and squeezes margins. The sector is one of the few that prefers a stronger dollar. The import-cost channel is the dominant linkage for domestic retail. | Buy when the dollar is rising. Avoid when the dollar is falling sharply and raising import costs. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples companies that source products and ingredients from abroad benefit from a strong dollar through lower input costs, though large multinational staples face an offsetting translation headwind on foreign sales. The net effect depends on the specific company's mix of domestic sourcing versus foreign sales. Domestically focused staples lean toward benefiting from a strong dollar. | Mixed signal. Domestic-sourcing staples buy on dollar strength, multinational staples prefer dollar weakness. |
| Financials | Most banks earn primarily domestic revenue, so they are relatively insulated from the translation effect, but a strong dollar that reflects strong US fundamentals often coincides with conditions favorable to domestic lending. The linkage is indirect and runs through the macro conditions that drive the dollar rather than through direct currency exposure. The sector is less dollar-sensitive than multinationals. | Mostly indirect. Modestly favored when a strong dollar reflects strong US growth. |
| Real Estate | US real estate is a domestic asset, so REIT earnings are largely insulated from currency translation, but a strong dollar can attract foreign capital into US property as a safe asset. The linkage is indirect and works through capital flows rather than through revenue translation. The sector has low direct sensitivity to the index. | Mostly indirect. Modestly favored when a strong dollar attracts foreign property investment. |
| Communication Services | The cyclical advertising and content companies in this sector earn meaningful foreign revenue, so a stronger dollar compresses their translated earnings while a weaker dollar amplifies them. The subscription portion is more domestically anchored. The sector has moderate exposure through its global digital platforms. | Buy when the dollar is falling. Avoid when the dollar is rising sharply. |
| Health Care | Large pharmaceutical companies earn substantial foreign revenue subject to translation, but domestic-focused health services are insulated, so the net sector sensitivity is moderate. The defensive nature of the sector usually dominates currency effects. The dollar is a secondary consideration for health care positioning. | Low sensitivity. Pharma leans toward preferring dollar weakness, services are insulated. |
| Utilities | Utilities are almost entirely domestic businesses serving local customers, so their revenue has essentially no currency translation exposure. The sector is the closest to genuinely unaffected by the dollar. Currency movements should not drive utility positioning. | Not meaningfully affected by the dollar. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the Trade Weighted Index | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Real Exports | The dollar's strength directly affects how competitive US exports are abroad, and real exports show whether that competitiveness effect is actually showing up in trade volumes. Reading them together confirms whether a strong or weak dollar is translating into real changes in export demand. This connects the financial signal to the real-economy outcome for export-dependent sectors. | When the dollar is weakening AND real exports are rising, the competitiveness benefit is materializing and export-heavy Industrials and Materials deserve overweight positions. When the dollar is weakening but real exports are flat, the currency benefit is not translating into volume, which suggests weak foreign demand is offsetting the currency advantage and argues for caution on export-dependent names. |
| Import Price Index | The dollar's level drives the cost of imported goods, and the import price index measures whether those costs are actually rising or falling. Reading them together confirms whether a strong dollar is delivering the input-cost relief that domestic retailers depend on. This links the currency signal to the margin outcome for import-dependent sectors. | When the dollar is strengthening AND import prices are falling, domestic retailers in Consumer Discretionary and Consumer Staples are getting genuine margin relief and deserve overweight positions. When the dollar is strengthening but import prices are not falling, foreign suppliers may be raising prices to offset the currency move, which removes the expected margin benefit for importers. |
| US Dollar Index | The US Dollar Index measures the dollar against a smaller set of major currencies, while the Trade Weighted Index covers a broader trade-relevant basket. Reading them together reveals whether dollar strength is broad-based or concentrated against a few major currencies. This distinction matters for which multinationals are most affected. | When both indexes are rising together, dollar strength is broad and all multinationals face translation headwinds, which favors domestic-focused sectors across the board. When the narrow index rises but the trade-weighted index is flat, the dollar is strong only against major currencies and companies with emerging-market exposure may be less affected, which allows more selective positioning within global sectors. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the dollar's movement being driven by the difference between US interest rates and foreign interest rates, by the difference between US economic growth and foreign growth, or by global investors seeking the safety of dollar assets during a period of stress? Each of these drivers has different durability and different implications, with rate-driven and growth-driven moves tending to persist while safe-haven moves can reverse quickly when stress fades.

2. Is the dollar strengthening or weakening broadly against most trading-partner currencies, or is the move concentrated against just a few currencies? A broad-based move affects all US multinationals and commodity producers, while a concentrated move affects only companies with exposure to the specific currencies involved.

3. Is the change in the trade-weighted dollar large enough and sustained enough to meaningfully affect corporate earnings, or is it a short-term fluctuation that companies can hedge through and that will not show up in reported results? Currency moves must be both large and durable to warrant a sector tilt, since companies routinely hedge short-term swings.

4. How much of the revenue of the sectors under consideration actually comes from foreign markets that are exposed to translation, and does the foreign revenue come from regions whose currencies are moving the most against the dollar? A sector with 40% foreign revenue concentrated in a sharply depreciating region faces a much larger earnings impact than one with the same foreign share spread across stable currencies.

5. Is the dollar's strength being matched by similar strength in foreign currencies against other currencies, or is the dollar uniquely strong, and what does this say about whether the driver is US-specific or part of a broader global currency realignment? A uniquely strong dollar points to US-specific factors, while a broad realignment points to global risk conditions.

6. Are foreign central banks intervening in currency markets to resist their currencies weakening against the dollar, and could that intervention reverse the dollar's move? Heavy intervention signals that the current dollar level may be unsustainable and could snap back, which affects the durability of any sector tilt based on the currency.

7. Is the dollar move consistent with the difference in real interest rates (interest rates adjusted for inflation) between the US and its trading partners, which is the most fundamental long-run driver of exchange rates? When the dollar moves in line with real rate differentials it is on fundamentally sound footing, while a move that diverges from rate differentials may be driven by sentiment that can reverse.

8. For commodity-producing sectors, is the weaker or stronger dollar actually moving the dollar-denominated price of the commodity as the historical relationship would predict, or has that relationship broken down because of supply-and-demand factors specific to the commodity? The dollar-commodity link is usually strong but can be overwhelmed by commodity-specific supply shocks.

9. Are US companies in the affected sectors able to reprice their products in foreign markets to offset the currency move, or are they locked into prices that force them to absorb the full impact of the exchange rate change? Companies with pricing power abroad can offset some currency damage, while those without it absorb the full translation hit.

10. Does the current level of the trade-weighted dollar look expensive or cheap relative to its long-run average and relative to estimates of fair value based on relative prices across countries, and what does that say about the likely future direction? A dollar that is far above its long-run fair value is more likely to weaken eventually, which would reverse the current sector implications.
