# Institute for Supply Management Manufacturing Index

## Part 1: Definition

The Institute for Supply Management Manufacturing Index, often called the manufacturing PMI, measures the direction of activity in the manufacturing sector based on a monthly survey of purchasing managers at manufacturing companies. It is reported as a single index number where readings above 50 indicate that manufacturing activity is expanding compared to the prior month and readings below 50 indicate it is contracting. The data is released on the first business day of each month for the prior month, making it one of the earliest reads on the economy's direction.

The index is constructed by surveying purchasing managers, the people who order materials and track production at manufacturing firms, about whether key parts of their business improved, stayed the same, or worsened compared to the prior month. New orders are included because incoming orders are the most forward-looking signal of future production and reflect customer demand before it shows up in output. Production is included because it captures current factory output and what manufacturers are actually making. Employment is included because hiring or firing in manufacturing reflects how confident firms are about sustained demand. Supplier deliveries are included because slower deliveries signal that demand is straining supply chains, a sign of a tightening economy, while faster deliveries signal slack. Inventories are included because the level of stock that firms hold reveals whether they expect demand to rise or fall. These five components are combined into the headline index, with each weighted to reflect its reliability as an economic signal. The index is built from a diffusion method, counting the share of firms reporting improvement, so the 50 level marks the dividing line between an expanding and a contracting manufacturing sector.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The manufacturing index matters for sector investing because it is one of the earliest and most reliable monthly signals of the direction of industrial activity, which drives the earnings of the cyclical sectors tied to manufacturing and global trade. When the index is above 50 and rising, manufacturers are expanding production and placing new orders, which lifts Industrials that make capital equipment, Materials that supply inputs, and Energy that powers production. When the index falls below 50, manufacturing is contracting, which pressures those same cyclical sectors and often warns of a broader slowdown because manufacturing weakness frequently leads the overall economy. The index matters with particular force for Industrials and Materials because their order books and volumes track manufacturing activity almost directly, so a turn in the index shows up quickly in their revenue. The new-orders component is especially valuable because it leads the rest of the economy, giving an early signal for rotating between cyclical and defensive sectors. The combination of an early-warning timing advantage and a direct link to manufacturing-dependent sectors makes the manufacturing index a high-priority input for cyclical sector positioning.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong expansion (above 55 and rising) | Manufacturing is expanding briskly, with new orders, production, and employment all growing. This usually appears in the early-to-mid stage of an expansion when demand is strong and factories are ramping up. Industrial producers, materials suppliers, and energy providers all see rising volumes, and the strength signals a healthy cyclical economy. The forward-looking new-orders component suggests the expansion will continue. | Industrials, Materials, Energy, Information Technology, Financials | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Consumer Discretionary, Real Estate, Communication Services |
| Modest expansion (50 to 55) | Manufacturing is growing but at a moderate pace, indicating a steady industrial economy without a boom. Cyclical sectors see modest volume growth, and the signal supports them without a strong tilt. The economy is expanding but not overheating in the manufacturing sector. | Industrials, Materials, Information Technology | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Energy, Consumer Discretionary, Real Estate, Communication Services, Financials |
| Contraction beginning (45 to 50) | Manufacturing is contracting, with new orders and production declining. This often appears in late expansion or early slowdown when demand is fading and factories are pulling back. Cyclical sectors tied to manufacturing face declining volumes, and because manufacturing leads the broader economy, the contraction warns of wider weakness ahead. Investors begin rotating toward defensive sectors. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Industrials, Materials, Energy, Information Technology | Consumer Discretionary, Real Estate, Communication Services, Financials |
| Deep contraction (below 45) | Manufacturing is contracting sharply, a level that has historically coincided with recessions. New orders collapse, production falls, and manufacturers cut employment and inventories. Cyclical sectors tied to manufacturing face significant revenue declines, and the deep contraction confirms a broader economic downturn. Defensive sectors strongly outperform as the cyclical economy weakens. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Industrials, Materials, Energy, Information Technology, Financials, Consumer Discretionary | Real Estate, Communication Services |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Industrials | Industrial companies make the machinery, equipment, and transportation goods whose demand tracks manufacturing activity directly, so a rising index means rising orders for their products. The new-orders component is essentially a leading indicator of industrial revenue. Falling manufacturing activity shrinks their order books first. | Buy when the index is above 50 and rising. Avoid when the index falls below 50. |
| Materials | Materials producers supply the metals, chemicals, and inputs that manufacturers consume, so manufacturing expansion directly increases demand for their products. When manufacturing contracts, materials demand falls and producers face excess capacity. The sector's volumes track manufacturing activity closely. | Buy when the index is above 50 and rising. Avoid when the index falls below 50. |
| Energy | Manufacturing is energy-intensive, so expanding factory activity increases demand for industrial energy, supporting energy producer volumes. Contracting manufacturing reduces industrial energy demand. The sector benefits from the production activity the index measures. | Buy when the index is above 55 with strong production. Avoid when the index falls into deep contraction. |
| Information Technology | Technology hardware and equipment are sold into manufacturing for automation and production systems, and the broader tech cycle correlates with manufacturing activity. A rising index supports tech hardware demand, while a falling index signals weakening business equipment spending. The sector has meaningful exposure through industrial technology. | Buy when the index is above 50 and rising. Less negative than pure cyclicals in contractions due to software resilience. |
| Financials | Manufacturing expansion drives business investment that requires financing, so a rising index supports loan demand from industrial borrowers. The connection runs through the broader cyclical economy that manufacturing leads. The sector benefits indirectly from manufacturing strength. | Buy when the index is rising and signaling expansion. Avoid when the index falls into deep contraction signaling recession. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples demand is unrelated to manufacturing activity, so the sector gains relative appeal when a falling index signals a coming slowdown. The defensive characteristics dominate when manufacturing-driven cyclicals weaken. The sector becomes a destination when the index turns down. | Buy when the index is falling below 50. Underperforms when strong manufacturing drives cyclical leadership. |
| Health Care | Health care demand is independent of manufacturing activity, so the sector behaves defensively when a falling index signals broader weakness. Its earnings stability becomes valuable when cyclical sectors falter. The sector is insulated from the manufacturing channel. | Buy when the index is falling. Defensive default in manufacturing slowdowns. |
| Utilities | Utility demand is stable and largely independent of the manufacturing cycle, though industrial power demand provides a minor link. The sector behaves defensively when manufacturing contracts. It holds up when the index turns down. | Buy when the index is falling and signaling a slowdown. Avoid when strong manufacturing drives cyclical leadership. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Consumer discretionary demand is driven more by household income and confidence than by manufacturing activity, so the sector has limited direct exposure to the index. The connection is indirect through the broader economy. The index is a secondary consideration for discretionary. | Low direct sensitivity. Follows consumer conditions more than manufacturing. |
| Real Estate | Real estate demand is driven by interest rates and property fundamentals rather than manufacturing activity, so the sector has little direct exposure to the index, aside from industrial property demand. The connection is weak and indirect. The index does not drive real estate positioning. | Low sensitivity, except industrial property which follows manufacturing. |
| Communication Services | The sector earns mostly from advertising and subscriptions unrelated to manufacturing, so it has minimal direct exposure to the index. The connection runs only through the broad economy. The index does not meaningfully drive positioning. | Not meaningfully affected by manufacturing activity. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the ISM Manufacturing Index | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Industrial Production Index | The manufacturing index is a survey of sentiment and direction, while industrial production measures actual physical output, so reading them together confirms whether the survey signal is being validated by hard production data. When both agree, the manufacturing signal is reliable. This pairs the forward-looking survey with the hard-data confirmation. | When the manufacturing index is above 50 AND industrial production is rising, the manufacturing expansion is confirmed and Industrials and Materials deserve overweight positions with confidence. When the index is above 50 but industrial production is flat, the optimism in the survey is not yet showing up in real output, which argues for waiting for confirmation before fully committing to cyclical positions. |
| Yield Curve | The manufacturing index is a coincident-to-leading real-economy gauge, while the yield curve is a forward-looking financial signal, so reading them together confirms whether a manufacturing slowdown is part of a genuine cycle turn that the curve is also forecasting. The two together separate a manufacturing soft patch from a real recession signal. This combines the real-economy and financial-market views of the cycle. | When the manufacturing index falls below 50 AND the yield curve has inverted, both the factory economy and the bond market are signaling contraction, which makes the rotation into Consumer Staples, Health Care, and Utilities urgent. When the index falls below 50 but the curve is still upward sloping, the manufacturing weakness may be a soft patch rather than a recession, which allows holding cyclical positions while monitoring. |
| GDP Growth | The manufacturing index leads GDP, so reading them together shows whether the manufacturing signal is being confirmed by overall economic growth or whether it is leading a turn that GDP has not yet shown. Manufacturing often turns before the broad economy. This positions manufacturing as an early read on the GDP trend. | When the manufacturing index is rising AND GDP growth is accelerating, the cyclical expansion is broad and confirmed, supporting Industrials, Materials, and Energy. When the index is falling but GDP growth is still positive, manufacturing is warning of a slowdown before GDP confirms it, which gives an early signal to begin rotating from cyclical sectors toward defensives. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Which of the five components driving the index (new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries, inventories) is responsible for the change in the headline number, and what does the leading new-orders component say about where manufacturing is heading in the coming months? The new-orders component is the most forward-looking, so a change driven by orders carries more predictive weight than one driven by lagging components.

2. Are the new orders that are driving the index coming from domestic customers, from export markets, or from government purchases, and what does the source say about which part of the economy is generating the demand? Domestic-driven orders point to internal economic strength, while export-driven orders point to global demand that benefits multinational industrials.

3. Is the supplier deliveries component showing that deliveries are slowing (a sign that demand is straining supply chains and the economy is tightening) or speeding up (a sign of slack and weakening demand)? Slowing deliveries during expansion can signal inflationary pressure building, while faster deliveries signal demand softening.

4. Is the inventories component showing that manufacturers are deliberately building stock because they expect higher demand, or are inventories rising because goods are not selling, which would be a warning sign? The reason behind an inventory change determines whether it is a positive signal of confidence or a negative signal of unsold output.

5. Are the regional manufacturing surveys from the various Federal Reserve districts confirming the direction of the national index, or are they diverging in a way that suggests the national reading may be revised? Confirmation across regional surveys strengthens the signal, while divergence suggests caution before acting on the national number.

6. Is the manufacturing index reading consistent with the hard data on factory output and orders, or is there a gap between how purchasing managers feel and what factories are actually producing? Survey sentiment and hard production data sometimes diverge, and the hard data ultimately determines sector earnings.

7. Are global manufacturing indexes from major economies such as China and the euro area moving in the same direction as the US index, and what does a synchronized move versus a divergence say about whether the manufacturing cycle is global or domestic? A synchronized global manufacturing move has stronger implications for multinational industrials and materials than a US-only move.

8. Is the manufacturing employment component showing that firms are hiring (a sign of confidence in sustained demand) or cutting jobs (a sign that firms expect demand to weaken)? Manufacturing employment decisions reflect firms' own forward view of demand, which adds to the signal from new orders.

9. How long has the index been above or below the 50 threshold, and does a sustained move carry more weight than a single month given that one month can be noisy? A sustained run above or below 50 is a more reliable signal of the manufacturing trend than a single reading that could reverse.

10. Is the level of the index consistent with where the broader economy sits in the business cycle, and does a deep contraction below 45 align with other recession signals or stand alone as a manufacturing-specific weakness? A manufacturing contraction confirmed by other recession indicators warrants a full defensive rotation, while an isolated manufacturing weakness may not.
