# Treasury Yields

## Part 1: Definition

This metric tracks the individual interest rates on US government debt at three key maturities: the 3-month Treasury bill, the 2-year Treasury note, and the 10-year Treasury note. Each is expressed as an annual percentage yield, and the data updates continuously through the trading day with daily closing values reported. While the yield curve looks at the relationship between these rates, this metric focuses on the level and direction of each individual rate because each one carries distinct information about a different part of the economy and the policy outlook.

These three rates are tracked because each sits at a different point on the maturity spectrum and reflects different forces. The 3-month rate is included because it tracks the central bank's current policy rate almost exactly, so it is the cleanest market read on what the central bank is doing right now with short-term interest rates. The 2-year rate is included because it reflects the market's expectation of where the central bank will set rates over the next two years, making it the best gauge of the near-term policy path and how much tightening or easing the market expects. The 10-year rate is included because it reflects long-run expectations for growth and inflation plus the extra yield investors demand for holding debt over a long horizon, making it the benchmark that drives mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and the valuation of long-duration assets. Each rate is measured directly from the prices at which these government securities trade in the market, so they represent real-time market consensus rather than survey estimates. Tracking all three together shows how policy expectations, growth expectations, and inflation expectations are evolving across different time horizons.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

These three rates matter for sector investing because they set the cost of money across different time horizons, and that cost directly determines the valuation of every sector while shaping the profitability of rate-sensitive businesses. The 3-month rate reflects current policy and directly affects the funding costs of banks and the returns available on cash, the 2-year rate reflects the expected policy path that influences near-term borrowing decisions, and the 10-year rate drives mortgage rates, corporate bond yields, and the discount rate applied to long-dated earnings. When the 10-year rate rises, it compresses the valuation of long-duration sectors such as Information Technology, Real Estate, and Utilities because their distant earnings are discounted more heavily, while it can help Financials by widening lending margins. When rates fall, the opposite occurs and long-duration sectors benefit from lower discount rates. These rates matter with particular force for Real Estate and Utilities because both are valued like bonds and carry heavy debt, so changes in the 10-year rate move their valuations directly. The combination of setting the economy-wide cost of capital and directly affecting rate-sensitive sectors makes these three rates a core input for sector positioning.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short rates (3-month, 2-year) rising sharply | The central bank is tightening policy, pushing short-term rates up to slow the economy and fight inflation. Rising short rates increase funding costs across the economy and signal that policy is becoming restrictive. Banks can earn more on new loans initially, but the tightening eventually slows borrowing and growth. Rate-sensitive and highly leveraged sectors face rising costs while cash-rich businesses benefit from higher returns on their holdings. | Financials, Energy | Real Estate, Utilities, Consumer Discretionary, Information Technology, Communication Services | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Materials, Industrials |
| 10-year rate rising | Long-term borrowing costs are climbing, usually because the market expects stronger growth, higher inflation, or increased government borrowing. Rising long rates lift mortgage rates and corporate borrowing costs and increase the discount rate applied to distant earnings. Long-duration sectors whose value depends on far-off cash flows are pressured, while sectors with near-term cash flows and pricing power hold up better. | Financials, Energy, Materials | Information Technology, Real Estate, Utilities, Communication Services | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Consumer Discretionary, Industrials |
| Rates broadly falling | Interest rates are declining across maturities, usually because the central bank is easing to support a weakening economy or because the market expects lower growth and inflation. Rate-sensitive sectors and bond-like sectors benefit most, while banks face narrowing lending margins. | Information Technology, Real Estate, Utilities, Communication Services, Consumer Discretionary | Financials, Energy | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Materials, Industrials |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Financials | Banks fund themselves at short-term rates and lend at longer-term rates, so the level and spread of these rates directly drive lending margins. Rising rates can widen margins on new loans, while the relationship between short and long rates determines overall profitability. Higher rates also lift the returns banks earn on their securities portfolios. | Buy when rates are rising with a positive spread between long and short. Avoid when short rates exceed long rates and squeeze margins. |
| Real Estate | Real estate is valued using capitalization rates tied to the 10-year rate, and properties are financed with long-term debt, so rising long rates directly lower property values and raise financing costs. Falling rates do the opposite and lift valuations. The sector is one of the most rate-sensitive in the market. | Buy when the 10-year rate is falling. Avoid when the 10-year rate is rising sharply. |
| Utilities | Utilities carry heavy debt loads and are valued like bonds for their steady dividends, so rising rates raise their borrowing costs and make their fixed payouts less attractive relative to bonds. Falling rates lower their costs and lift their bond-like valuations. The sector trades inversely to long-term rates. | Buy when rates are falling. Avoid when rates are rising sharply. |
| Information Technology | Tech valuations depend heavily on earnings expected far in the future, so the 10-year discount rate strongly affects how those distant earnings are valued today. Rising long rates compress tech valuations, while falling rates expand them. The sector's long-duration profile makes it highly rate-sensitive. | Buy when the 10-year rate is falling or stable. Avoid when the 10-year rate is rising sharply. |
| Communication Services | The long-duration growth companies in this sector share tech's sensitivity to the 10-year discount rate, so rising rates pressure their valuations while falling rates support them. The more stable telecom portion is less sensitive. The sector's growth components track long-term rates. | Buy when rates are falling. Avoid when long rates rise sharply. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Big-ticket discretionary purchases such as homes and autos are financed, so the level of rates affects the cost of that financing and the affordability of large purchases. Rising rates raise financing costs and dampen demand, while falling rates support it. The sector is sensitive to rates through consumer financing. | Buy when rates are falling or stable. Avoid when rates are rising sharply. |
| Energy | Energy companies have near-term cash flows and pricing power that hold up when rates rise on inflation expectations, so the sector is relatively insulated from the discount-rate pressure that hurts long-duration sectors. Energy can perform when rising rates reflect strong growth and inflation. The sector benefits from the conditions that often accompany rising rates. | Buy when rates rise on strong growth and inflation. Less negative than long-duration sectors when rates rise. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples have stable near-term cash flows and modest debt, so they are less sensitive to discount-rate changes than long-duration sectors, though their bond-like dividend appeal gives them some rate sensitivity. The sector's defensive earnings dominate its rate exposure. Rates are a secondary consideration. | Modest sensitivity. Mild benefit from falling rates through dividend appeal. |
| Health Care | Health care has relatively near-term, stable cash flows and strong balance sheets, so the sector is less rate-sensitive than long-duration growth sectors. Its defensive characteristics dominate any rate effect. Rates are a secondary driver for health care. | Low sensitivity. Defensive characteristics dominate rate effects. |
| Materials | Materials companies have near-term cyclical cash flows and pricing power tied to commodities, so they are relatively insulated from discount-rate pressure and can perform when rising rates reflect strong growth. The sector follows the growth cycle more than the rate level. Rates are a secondary consideration. | Modest sensitivity. Can perform when rates rise on strong growth. |
| Industrials | Industrials have a mix of near-term and longer-term cash flows and moderate debt, so they have moderate rate sensitivity but are driven more by the business investment cycle. The sector follows growth conditions more than the rate level directly. Rates are a secondary driver. | Modest sensitivity. Follows the investment cycle more than rates. |

|---|---|---|

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the 3-month rate moving because the central bank is actively changing its policy rate, and if so, is this the beginning, middle, or end of a tightening or easing cycle? The position within the policy cycle matters because early tightening affects sectors differently than late tightening, and the 3-month rate is the cleanest read on the current policy stance.

2. Is the 2-year rate signaling that the market expects the central bank to raise, hold, or cut rates over the next two years, and does that expected path differ from what the central bank itself has communicated? When the market's expected path diverges from the central bank's stated intentions, it signals tension that often resolves with a repricing that moves rate-sensitive sectors.

3. Is the 10-year rate rising or falling because of changes in expected real growth, changes in expected inflation, or changes in the extra compensation investors demand for holding long-term debt? Each component points to different sector implications, with growth-driven moves favoring cyclicals and inflation-driven moves favoring commodity sectors.

4. Is the 10-year rate being held down by heavy buying from foreign central banks, domestic pension funds, or the central bank itself, in a way that disconnects it from the underlying growth and inflation outlook? When non-economic buyers suppress the long rate, its signal about growth and inflation becomes less reliable and its valuation support for long-duration sectors may be artificial.

5. How far have the individual rates moved relative to their levels over the past several years, and are current levels high or low by historical standards in a way that affects how much further they can move? The starting level matters because rates near historical extremes have different room to move and different implications for rate-sensitive sectors.

6. Are the short rates and the long rate moving in the same direction, or are they diverging in a way that signals the market expects current policy to be reversed in the future? Divergence between short and long rates often signals an approaching turning point in the policy cycle that affects when to rotate between sectors.

7. How much debt do the sectors under consideration carry, and how soon must that debt be refinanced at current rates, given that highly leveraged sectors with near-term maturities are most exposed to rising rates? A sector with heavy debt maturing soon faces a direct earnings hit from rising rates, while one with little debt or long-dated fixed-rate debt is more insulated.

8. Are real interest rates (these nominal rates minus expected inflation) positive or negative, and what does that say about whether monetary conditions are genuinely restrictive or still accommodative? A nominal rate that looks high can still be stimulative if inflation is higher, so the real rate is the better gauge of how much pressure rates are putting on rate-sensitive sectors.

9. Are the rates in other major economies moving in the same direction as US rates, or is the US diverging, and what does that say about whether the rate move reflects global or US-specific forces? A globally synchronized rate move points to global conditions, while a US-specific move points to domestic policy or growth factors, which affects multinational and domestic sectors differently.

10. Is the change in rates large enough and durable enough to meaningfully affect the valuations and borrowing costs of rate-sensitive sectors, or is it a short-term fluctuation that will not change the fundamental picture? Only sustained and meaningful rate moves warrant a sector rotation, since rate-sensitive sectors can absorb minor temporary fluctuations.
