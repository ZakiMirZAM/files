# AAII Asset Allocation Survey

## Part 1: Definition

The AAII Asset Allocation Survey measures how members of the American Association of Individual Investors have actually divided their own portfolios across three destinations: stocks and stock funds, bonds and bond funds, and cash such as savings accounts, money market funds and certificates of deposit. Each reading is a percentage of the reported portfolio, the three shares total one hundred percent, and the survey is published monthly. The record begins in November 1987 and runs without a break, which means it covers four recessions, the 2000 market peak and the 2009 market bottom. Roughly six hundred members respond in a typical month.

The survey is built by asking members what they currently hold rather than what they expect the market to do. The stock share combines stock funds with individually held stocks, and both are included because the destination of the money matters more than the wrapper it arrives in. The bond share combines bond funds with individual bonds for the same reason. The cash share is included as the third leg because it is the only one of the three that represents purchasing power not yet committed, which is what makes the survey more than a description of the past. Because the three shares must total one hundred percent, no share can be read on its own: a rising stock share is always funded by a falling bond share, a falling cash share, or both, and identifying which one moved is the substance of the analysis. The survey is interpreted as a positioning gauge, and its contrarian value comes from the tendency of individual investors to hold the most stock after prices have already risen and the most cash after prices have already fallen. Across the full record the shares average roughly 62 percent stocks, 16 percent bonds and 22 percent cash, and those averages are the reference points against which any month should be read.

The distinction between this survey and a sentiment poll is the reason to carry both. A sentiment poll records an opinion that costs nothing to hold and can reverse in a week. This survey records money that has already moved, which is slower, harder to reverse and therefore more informative about how much buying power is left.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Asset Allocation Survey matters for sector investing because it measures the fuel available to push prices higher rather than the mood of the people who might supply it. When the cash share falls to an extreme low, households have already committed their money, and the marginal buyer who would have to bid stocks higher has largely spent. That condition has historically preceded weak returns, and it argues for rotating toward the defensive sectors that lose least when the bid fades. When the cash share climbs to an extreme high, the selling has largely happened and a reservoir of purchasing power sits on the sidelines waiting for a reason to return, which is the condition under which high-beta cyclical and growth sectors lead the recovery. The record supports the pattern: the stock share peaked at 77.0 percent in January 2000 at the top of the dot-com market, and bottomed at 40.8 percent in March 2009, the same month the cash share peaked at 44.8 percent, which was the exact month the market turned. Across the four recessions in the record the stock share averages 54.8 percent against 62.9 percent during expansions, so the series carries a genuine cyclical signal and not only a signal at turning points. The metric is most useful at extremes and says little in its normal band, so it works as a positioning check on a sector call rather than as the reason for one. Its value is that it answers a question no price-based indicator can answer, which is whether the money required to sustain a move still exists.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the AAII Asset Allocation Survey | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Bull-Bear Spread (AAII) | The Bull-Bear Spread records what the same population of individual investors says, while the Asset Allocation Survey records what they have actually done with their money. Reading them together separates an opinion from a commitment, which is the single most useful cross-check available on either one. Words are cheap and reverse quickly, positioning is costly and reverses slowly. | When investors say they are bearish but the survey shows them still holding a record stock share and no cash, the pessimism is talk and the buying power is still gone, so the defensive call stands and the stated bearishness should be ignored. When they say they are bearish and the survey confirms they have actually raised cash to an extreme, both the mood and the money agree, and the contrarian case for high-beta cyclicals and technology is at its strongest. |
| Shiller CAPE (PE10) | The survey measures how much money is committed, while the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio measures what that money paid. Reading them together separates a crowded position at a fair price from a crowded position at an expensive one. Positioning explains the flow, valuation explains the consequence. | When the stock share is extreme AND the CAPE is far above its long-run mean, households are fully committed to an expensive market with no reserve left to defend it, which is the most vulnerable configuration and the strongest case for staples, health care and utilities. When cash is extreme AND the CAPE is below its mean, a large reserve faces a cheap market, which is the strongest case for the cyclical and growth sectors that lead off a bottom. |
| Fed Funds Rate | Cash is not only a residual, it is an asset with a yield, so the level of short rates determines whether a high cash share reflects fear or simply reflects cash paying enough to be worth holding. Reading them together prevents misreading a rational allocation as a capitulation. This distinction decides whether the reserve is waiting to buy or content to stay put. | When the cash share is high AND short rates are high, the reserve is being paid to wait and may not return quickly, which weakens the contrarian case and keeps the utilities call alive since cash competes with utility yields for the same money. When the cash share is high AND short rates are low, the reserve is earning nothing and is under pressure to move into risk assets, which strengthens the case for high-beta cyclicals and technology. |
| VIX | The survey moves monthly and shows a slow position, while the volatility index moves intraday and shows the fear priced into options right now. Reading them together shows whether a capitulation in positioning has been confirmed by a genuine panic. A slow gauge and a fast gauge agreeing is a stronger signal than either alone. | When cash is far above average AND the volatility index is spiking, households have raised a reserve into a genuine panic, which is the classic bottom configuration and the strongest case for adding cyclicals and technology. When cash is high but volatility is calm, the reserve reflects a considered decision rather than fear, which is a weaker contrarian signal and argues for waiting. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Which of the three shares actually moved to produce the current reading, given that the three must total one hundred percent and a rising stock share can be funded by selling bonds, by spending cash, or by both? A stock share that rose because cash was spent means purchasing power was consumed, while a stock share that rose because bonds were sold means the reserve is still intact, and only the first is a warning.

2. Is the reading at a genuine historical extreme against the long-run averages of roughly 62 percent stocks, 16 percent bonds and 22 percent cash, or merely somewhere in its normal band where the survey carries no contrarian information? The metric is only informative at extremes, so a reading inside the normal band should not influence a sector decision at all.

3. Did the allocation change because investors actively bought or sold, or because the market moved and revalued the holdings they already had? A rising stock share produced by a rally rather than by purchases is arithmetic rather than a decision, and it reveals nothing about conviction or remaining buying power.

4. Is the current cash share explained by the level of short-term interest rates rather than by fear, given that cash paying a meaningful yield is a rational holding and not a capitulation? A high cash share against high short rates may persist indefinitely and does not carry the same contrarian weight as a high cash share against a zero yield.

5. Does the survey agree with the Bull-Bear Spread from the same organization, and if it does not, which one should be believed? Stated pessimism alongside a record stock allocation means the money contradicts the mood, and the money is the more reliable witness because it has already been committed.

6. How does the current reading compare with the same survey at the recognized extremes of 77.0 percent stocks in January 2000 and 40.8 percent stocks with 44.8 percent cash in March 2009? Anchoring the present against the two readings whose outcomes are known is more informative than comparing it against a rolling average alone.

7. Has the allocation drifted structurally over the decades rather than cycled, given that the stock share has averaged progressively more in each decade of the record? A reading that looks extreme against the full history since 1987 may be ordinary against the last fifteen years, and mistaking a structural drift for a cyclical extreme produces a false signal.

8. Is the survey population representative of the investors who actually set prices, given that it samples self-directed individual members rather than the institutions that own most of the market? The survey describes one identifiable group of investors, and its readings should be weighted accordingly rather than treated as the market's aggregate position.

9. How quickly did the allocation reach its current level, since a sharp move into cash over a few months indicates a decisive reaction to an identifiable event, while a slow drift indicates a gradual change of view? The speed of the shift affects how likely it is to reverse and therefore how tradeable the signal is.

10. Is the reading extreme enough and persistent enough to act on, or is it a single month that could revert before any sector rotation could be executed? A single extreme print is not a position, and only a reading that holds for several months justifies moving a sector allocation on the strength of this survey.
