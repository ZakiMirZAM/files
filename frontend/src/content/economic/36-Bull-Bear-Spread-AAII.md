# Bull-Bear Spread (AAII)

## Part 1: Definition

The Bull-Bear Spread from the American Association of Individual Investors measures the mood of individual investors by subtracting the percentage who expect the stock market to fall (bears) from the percentage who expect it to rise (bulls). It is expressed in percentage points, where a positive number means optimists outnumber pessimists and a negative number means pessimists outnumber optimists. The data comes from a weekly survey of individual investors and is released weekly. The spread is most useful as a contrarian signal, since extreme optimism often appears near market tops and extreme pessimism near market bottoms.

The spread is constructed from a weekly poll that asks individual investors whether they feel bullish, bearish, or neutral about the direction of the stock market over the next six months. The bullish percentage is included because it captures the share of investors who expect rising prices and are likely already positioned for gains, which matters because widespread optimism can mean buying power is already committed. The bearish percentage is included because it captures the share expecting declines, which matters because widespread pessimism can mean selling has largely occurred and cash is waiting to be deployed. Subtracting the bearish from the bullish percentage produces the spread, which is included as the key figure because the balance between optimists and pessimists reveals the prevailing mood more clearly than either reading alone. The spread is interpreted as a sentiment gauge, and its value as a contrarian signal comes from the tendency of individual investors as a group to be most optimistic after prices have already risen and most pessimistic after prices have already fallen, which means extremes often precede reversals.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Bull-Bear Spread matters for sector investing because it reveals the prevailing mood of individual investors, which at extremes serves as a contrarian signal for the overall risk environment and the rotation between aggressive and defensive sectors. When the spread reaches extreme optimism, it often signals that buying power is already committed and the market is vulnerable to a pullback, which favors trimming aggressive cyclical and growth sectors and adding defensives. When the spread reaches extreme pessimism, it often signals that selling has largely occurred and the market is positioned for a rebound, which favors adding aggressive cyclical and growth sectors that lead in recoveries. The metric matters because sentiment extremes have historically coincided with turning points, giving a contrarian edge for timing the rotation between risk-on and risk-off positioning. However, the spread is most useful at extremes and provides little signal in its normal range, so it functions as a supplementary timing tool rather than a primary driver. The combination of a contrarian signal at extremes and a read on the prevailing risk mood makes the spread a useful but secondary input for sector rotation timing.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the sentiment reading at a genuine historical extreme that has reliably preceded market reversals, or merely elevated or depressed within its normal range where it provides little contrarian signal? The contrarian value of the spread comes only at true extremes, so a reading in the normal range should not drive sector decisions.

2. Is the extreme sentiment being driven by recent market price action, where investors become bullish after gains and bearish after losses, which is exactly the pattern that makes the signal contrarian? When sentiment simply follows recent prices, it confirms the crowd is positioned for a continuation that often reverses.

3. Do individual investors' stated views match their actual positioning, or are they expressing a mood that they have not acted on, which would weaken the signal? Sentiment that is backed by actual positioning carries more weight than a stated mood that has not translated into buying or selling.

4. Is the individual investor sentiment confirmed by other sentiment measures such as professional surveys and options positioning, or is it an isolated reading that may not reflect the broader market mood? Confirmation across sentiment measures strengthens the contrarian signal, while an isolated extreme is less reliable.

5. Is the extreme pessimism occurring against a backdrop of genuine economic weakness, where the pessimism may be justified, or against a healthy economy, where it is more likely a contrarian buying opportunity? Pessimism justified by real weakness is less of a contrarian signal than pessimism disconnected from a healthy economy.

6. How quickly did sentiment reach the current extreme, since a rapid swing to pessimism after a sharp decline often marks a more actionable bottom than a slow drift? The speed of the sentiment swing affects how reliably it marks a turning point.

7. Is the sentiment extreme consistent with where the market sits relative to its recent highs or lows, given that extreme pessimism near recent lows is more meaningful than extreme pessimism after a long decline that may continue? The market's price level relative to the sentiment extreme affects the contrarian read.

8. Are there specific events or news driving the sentiment extreme that could persist and justify the mood, or is the extreme a general overreaction likely to fade? Event-driven sentiment may persist while the event remains unresolved, whereas a general overreaction is more likely to reverse.

9. Is the survey sample representative of the broader investor base, or could it be skewed by the particular group of individual investors who respond? Because the survey reflects a specific group, its representativeness affects how much weight to place on the reading.

10. Is the sentiment extreme reaching a level and persistence that warrants acting on the contrarian signal, or is it a brief spike that could reverse before a market turn materializes? Only a genuine and persistent extreme warrants a contrarian sector shift, since brief sentiment spikes can reverse without a market turn.
