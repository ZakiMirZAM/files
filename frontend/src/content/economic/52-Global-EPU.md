# Global Economic Policy Uncertainty (GEPU)

## Part 1: Definition

The Global Economic Policy Uncertainty index measures how much uncertainty about economic policy exists across the world's major economies. It is built by Baker, Bloom, and Davis by counting how often newspapers in each country discuss the economy, policy, and uncertainty together, turning that frequency into a national index, and then combining the national indices into a single global figure weighted by each country's share of world output. The GDP-weighted average means that uncertainty in large economies such as the United States, China, and the major European countries moves the global index more than uncertainty in small economies. The index is expressed as a level relative to its own history, where higher numbers mean greater policy uncertainty, and it is published monthly. Because it is based on what newspapers actually report rather than on forecasts, it captures the climate of policy uncertainty as it is being experienced.

The index is constructed by measuring the share of news coverage devoted to economic-policy uncertainty in each country, normalizing those shares into national indices, and aggregating them with current GDP weights so the global figure reflects where the world's economic activity is concentrated. A rising level is interpreted as a deteriorating policy climate in which governments, central banks, trade rules, or fiscal paths are unusually unpredictable. High and rising readings are associated with businesses delaying investment and hiring, households deferring big purchases, and investors demanding more compensation for risk, because uncertainty about the rules of the game makes commitments harder to plan.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Global EPU matters for sector investing because policy uncertainty shapes the willingness of businesses and investors around the world to commit capital, which determines whether risk-on, globally exposed sectors or defensive, domestically stable sectors are favored. When global policy uncertainty is low, the climate is predictable and supports a risk-on environment where cyclical, trade-exposed, and growth sectors can lead, because firms invest and expand confidently. When global policy uncertainty is high, the climate is unpredictable and pressures globally exposed cyclicals first, because cross-border investment, trade, and supply chains are most disrupted by unstable policy, while defensive sectors with stable domestic demand hold up better. The metric matters with particular force for globally exposed sectors such as Information Technology, Industrials, and Materials, whose multinational revenues and capital plans are most sensitive to worldwide policy conditions, and it provides useful context for defensive sectors that are more insulated from global policy swings. Because the index is global and GDP-weighted, it is most informative about sectors with large international footprints.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside the Global EPU | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| US Economic Policy Uncertainty | The US daily EPU is a faster, country-specific version of the same idea, so reading them together shows whether elevated global uncertainty is driven by the US or by other major economies. | When both the global and US indices rise together, the uncertainty is broad and globally exposed cyclicals face the most pressure. When the global index rises while the US index is calm, the source is abroad, which points to non-US exposures as the main risk and may leave domestically focused US sectors more insulated. |
| Geopolitical Risk Index | The GPR captures conflict and geopolitical tension while the EPU captures economic-policy uncertainty, so reading them together distinguishes policy-driven uncertainty from conflict-driven risk. | When both are elevated, the risk environment is severe and defensive positioning is warranted across globally exposed sectors. When the EPU is high but the GPR is calm, the issue is policy unpredictability rather than conflict, which favors stable domestic sectors over a broad geopolitical hedge such as Energy. |
| VIX | The EPU measures the policy-uncertainty climate while the VIX measures current market fear, so reading them together shows whether elevated policy uncertainty has yet been priced into market volatility. | When the EPU is high but the VIX is low, policy risk is building during market complacency, a setup that favors adding defensives before volatility rises. When both are elevated, fear and policy uncertainty reinforce each other, strengthening the case for defensive positioning. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the elevated global index driven by a few large economies, given the GDP weighting, or is the uncertainty broad-based across many countries?

2. Is the source of the global uncertainty the United States or another major economy, since that determines which regional exposures within sectors are most at risk?

3. Is the policy uncertainty about a specific, resolvable event such as an election, or about an open-ended structural issue that may keep the index elevated for a long time?

4. Has the high reading begun to show up in real activity such as falling global investment and trade, or is it still confined to news coverage?

5. Is the uncertainty primarily about monetary policy, fiscal policy, trade policy, or regulation, since each affects different sectors with different force?

6. Is the index elevated alongside the geopolitical risk index, indicating conflict-driven uncertainty, or is it elevated on its own as a purely policy-driven phenomenon?

7. How does the current level compare with past episodes, and did those episodes precede meaningful weakness in globally exposed cyclical sectors?

8. Is the rise sustained over several months or a short-lived spike that markets are likely to look through?

9. Are globally exposed sectors already discounting the uncertainty in their valuations, or is the risk not yet reflected in prices?

10. Is the uncertainty being accompanied by a flight to safety in bonds and the dollar that would compound the pressure on risk-on, globally exposed sectors?
