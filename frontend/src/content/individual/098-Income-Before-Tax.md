# Income Before Tax

## Part 1: Definition

Income before tax is the profit a company earns after every cost except income tax. The figure is also called pretax income or EBT, short for earnings before tax. The income statement reaches this line by taking operating income and adding the non-operating items, which are the gains and losses from outside the main business. Operating income is the profit from the core business before interest and tax. Income before tax is the profit that belongs to the owners and the tax authority together, one step above the final bottom line.

In the common-size table the line's share of revenue is the pretax margin. Pretax margin is pretax profit divided by revenue. The line has two useful roles. Pretax income is the base on which the tax rate is figured, so it is the denominator of the tax burden ratio. Pretax income is also the cleanest profit figure for comparing companies in different countries, because tax rules have not yet changed the number.

Common readings of the line:

| Pretax margin behavior | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Close to the operating margin | Non-operating items are small. The pretax result is the operating result. |
| Below the operating margin | Net interest cost or other non-operating losses pulled the figure down. |
| Above the operating margin | Net interest income or non-operating gains lifted the figure. |
| Swinging year to year | One-time non-operating items are moving the line. Single years are not typical. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The line stacks the figures above it on the income statement:

`Income Before Tax = Operating Income + Total Other Income Expenses Net`

Operating income is the profit from the core business after the cost of goods sold and operating expenses, but before interest and tax. The non-operating line is the net of items outside operations, mainly interest income, interest expense, investment gains and losses, and asset-sale gains. Adding the two gives income before tax. In the common-size table the rows above sum down to this line.

Income before tax then becomes the base for the tax calculation. Income tax expense is applied to it, and net income is what remains after the tax is subtracted. Net income is the final line on the income statement. The pretax line itself adds no new information beyond its two sources. The whole reading is deciding how much of any move came from the operating margin and how much came from the non-operating items. The value of the line is its position, sitting after financing has entered through net interest but before tax rules differ by country.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Investors use pretax income for what its position offers. As the base for the tax rate, the line anchors the tax analysis, because the effective tax rate is tax expense divided by this line. Effective tax rate is the actual rate the company paid as a share of pretax profit. The durability of that rate matters for valuing the earnings.

As the profit figure before tax rules apply, pretax income compares operations across countries. Two companies' pretax margins differ only by business and financing, not by where each company is taxed. A reader comparing a domestic firm with a multinational gets a fairer read at the pretax line than at the after-tax bottom line.

Pretax income also feeds the interest burden factor in the DuPont breakdown of return on equity. Interest burden is pretax income divided by operating income, and it prices the cost of the financing layer. Comparing the line's growth against net income's growth isolates the effect of the tax layer. Comparing its growth against operating income's growth isolates the effect of the non-operating items.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Everything that moves operating income moves this line, because operating income is the larger of its two sources. Revenue, cost of goods sold, and operating expenses all flow up into operating income and then into the pretax figure. The non-operating items add their own movement, mainly net interest cost and the one-time gains and losses that arrive in particular years.

The line's swings blend the two sources. Operating leverage amplifies the operating side, meaning a small revenue change produces a larger profit change. The non-operating side adds spikes from asset sales and investment results. Nothing moves the line on its own, because the line is a sum. Its analysis is the analysis of its parts. The value of the figure is where it sits on the statement, not any new signal it produces.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The pretax margin map across sectors is the operating margin map adjusted for financing. Sectors that carry heavy debt show pretax margins below their operating margins, because net interest cost eats the gap. Cash-rich sectors can show the opposite, with net interest income lifting the pretax margin above the operating margin.

| Industry type | Typical pretax versus operating margin | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and capital-heavy operators | Pretax below operating | Large debt loads create a steady net interest cost |
| Cash-rich technology | Pretax near or above operating | Large cash balances earn interest that lifts the line |
| Highly indebted companies | Pretax well below operating | Heavy interest expense narrows the result |
| Multinationals | Best compared at this line | Tax rules differ by country, so the pretax figure is the fair comparison |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | The operating constructs above this line do not apply to financial firms |

A cross-sector comparison of pretax margins is meaningless when the businesses carry very different debt loads, because the non-operating layer distorts the figure differently in each case.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the line's own multi-year series first, then read its pretax-margin series. The margin's path splits into the operating margin's path and the non-operating items' path. The reading assigns every move to one source or the other. The line's trend against net income's trend reads the drift in the tax layer, where divergences mark tax-rate changes and one-time tax items.

The line's trend against operating income's trend reads the non-operating layer, where the gaps show the path of net interest cost and the one-time punctuations. The pretax line itself trends as its parts do, so it produces no signal of its own. A one-time gain in a single year is not a trend, and it should be set aside when reading the underlying path. The value of the line is the breakdown its position allows, separating the financing layer from the tax layer.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Income

**Why read the two together.** Operating income is the profit from the core business before non-operating items and tax. Income before tax is operating income plus the non-operating line. Reading the two together shows how much of the pretax result came from the operations and how much came from outside them. The operating line is the repeatable source and the gap between the two lines is everything non-operating.

**What the combination tells you.** A pretax figure that tracks operating income closely means the non-operating items are quiet and the result is durable. A pretax figure that diverged from operating income means net interest or one-time items moved it, and that movement may not repeat. Across the years the gap between the two lines shows whether profit growth is operating or non-operating. Durable growth comes from the operating side.

### Income Tax Expense

**Why read the two together.** Income tax expense is the tax charged on this pretax line. Pretax income is the base the tax is figured on. Reading the two together gives the effective tax rate, which is tax expense divided by pretax income. The pretax line is the denominator and the tax line is the numerator of that rate.

**What the combination tells you.** A pretax figure that grew faster than net income means the tax layer absorbed part of the gain, so the effective rate rose. A pretax figure and a net income that grew in step means the tax rate held steady. Watching the two across the years shows whether the company keeps a stable share of its pretax profit after tax. A rising tax rate quietly eats earnings growth.

### Tax Burden

**Why read the two together.** Tax burden is the DuPont factor that measures the share of pretax profit kept after tax, figured as net income divided by pretax income. Pretax income is the denominator of that factor. Reading the two together connects the pretax result to how much of it the owners actually keep. The pretax line sets the base and tax burden measures the keep-rate on it.

**What the combination tells you.** A high tax burden factor means most of the pretax profit survives as net income, which points to a low effective tax rate. A falling tax burden factor with steady pretax income means tax is taking a larger share each year. Together the pretax line and the tax burden factor show whether the path from pretax to net income is stable or eroding. A durable keep-rate supports the valuation of the earnings.

## Part 8: Limitations

The line is a sum, so it adds no information beyond its two sources, and every move in its margin belongs to either the operating side or the non-operating side. One-time non-operating items distort single years, which makes any single reading unreliable until the items are stripped out. The line must always be read through its parts rather than on its own.

The pretax figure is also an imperfect base for cash-tax analysis. The accounting profit it shows differs from the taxable profit the tax authority uses, because some items are recognized at different times in the two systems. The deferred-tax accounting carries that gap. The line's strengths come from its position rather than from any signal it generates. The line serves as the tax base and as the cross-country comparison point. The line is the second-to-last figure on the income statement, read through its components and used for where it sits.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How does the pretax margin's path split between the operating margin and the non-operating items across the years?
2. What is the effective tax rate figured on this base, and is that rate stable or drifting?
3. How does comparing companies at the pretax line re-rank peers whose after-tax margins differ mainly because of tax rules?
4. Which years carry one-time non-operating items, and are those years set aside when reading the trend?
5. How has the line's path diverged from net income's path, and what tax-rate change or item explains the gap?
6. How does net interest cost compare across the indebted peers, showing the financing toll at this line?
7. What did the line show in the worst year on the chart, and what does that trough say about the company's safety?
8. Is the line's growth converting into net income at a steady rate, or is the tax layer absorbing it?
9. For a multinational, which countries produce the pretax result, based on the segment disclosures?
10. What pretax margin do the valuation assumptions imply, and do the operating and non-operating parts support it?
