# Equity Reinvestment Rate

## Part 1: Definition

The equity reinvestment rate measures the share of profit that shareholders leave invested in the business after the effect of borrowing is removed. Firm-wide reinvestment counts everything spent on growth, but part of that spending is paid for with new debt rather than with shareholders' money. The equity reinvestment rate strips the debt-funded part out, so what remains is the reinvestment that actually falls on the equity holders.

The rate answers one question: how much of net income do shareholders truly plow back? The rate drives the equity-track growth formula, where growth equals the equity reinvestment rate times the noncash return on equity. The engine aggregates the rate over up to five years, because a single year of reinvestment is too lumpy to read.

| Rate level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.20 | Shareholders fund little growth. Most profit is free to be paid out. |
| 0.20 to 0.60 | A typical reinvesting company. Growth and payout share the profit. |
| 0.60 to 1.00 | Heavy reinvestment. Most profit stays in the business. |
| Above 1.00 | Reinvestment exceeds profit. The excess is funded by new share issues, which dilute existing holders. |
| Negative | New borrowing or asset sales exceed the reinvestment. The equity holders are taking capital out. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Equity Reinvestment Rate = [(CapEx − Depreciation) + Change In Noncash Working Capital − Net New Debt] ÷ Net Income`

The top of the ratio builds up equity reinvestment in three pieces. CapEx minus depreciation is net capital spending, meaning investment in equipment beyond what merely replaces wear. The change in noncash working capital is the money newly tied up in inventory and receivables net of payables, with cash itself excluded. Net new debt is the year's change in total debt, measured as the balance-sheet difference between this year's total debt and last year's. Subtracting net new debt removes the part of the reinvestment that lenders paid for. When a year's debt change is unavailable the engine approximates instead, scaling the reinvestment by the equity share of book capital and labeling the result.

Net income is the bottom of the ratio because the question is equity-side. Both top and bottom are summed over up to five recent years before dividing, so one heavy investment year cannot set the rate.

The rate is deliberately uncapped. A rate above 100 percent is real and meaningful: shareholders are reinvesting more than the company earns, and the difference is funded by issuing new shares.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The equity-track valuation needs a growth rate that is consistent with its own cash flows. The cash flow being discounted is what is left for shareholders after equity reinvestment, so the growth rate must come from that same reinvestment. Using the plain retention ratio instead would mix a dividend concept into a cash-flow model and misstate both.

The rate also exposes who pays for growth. Two companies can report identical capital spending while one funds it with debt and the other with retained profit. Only the equity reinvestment rate separates the two, and the difference decides how much cash shareholders can ever take out.

A rate above one carries a specific message that no other metric states plainly: growth is being bought with dilution. Dilution means existing shareholders own a smaller slice after new shares are issued.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Capital spending plans move the top directly, and so does the working-capital cycle. A year of inventory build raises the rate while a year of working-capital release lowers it. Debt policy cuts the other way: a large refinancing or a new term loan raises net new debt, which lowers the equity rate even when total investment is unchanged.

The denominator swings with earnings. A weak profit year inflates the rate mechanically because the same reinvestment is divided by a smaller base. Aggregation over the window softens all of these effects but cannot remove a sustained shift. Data availability matters too, since years without a usable debt change fall back to the book-capital approximation.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Young growth companies | Often above 1.00 | Investment outruns profit, and the gap is filled with share issues. |
| Mature manufacturers | 0.20 to 0.60 | Steady net investment funded comfortably from earnings. |
| Utilities and telecom | Low equity rate despite heavy capex | Much of the spending is debt-funded, and the subtraction removes it. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Debt is raw material for a financial company, so the debt subtraction loses its meaning. The app values financials on dividends instead. |
| Declining businesses | Near zero or negative | Little net investment, and borrowing or disposals can exceed it. |

Comparing the rate across sectors without noting debt policy is meaningless, because the same physical investment produces very different equity rates under different funding.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A stable rate across windows describes a settled funding policy. A rate climbing toward and past one warns that the growth plan has outgrown internal funding, and share issuance will follow if it has not already. A falling rate at a maturing company is healthy, since fading growth should need less reinvestment.

Single-year spikes deserve suspicion rather than extrapolation. An acquisition, a disposal, or a working-capital swing can distort one year badly. That distortion is exactly why the engine aggregates. Compare the latest window against the prior one instead of reading year-to-year wiggles.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Reinvestment Rate

**Why read the two together.** The firm-side reinvestment rate measures total reinvestment against after-tax operating profit, with no adjustment for funding. The equity reinvestment rate removes the debt-funded share. The gap between the two is precisely the role debt plays in paying for growth.

**What the combination tells you.** Similar values mean growth is equity-funded and the capital structure is stable. A firm rate far above the equity rate means lenders are financing the expansion, which raises future fixed charges. An equity rate above the firm rate means debt is being repaid out of profit at the same time as the company invests.

### FCFE intrinsic value

**Why read the two together.** The FCFE valuation discounts the cash left for shareholders after equity reinvestment, so this rate sits inside every projected cash flow of that model. The growth rate of the model is this rate times the noncash return on equity. The valuation and the rate must be consistent or the model contradicts itself.

**What the combination tells you.** A high rate lowers near-term equity cash flows but funds the growth that raises later ones, and the valuation shows the net effect. A rate above one produces negative projected cash flows in some years, and those negative present values are the dilution cost of the coming share issues. A low rate with high assumed growth is the inconsistency to hunt for.

### Common Stock Issued

**Why read the two together.** Common stock issued is the cash raised from selling new shares, taken straight from the financing section of the cash flow statement. A rate above 100 percent predicts exactly this line, because reinvestment beyond earnings must be funded somewhere. The line item is the direct test of the prediction.

**What the combination tells you.** A rate above one alongside heavy issuance confirms that growth is dilution-funded, and the question becomes whether the projects earn more than the cost of the new equity. A rate above one without issuance means cash reserves or hidden borrowing are filling the gap instead. Persistent issuance with a modest rate suggests shares are being issued for compensation rather than for growth funding.

## Part 8: Limitations

The debt input is a balance-sheet difference rather than a true issuance flow, so a debt repayment and a new borrowing in the same year net against each other and vanish. Currency movements and acquired debt also land in that difference without representing funding decisions. Years that fall back to the book-capital approximation are labeled but are still an approximation.

The denominator is net income with all of its accounting noise. A window that includes a large writedown understates earnings and overstates the rate. The rate also says nothing about the quality of the reinvestment: a rate of 80 percent is admirable at a high return on equity and destructive at a low one, and the rate alone cannot tell the two apart.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is driving the current rate: net capital spending, a working-capital build, or a swing in net new debt?
2. Is the rate above 100 percent, and if so, does the share-issuance line confirm that new equity is funding the excess?
3. How different is the equity rate from the firm-side reinvestment rate, and what does the gap say about reliance on lenders?
4. Which years in the window used the book-capital approximation instead of the measured debt change?
5. Did an acquisition or disposal distort the window, and what does the rate look like with that year set aside?
6. What return does the reinvested equity earn, and does the rate times the noncash ROE match the growth the valuation uses?
7. Is the rate rising while the return on equity falls, meaning more money is chasing worse projects?
8. For a company with a negative rate, is capital being returned deliberately or is the business shrinking involuntarily?
9. How would the rate change if working capital normalized to its five-year average share of revenue?
