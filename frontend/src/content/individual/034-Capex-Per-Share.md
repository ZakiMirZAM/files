# Capex Per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Capex per share measures how many dollars of each share's claim on the year's cash were reinvested in physical productive assets. Capital expenditures, often shortened to capex, is the cash a company spends on long-lived physical assets during the year. Those assets include property, plant, equipment, and infrastructure. Capex is reported in the investing section of the cash flow statement, which is the financial report that tracks cash moving in and out of the business. Shares outstanding is the total number of ownership units currently held by investors. The metric answers one question: how much of each share's share of the year's cash went back into the assets the business runs on?

Capex per share separates into two kinds of spending that the reported line does not label. Maintenance capex is the spending needed to keep current operations running. Growth capex is the spending used to expand the business. The cash flow statement records capex as a cash outflow with a minus sign. This app shows capex as a positive number so the per-share figure reads as a dollar amount of reinvestment.

| Capex per share level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Low relative to per-share cash flow | The business reinvests little in physical assets. The model may be capital light, or the company may be holding back spending. |
| Moderate relative to per-share cash flow | A normal reinvestment pace. Often close to the wear-and-tear charge, which suggests the company is mostly maintaining. |
| High relative to per-share cash flow | A large part of each share's cash is going into assets. This points to a building phase or to a business that is heavy on equipment by nature. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Capex Per Share = Capital Expenditures ÷ Shares Outstanding`

Capital expenditures is the top number. Capital expenditures is the cash the company spent during the year buying and improving long-lived physical assets. The figure is taken from the investing section of the cash flow statement. The line is usually labeled investment in property, plant, and equipment. The figure measures cash actually paid out this year, not an accounting estimate.

Shares outstanding is the bottom number. Shares outstanding is the total count of ownership units held by investors at the measurement date. Dividing the spending by the share count spreads the total reinvestment bill across the owners who funded it. The result states the spending in per-share terms so it can be set beside earnings per share and cash flow per share.

Two versions of the top number exist. Gross capex is the full amount the company spent. Net capex subtracts depreciation, which is the yearly accounting charge that spreads the cost of past asset purchases over their useful life. Net capex therefore measures spending above the level needed to replace assets as they wear out. This app uses the gross capex line straight from the cash flow statement.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Capital intensity quietly separates good businesses from weak ones, and capex per share is where that intensity shows up in owner units. Two companies can report the same earnings per share while one must pour half its cash into machinery just to stand still and the other needs almost nothing. The first company's earnings are partly an accounting appearance. The second company's earnings are nearly all available to the owner.

Capex per share also reads the investment cycle directly. The income statement smooths the cost of assets over many years through depreciation, so the income statement hides when the real spending happens. Capex per share prints the spending in the year the cash leaves. A building phase, a pause, and a harvest phase each appear here before they appear anywhere else.

Free cash flow depends on this line. Free cash flow is the cash left from operations after the capex needed to run the business is paid. Free cash flow is the owner's true take, equal to sales minus operating costs minus the capex required to maintain the business. Because the valuation subtracts this exact spending, the future path of capex per share is one of the most consequential assumptions in the model, and its history is the evidence that assumption must answer to.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Capital expenditures moves with the investment cycle. Expansion programs, waves of equipment replacement, and shifts to new technology all push the number up. Less voluntary forces move it too. Regulatory mandates can require spending. Inflation in the price of equipment and construction raises the cost of the same physical renewal each cycle. Competitive pressure can force matching spending when rivals add capacity.

What does not pass through this line matters just as much. Acquisitions buy assets through a different line of the cash flow statement. Leases rent assets through the operating section instead of buying them. Outsourcing pushes asset spending onto suppliers. A company can lighten this line without lightening its real dependence on assets. The share count adds its own effect. Buybacks shrink the share count and raise capex per share even when total spending does not change. Single years are lumpy because large projects land in one year, so the multi-year trend is the better unit of analysis.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Capex per share is a sector signature before it is a company signal. The right level depends almost entirely on the kind of business.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-intensive (utilities, telecom, semiconductors, miners, railroads) | High capex per share by nature | The competitive position is literally built from this spending, so the assets must be funded continually. |
| Capital-light (software, services) | Very low capex per share | Growth spending flows through expensed research and marketing instead, so little reaches the capex line. |
| Cyclical industrials | Capex swings with the cycle | Companies add capacity when demand is strong and freeze spending when demand falls. |
| High-return businesses | High capex can still be good | Heavy spending is welcome when the new assets earn a return above the cost of the capital. |

High capex per share is not automatically bad. Spending is good when the assets earn strong returns and bad when they do not. For capital-intensive companies, compare against depreciation to see who is building, who is maintaining, and who is quietly underinvesting. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two businesses have different asset models, because a capital-light company will always sit far below a capital-heavy one for reasons that say nothing about quality.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The per-share trends chart shows the investment rhythm in owner units, and the shapes matter more than the levels. A steady level usually means a maintenance pace, where spending roughly replaces assets as they wear out. A multi-year ramp marks a growth or capacity-expansion phase, and the test is whether later years show the revenue and free cash flow the spending was meant to produce. A build that never converts into growth is capital consumed, the worst pattern on this page.

A sharp drop has two readings the chart alone cannot separate. The drop can mean a large project finished and released the cash, or it can mean dangerous underinvestment that borrows from future revenue. The Capex / Depreciation companion and the age of the asset base help decide between them. Big single projects make the line lumpy, so one high or low year is rarely the real trend. Share-count changes can also move the per-share figure even when total capex is flat, so check whether buybacks rather than spending explain a rise.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capex, so capex per share is one of the two numbers that build it. Operating cash flow is the cash the core business generates before reinvestment. Reading capex per share against free cash flow shows how much of the operating cash the business keeps and how much it must spend to stay in business.

**What the combination tells you.** Rising capex per share with falling free cash flow means the company is plowing more of its cash into assets, which is good if the assets earn a return and bad if they do not. Low capex per share with high free cash flow points to a capital-light business that converts most of its operating cash into owner cash. Watching the two lines together over several years shows whether a spending program is a temporary drag or a permanent weight on what the owner keeps.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Depreciation approximates the yearly cost of replacing assets as they wear out, so comparing capex to it shows whether the company is spending above or below replacement level. A ratio above 1 means capex exceeds the wear-and-tear charge, which usually means the asset base is expanding. A ratio below 1 means the company is spending less than the assets are wearing down, which shrinks the asset base over time.

**What the combination tells you.** Capex per share tells you the size of the spending in owner terms, and Capex / Depreciation tells you whether that spending is building, maintaining, or harvesting. A high capex per share with a ratio well above 1 confirms a real growth phase. A falling capex per share with a ratio below 1 warns that the drop may be underinvestment rather than discipline. Together they turn a single spending figure into a read on which phase of the investment cycle the company is in.

### Marginal ROIC

**Why read the two together.** Capex per share records how much new capital the company is putting to work, and marginal ROIC measures the return that the newest capital earns. Return on invested capital is the profit a company earns for each dollar of capital it uses. The marginal version focuses on the most recent capital rather than the whole base.

**What the combination tells you.** Heavy capex per share paired with a marginal ROIC above the cost of capital means the spending is creating value, so the near-term drag on cash is worth paying. Heavy capex per share with a marginal ROIC below the cost of capital means the company is reinvesting into returns that destroy value. The pair answers the question the spending figure alone cannot, which is whether the assets being bought are earning their keep.

## Part 8: Limitations

Gross capex mixes maintenance and growth spending in one number, so a high figure can be good or bad depending on what the cash bought. The cash flow statement does not split the two, so the metric cannot say on its own whether the spending expands the business or just preserves it. One large project can make a single year look unrepresentative, which is why the trend matters more than any one reading. The per-share form adds buyback mechanics that can raise the figure with no change in actual spending.

Capex per share also understates total reinvestment for companies that grow by buying other companies, because acquisitions are recorded outside capex. A serial acquirer can show a low capex line while reinvesting heavily through deals. The metric records what was spent, not what the spending earned. A dollar of capex that compounds for a decade and a dollar that produces nothing look identical on this line, so the return metrics must supply the judgment this figure cannot.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is capex per share rising, falling, or steady over the chart, and does that pattern point to a building phase, a maintenance pace, or a harvest phase?
2. What is the spending buying in management's own words, and do the stated projects have completion dates and expected returns that later charts can check against?
3. How much of the current capex is genuine growth spending versus the maintenance bill the business cannot skip, and what would free cash flow per share be at maintenance-only levels?
4. Have past building phases on the chart converted into revenue per share and free cash flow per share growth within a reasonable time, and at what conversion rate?
5. What is the marginal return on invested capital on recent years' spending, and is the newest capital earning above the cost of that capital?
6. How does capex per unit of revenue compare against direct competitors, and is any advantage real efficiency or just leases, outsourcing, and acquisitions moving spending off this line?
7. If capex per share is falling, what is the age and condition of the asset base, and is the released cash a finished project or deferred bills piling up?
8. Is the industry currently in a disciplined or expansionary capex cycle, and is the company spending against the cycle or joining a top-of-cycle capacity wave?
9. How much of any rise in capex per share comes from buybacks shrinking the share count rather than from more dollars actually spent?
10. What does the valuation assume this line does over the projection years, and is that assumption an extension of the chart's history or a hopeful departure from it?
