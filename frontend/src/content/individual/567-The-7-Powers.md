# The 7 Powers

## What it is

The 7 Powers is a framework for judging whether a company can defend its profits from competitors for many years. The framework comes from Hamilton Helmer in his book 7 Powers. The framework names seven and only seven ways a company keeps rivals from copying its edge.

In this framework a power always has two parts that must appear together.

1. A benefit. A benefit is a real improvement in cash flow through lower cost, higher pricing, or a smaller investment need. Cash flow is the cash a business generates after paying its bills.
2. A barrier. A barrier is an obstacle that leaves competitors unable or unwilling to copy the benefit.

Helmer calls a benefit with no barrier a mere advantage, because rivals will copy it and drive the extra profit toward zero. A power is a precise version of what many investors loosely call a moat. A moat is a lasting competitive advantage that protects a company's profits.

## The seven powers

| Power | The benefit (better economics) | The barrier (why rivals cannot copy) |
|---|---|---|
| Scale Economies | Cost per item falls as production volume grows. | A follower would have to gain share to match the leader's cost. The leader can fight back from its lower cost, so the attack destroys value. |
| Network Economies | Each extra user makes the product more valuable to every other user, so the leader can charge more. | A follower offers less value because it has fewer users. The discount needed to close that gap is too large to be worth it. |
| Counter-Positioning | A newcomer runs a better business model that costs less or earns more. Business model means the way a company makes money. | The established company will not copy the model because copying would damage its existing business. |
| Switching Costs | A customer who already bought faces a loss to change suppliers, so the company can charge that customer more on the next purchase. | A rival must pay the customer enough to cover that loss. Doing so puts the rival at a cost disadvantage. |
| Branding | Trust built over a long history lets the company charge more for the same product. | A copycat must spend many years building the same trust, with no guarantee it ever arrives. |
| Cornered Resource | Exclusive access to a valuable resource on good terms raises value on its own. | The resource is held by legal right or personal choice that rivals cannot obtain. |
| Process Power | An embedded way of operating lowers cost or raises quality year after year. | The way of operating is complex and partly hidden. Rivals cannot copy it quickly even when they watch closely. |

## How this section works

This section does not hand the user a verdict. The user judges which of the seven powers the company holds by answering the section's questions first, in the user's own words. Each question asks for a specific benefit and the barrier that protects it. Writing the answer forces the user to hunt for real evidence in the company's numbers and history.

The Explain using AI button on each question is a tutor, not a shortcut. The button helps only after the user has tried the question. The tutor then checks the reasoning and points to what the user missed. A power counts only when a benefit comes paired with a barrier. A benefit that any rival can copy is not a power and deserves no weight in the verdict.

## Why it matters for valuation

A company earns excess returns when it earns more than its cost of capital. Cost of capital is the return investors require for the risk they take. Excess returns are the profits left above that required return. Competition normally drags excess returns down toward zero over time. A power is the reason that decline does not happen. A benefit protected by a barrier lets the company keep earning excess returns while rivals fail to copy it.

Valuation cares about how long those excess returns last. A discounted cash flow model, which values a company as the sum of its future cash flows in today's money, ends with a terminal value. The terminal value is the value placed on every year beyond the explicit forecast. A durable power justifies a longer stretch of high returns and a larger terminal value. A weak or copied advantage justifies neither, so the terminal value must be smaller and the whole valuation lower.

## Common traps

1. Counting a benefit that has no barrier. Market leadership and wide name recognition feel like power, yet rivals can compete them away when no barrier protects them.
2. Mistaking scale for branding. A large company can afford advertising that a small rival cannot, and that reach comes from size rather than trust. The advantage is Scale Economies, not Branding.
3. Reading counter-positioning as full protection. Counter-positioning only holds against the established company that will not copy the new model. A rival using the same new model faces no such barrier, so a second route to power is still needed.
4. Overrating switching costs. Switching costs pay off only on later sales to a customer the company already has. A company with no follow-on product, or no repeat purchase, earns nothing from them.
5. Calling a talented person a cornered resource. A resource is power only when its pay stays well below the value it creates. When a star is paid the full value of the work, the excess return flows to the person rather than the company.
6. Assuming process power too easily. Process power is the rarest of the seven, and smooth operations are usually copied once rivals study them. Genuine process power resists copying for years even under open observation.
7. Treating a power as permanent. A shift in technology can dissolve switching costs or a network within a few years. Every power needs rechecking as the industry changes.
