# Deferred Tax Liabilities Non Current

## Part 1: Definition

Deferred tax liabilities non current is income tax a company expects to pay in a future year beyond the next twelve months. The tax is owed because the company reported more profit to shareholders than it reported to the tax authority in earlier years. A liability is something the company owes. The word non current means the tax comes due more than one year out. The line answers one question: how much future tax has the company pushed forward by reporting profit and taxable income on different schedules?

The gap arises from timing, not from cheating. Companies keep two sets of figures. One set follows accounting rules and goes to shareholders. The other set follows tax rules and goes to the tax authority. Some items are counted at different times in the two sets. When the company pays less tax now and will pay more later, the future tax shows up here as a deferred tax liability. Warren Buffett described a deferred tax liability as resembling an interest-free loan from the Treasury that comes due only at the company's election, because the company holds the cash now and pays the tax later with no interest charged.

The table below reads common situations for this line.

| What you see | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| A large and growing balance | The company keeps deferring tax, often through heavy investment in equipment. |
| A balance that is stable for years | Deferrals are being created and reversed at about the same pace. |
| A shrinking balance | Past deferrals are reversing and the future tax is now being paid. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Deferred tax liabilities non current is not a calculated ratio. The line is a balance reported on the balance sheet. The balance sheet is the statement that lists what a company owns and owes on a single date. The balance comes from the timing differences between accounting profit and taxable profit.

`Deferred Tax Liability = Timing Difference × Tax Rate`

The timing difference is the amount of profit reported to shareholders before it is reported to the tax authority. The tax rate is the company's expected future rate on that profit. The most common source is depreciation. Depreciation is the spreading of an asset's cost over its useful life. Tax rules often let a company write off equipment faster than the accounting books do. The company then pays less tax early and more tax later, and the later tax sits in this line.

The balance rises when new timing differences are created, often by fresh investment in long-lived assets. The balance falls when old differences reverse and the deferred tax is paid. A cut in the tax rate also lowers the balance, because the future tax is recalculated at the lower rate.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Deferred tax liabilities non current is a source of funding that costs nothing. The company holds cash today that it will hand to the tax authority only in later years. No interest is charged on the delay. A growing balance often means the company is investing heavily and earning the right to defer tax in the process.

The line also reveals the gap between reported profit and cash tax. Two companies with the same reported profit can pay very different amounts of cash tax. Deferred tax liabilities non current marks part of that gap. A large balance signals that reported profit has run ahead of taxable profit.

The line matters most for capital-heavy businesses. Companies that buy a lot of equipment build the largest balances. The balance shows how much of their tax bill they have legally postponed.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Capital spending is the first driver. New equipment creates fresh depreciation timing differences. A company in a heavy investment phase will see this balance grow. A company that stops investing will see old differences reverse and the balance fall.

The tax rate is the second driver. The balance is the timing difference multiplied by the future tax rate. A legislated rate cut shrinks the balance even though no tax was paid, because the future tax is now smaller. A rate increase raises the balance the same way.

Accounting and tax method choices are the third driver. The depreciation method, the treatment of leases, and the handling of certain reserves all change the size of the timing differences. Differences in these choices make the line hard to compare across companies. A one-time write-down or asset sale can also reverse a large block of deferred tax at once.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The size of this line depends on how much long-lived equipment the business owns. The table shows the pattern by sector.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and telecom | Large and persistent | Massive long-lived assets create big depreciation timing gaps. |
| Heavy industry and energy | Large | Plants and equipment generate ongoing deferrals. |
| Railroads and transport | Large | Fleets and track are depreciated faster for tax. |
| Software and services | Small | Few long-lived physical assets to depreciate. |
| Retail | Moderate | Stores and fixtures create some timing differences. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is meaningless. A utility with a huge deferred tax balance is not riskier than a software firm with almost none. The balances reflect asset intensity, not financial strain. The line only carries meaning against companies with similar asset bases and tax methods.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the deferred tax liabilities non current line as a multi-year series and as a percent of total assets. Total assets is everything the company owns. The vertical analysis on this page shows each balance-sheet line as a share of total assets, so a rising raw number can still be a flat share if the company is growing.

A steady rise in the balance that also lifts the percent of total assets usually means the company keeps investing in equipment and keeps creating new tax deferrals. A steady decline often means investment has slowed and past deferrals are reversing into cash tax payments. A sharp one-year drop frequently follows a legislated tax-rate cut rather than any real cash payment, so check whether tax law changed in that year. High volatility can come from large asset sales or write-downs that reverse deferrals in a single period. One base effect can fake a trend. A balance that falls only because the tax rate was cut looks like deleveraging but reflects no cash outflow.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Tax Burden

**Why read the two together.** Tax burden measures the share of pre-tax profit that survives after tax, so a high tax burden means a low effective tax rate. Deferred tax liabilities non current grows when the company pays less cash tax now than its reported profit implies. Reading both shows whether a low effective rate is permanent or just postponed.

**What the combination tells you.** A low reported tax rate paired with a rising deferred tax balance means much of the tax saving is deferred, not erased, and will reverse in future years. A low rate with a stable deferred balance points to permanent tax advantages rather than timing. A rising deferred balance warns that cash tax will eventually catch up with reported tax. The pair separates tax that is gone forever from tax that is merely delayed.

### Income Quality (CFO/NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income quality compares operating cash flow to net income, testing whether reported profit turns into cash. Deferred tax liabilities non current is one reason cash tax can be lower than the tax expense on the income statement. Reading both shows how tax timing inflates near-term cash relative to reported profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A high income quality reading supported by growing deferred tax means cash flow is being lifted by postponed tax, which can fade when deferrals reverse. Income quality that stays strong as the deferred balance flattens points to genuine cash generation. A reversing deferred tax balance can pull cash flow below net income as the postponed tax is finally paid. The pair shows how much of today's cash strength rests on tax timing.

### Debt To Assets

**Why read the two together.** Debt to assets measures how much of the company's assets are funded by debt. Deferred tax liabilities non current is a non-debt obligation that funds part of the asset base at no interest cost. Reading both shows the full picture of how the company finances itself beyond formal borrowing.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with low debt to assets but a large deferred tax balance is using interest-free tax deferral instead of loans to fund its assets. The deferred tax obligation carries no interest and no fixed due date, so it is gentler than debt. Treating the deferred tax balance as if it were debt overstates the company's true financial risk. The pair separates costly borrowed funding from costless deferred funding.

## Part 8: Limitations

Deferred tax liabilities non current is an estimate, not a fixed bill. The balance depends on the future tax rate, which can change by law, and on assumptions about when differences reverse. A rate change can swing the balance without any cash moving. For a fast-growing capital-heavy company, the balance may keep rolling forward for decades and never come due in practice, which is why treating the whole balance as near-term debt overstates the risk.

The line is also hard to compare across companies. Depreciation methods, lease treatment, and reserve policies all differ between companies. Two similar businesses can therefore report very different balances. The line nets some deferred tax assets against deferred tax liabilities in ways that vary by company. A large balance is not automatically a warning, and a small balance is not automatically safe. The line is a useful read on postponed tax and a weak read on how much cash will actually leave the company and when.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current balance produced by heavy ongoing investment in equipment, by a tax-rate change, or by both? What does the balance become if investment slows?
2. How fast is the deferred tax liability growing compared with the company's spending on long-lived assets?
3. Did a legislated tax-rate change move the balance in any recent year without an actual cash payment?
4. Is the low effective tax rate permanent, or is much of the saving sitting in this line and due to reverse later?
5. What share of the balance comes from depreciation timing versus other items such as leases or reserves?
6. How does deferred tax liabilities non current as a percent of total assets compare with direct competitors using similar assets and tax methods?
7. Is operating cash flow being lifted by postponed tax, and what happens to cash flow when deferrals reverse?
8. Is the balance likely to keep rolling forward for years, or are old differences starting to reverse into cash tax?
9. Has any large asset sale or write-down reversed a block of deferred tax in a single period?
10. Does management disclose when the major timing differences are expected to reverse, or is the timing left vague?
