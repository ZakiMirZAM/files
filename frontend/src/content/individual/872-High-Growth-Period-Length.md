# High Growth Period Length

## What it is

The high-growth period is the number of years a valuation lets a company keep growing faster than the economy before the stable phase begins. The length of that window is one of the most value-sensitive choices in any valuation, because every extra year of above-normal growth adds value. The engine sets the window from three observable determinants rather than from preference: the company's size, the excess returns it currently earns, and the durability of its competitive advantage as read from its life-cycle stage.

The engine maps every company onto one of three archetypes drawn from the standard valuation framework. A zero-year window fits a firm that is already stable, so the perpetuity starts today. A five-year window fits a moderate advantage that competition should close within a few years. A ten-year window with a gradual descent fits a durable advantage or a very high measured growth rate.

## The three determinants

| Determinant | What is measured | How it moves the window |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Market value in US dollars, with a small-company line near five billion | A smaller company has more room to run before its own scale becomes the constraint, so small size lengthens the window. |
| Current excess returns | The return on capital minus the cost of capital | A spread of eight points or more is a durable advantage that erodes slowly. A spread of two to eight points is a modest advantage. A spread below two points is noise, and growth without excess returns adds no value. |
| Durability of the advantage | The life-cycle stage read from the company's own revenue and margin history | An emerging or growth-stage company argues for a long window. A mature company argues for a short one. A declining company fits a stable model with no high-growth phase at all. |

The determinants are scored together, and the score maps onto the three archetypes.

| Archetype | High-growth window | Profile |
|---|---|---|
| Already stable | 0 years, one-stage perpetuity | Growth at or below the risk-free rate, no meaningful excess returns, mature or declining history |
| Moderate advantage | 5 years, two-stage model | An advantage competition should close within a few years |
| Durable advantage | 10 years, three-stage model with a transition | Strong scores across the determinants, or measured growth above 25 percent |

Two overrides sit on top of the scoring. A company whose growth already runs at or below the risk-free rate, with no meaningful excess returns and a mature or declining profile, is stable today and gets zero years. A measured growth rate above 25 percent gets the full ten-year window with a transition, because growth that extreme cannot drop to stable overnight.

## How this section works

The Forecast section states the window the engine chose and the archetype it matched, together with the reasoning sentences for each determinant. The window then drives the stage count: zero years means a one-stage perpetuity, five years means a two-stage model, and ten years means a three-stage model with a five-year transition.

The ceiling on stable growth is the risk-free rate, which serves as the proxy for the nominal growth of the economy. Nominal means measured in current money, inflation included. The ceiling is applied in the currency of the valuation, and that detail matters. A company that reports in a high-inflation currency carries a high risk-free rate and a high nominal growth ceiling in that currency. Translating such a valuation into dollars without matching the currency of the growth rate to the currency of the discount rate produces a large error in either direction.

## Why it matters

The difference between a five-year and a ten-year window can move a fast grower's value by a third or more. Making the window a scored function of size, spread, and stage forces the analyst to defend the inputs rather than the conclusion. A reader who disagrees with the window can see exactly which determinant to argue with.

The window also disciplines optimism. High growth must be paid for with reinvestment every year it lasts, so a longer window is not automatically a higher value. A company earning thin excess returns gains almost nothing from extra growth years.

The determinants reinforce each other in a way worth noticing. Excess returns are what make growth valuable, the life-cycle stage says how long the returns have left, and size says how much room the growth has to run. A company strong on one determinant and weak on the others earns the middle window, which is the scoring behaving as intended.

## Common traps

The first trap is granting a long window because the company is exciting. Excitement is not a determinant. Size, measured excess returns, and the life-cycle stage are, and all three are visible in the accounts.

The second trap is treating the stable-growth ceiling as pessimism. A company growing faster than the economy forever would eventually become the economy. The risk-free rate is the arithmetic stand-in for that limit, not a forecast of weakness.

The third trap is ignoring currency when judging the ceiling. A 12 percent stable growth rate is impossible in dollars but can be reasonable in a currency whose risk-free rate is 14 percent. Judge the ceiling in the valuation currency, then worry about translation separately.
