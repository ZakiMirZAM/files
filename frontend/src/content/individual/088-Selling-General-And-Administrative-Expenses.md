# Selling General And Administrative Expenses

## Part 1: Definition

Selling, general and administrative expense, usually shortened to SG&A, is the combined cost of selling the product and running the company. The line adds two things together. The first is selling and marketing cost, which is the cost of winning customers. The second is general and administrative cost, which is the cost of the corporate office: executive pay, finance, legal, human resources, rent on head offices, and the back-office systems that keep the business running. The line sits below gross profit, in the operating-cost block that comes after the direct cost of the product. The line answers one question: how much does it cost to sell the product and run the organization around it?

A beginner term to define first is overhead. Overhead is a cost the company carries no matter which product makes the sale, such as the salary of the chief executive or the rent on headquarters. General and administrative cost is overhead. In the vertical analysis table the app shows SG&A as a percent of revenue. A reading of 20 percent means twenty cents of every sales dollar goes to selling and running the company.

Common readings of SG&A as a percent of revenue:

| SG&A share of revenue | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 10 percent | Lean. Common in companies that sell in bulk or run very tight overhead. |
| 10 to 25 percent | Typical for many established companies. |
| 25 to 40 percent | Heavy. Common in consumer brands and growth companies that spend a lot to sell. |
| Above 40 percent | Very heavy. The company carries large selling cost, a large corporate structure, or both. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

This line is not a ratio. The company reports SG&A as a dollar figure, and the app converts it to a percent of revenue for the vertical analysis table:

`SG&A Share = Selling General And Administrative Expenses ÷ Revenue`

SG&A is the top number, and the line is itself a sum of its two parts:

`SG&A = Selling And Marketing Expenses + General And Administrative Expenses`

The selling and marketing part is the cost of advertising, the salesforce, promotions, and distribution. The general and administrative part is the cost of the corporate office: management salaries, finance, legal, human resources, and head-office rent. Many companies report only the combined SG&A figure and never split it, which is why the combined line is often the only level at which two companies can be compared.

Revenue is the bottom number. Revenue is the total sales for the year. Dividing by revenue states the cost in cents per sales dollar, which puts a small company and a large company on the same scale. The app shows SG&A alongside its two components, Selling And Marketing Expenses and General And Administrative Expenses, where the company reports the split.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

SG&A measures the cost of everything the company does beyond making the product. Gross margin shows how profitable the product is on its own. SG&A shows how much of that product profit is consumed selling the product and running the company. The gap between the two is most of what becomes operating profit.

The line is a strong read on management discipline. A company that holds SG&A steady as a percent of revenue while sales grow is spreading a fixed overhead over more sales, which lifts profit. A company whose SG&A share creeps up year after year is letting overhead grow faster than the business, which is a common sign of a bloated or poorly run organization.

The general and administrative half deserves special caution. The corporate office is the easiest place for cost to hide and the hardest for an outsider to check. A company can window-dress this figure by shifting cost between lines, so a reader should watch the trend and compare it with similar companies rather than trust a single year's number.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Scale moves the line strongly. Much of administrative cost is fixed, meaning it does not rise much when sales rise. A growing company spreads that fixed overhead across more revenue, so the SG&A share falls even when the dollar cost climbs. A shrinking company sees the share rise because the same overhead is divided over fewer sales.

The selling model drives the other half. A company that advertises heavily or runs a large salesforce carries a high selling cost inside SG&A. A company that sells through outside retailers carries less. Acquisitions also move the line, because a merger often brings duplicate head-office functions that inflate SG&A until the company removes the overlap.

One-off items distort single years. Legal settlements, executive severance, and the cost of integrating an acquisition can land in SG&A and lift the share for a year. A reader should strip these one-time charges out before judging the underlying overhead trend.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal SG&A share depends on how much selling and corporate structure the sector requires.

| Industry type | Typical SG&A share | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer brands | High, often 25 to 40 percent | Heavy advertising and a large marketing organization sit inside the line. |
| Software and subscription | High during growth | Large sales teams and customer-acquisition spending dominate the line while the company grows. |
| Industrial manufacturing | Moderate | Selling is technical and lean, and overhead is steady. |
| Wholesale and distribution | Low | Thin margins force tight overhead, and selling is minimal. |
| Commodity producers | Very low | Output sells at a market price with little selling effort and a small corporate structure. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one sector must advertise heavily and the other sells into a market at a set price. Compare SG&A shares only among companies that sell and organize themselves in similar ways.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read SG&A as a percent of revenue across the multi-year chart. The raw dollar figure grows with the company and tells you little. The share of revenue tells you whether overhead is being controlled as the business changes size.

A share that falls steadily while revenue grows is the best pattern. A falling share means fixed overhead is being spread over more sales, which is operating leverage at work. A share that rises steadily is a warning that overhead is growing faster than the business, which erodes profit even when sales rise. A step change usually points to an acquisition, a restructuring, or a one-time charge, and the reader should confirm which. High volatility in the share often signals lumpy one-off items rather than a real change in the cost base. Where the company splits SG&A into its selling and administrative parts, check which half is moving, because a rising selling share and a rising administrative share carry very different messages.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Selling And Marketing Expenses

**Why read the two together.** Selling and marketing expense is one of the two halves that make up SG&A. Reading the selling half beside the SG&A total shows how much of the overhead is the cost of winning customers and how much is the cost of the corporate office. A flat SG&A total can hide a rising selling half offset by a falling administrative half.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising SG&A total driven by the selling half means the company is spending more to grow, which is healthy if revenue responds. A rising total driven by the administrative half means overhead is expanding, which rarely produces sales and usually signals bloat. Splitting the total into its two parts turns one number into a diagnosis of where the money is going. The pair separates investment in demand from growth in pure overhead.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is operating income as a percent of revenue, the profit left after every operating cost. SG&A is usually the largest operating cost between gross margin and EBIT margin. A change in the SG&A share flows almost directly into the EBIT margin.

**What the combination tells you.** When EBIT margin falls and SG&A is the line that rose, the profit pressure comes from selling and overhead rather than from the product. When EBIT margin improves as the SG&A share falls, the company is converting more of its gross profit into operating profit by controlling overhead. Reading the two together shows whether operating profit is driven by the product or by cost discipline above it.

### Operating Leverage (DOL)

**Why read the two together.** Operating leverage measures how sharply operating income moves when revenue moves, and it is driven by how much of the cost base is fixed. The administrative half of SG&A is largely fixed cost. A company with heavy fixed SG&A has high operating leverage and sees profit swing widely with sales.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high fixed SG&A base and high operating leverage will see its SG&A share fall fast in good years and rise fast in bad years. A company with a flexible cost base shows a steadier SG&A share through the cycle. Reading the two together tells you how much of today's profit would survive a drop in sales, because the fixed overhead does not shrink when revenue does.

## Part 8: Limitations

SG&A blends two very different costs into one line. The selling half is partly an investment in future demand. The administrative half is pure overhead. A company can report a steady SG&A total while shifting its mix from selling toward overhead, which weakens future sales while looking unchanged. The combined number alone cannot reveal that shift unless the company splits it.

Reporting choices cut both ways. Some companies expense costs through SG&A that others capitalize, meaning they record the cost as an asset and spread it over years. The general and administrative half is the easiest line to manage, because cost can be moved between lines and one-time charges can be labeled as ongoing or unusual at the company's choice. A single year's SG&A is therefore weaker evidence than a multi-year trend compared against close competitors. The line says how much was spent, not whether the spending produced anything.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is SG&A as a percent of revenue this year, and how does that compare with three and five years ago? Is overhead getting lighter or heavier relative to sales?
2. Does the company split SG&A into selling and administrative parts? If so, which half is driving the change in the total?
3. Did the SG&A share fall because fixed overhead was spread over rising sales, or did it fall because the company cut selling spending that demand depends on?
4. Are there one-time charges inside SG&A this year, such as legal settlements, severance, or acquisition costs? What is the share without them?
5. Has a recent acquisition added duplicate overhead, and has the company actually removed the overlap or only promised to?
6. How does the company's SG&A share compare with direct competitors that sell and organize themselves the same way?
7. Is any cost being shifted between SG&A and cost of revenue in a way that flatters gross margin while inflating SG&A, or the reverse?
8. In the most recent downturn, how much of SG&A proved fixed and how much could management cut? What does that say about profit in the next bad year?
9. Is the administrative half growing faster than sales over time, which would point to a corporate structure expanding without producing revenue?
10. If sales fell by a quarter, how far could SG&A fall with them, and what would the operating margin become?
