# Net Debt To EBITDA

## Part 1: Definition

The net debt to EBITDA ratio measures how many years of operating earnings a company would need to pay off its debt, after first using its cash. Net debt is the company's total borrowings minus the cash it already holds. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA is a rough measure of the cash a business generates from normal operations. The ratio answers one question: how large is the debt compared with the earnings available to repay it?

A ratio of 2 means the company carries two years of operating earnings in net debt. A ratio of 6 means the debt would take six years of earnings to clear, which is a heavy load. A lower number means the debt is small relative to earnings and easier to repay or refinance.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1 | Very light debt. Cash and one year of earnings could nearly clear all borrowings. |
| 1 to 3 | A comfortable level for most established companies with steady earnings. |
| 3 to 4 | Elevated. Tolerable when earnings are stable and predictable. |
| 4 to 6 | Heavy debt. Common in buyout-owned companies and risky if earnings fall. |
| Above 6 | A large load relative to earnings. Refinancing risk becomes a central concern. |
| Negative | Cash exceeds total debt, so the company holds more cash than it owes. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Net Debt To EBITDA = (Total Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA`

Net debt is the top number. Net debt starts with all interest-bearing borrowings, then subtracts cash and easily sold investments. Cash is subtracted because the company could use that cash to pay down debt immediately. A version that skips the cash subtraction is called gross debt to EBITDA, and it is stricter because it gives no credit for the cash on hand. This app uses the net version, which subtracts cash, for the headline tile.

EBITDA is the bottom number. EBITDA is operating profit with depreciation and amortization added back. Depreciation and amortization are non-cash charges that spread the cost of equipment and intangible assets over many years. Adding them back gives a rough picture of operating cash before financing and tax. EBITDA is used because the ratio asks how fast the business could generate cash to clear its debt, and EBITDA approximates that cash before interest and taxes.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The net debt to EBITDA ratio sizes the debt against the earnings that must repay it. The dollar amount of debt means little on its own, because a debt that is small for one company can overwhelm another. Expressing the debt in years of earnings makes companies of different sizes directly comparable, which is why the ratio is one of the first numbers analysts discuss.

The ratio measures the total size of the debt rather than the yearly cost. A company can afford its interest comfortably while still carrying a debt so large that refinancing it becomes the real risk. The ratio captures that size risk, which a coverage ratio based on interest alone misses.

Subtracting cash sharpens the picture for companies that hold a large cash pile. A company with heavy borrowings but equal cash is far safer than the gross debt figure suggests, and the net version reflects that. The subtraction is most useful when the cash is genuinely free rather than already promised to a project.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move the ratio. New borrowing to fund acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends raises net debt and pushes the ratio up. Repaying debt or building a cash balance lowers net debt and pulls the ratio down. A swing in EBITDA moves the bottom number, so a strong year can lower the ratio and a weak year can raise it sharply even when the debt has not changed.

The bottom number can be inflated. EBITDA ignores the real cash a company must spend on equipment to keep running, so an asset-heavy business can look less leveraged than it truly is. Companies sometimes report an adjusted EBITDA that adds back further costs, which shrinks the ratio and flatters the picture. The cash subtraction can also mislead when the cash is trapped overseas or already committed, because that cash is not truly available to repay debt.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The acceptable level depends on how steady the company's earnings are and how much it must spend on equipment. Steady earnings and light equipment needs support a higher ratio safely.

| Industry type | Typical comfortable range | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Stable subscription and consumer staples | Up to 3 or higher | Predictable earnings make heavier debt manageable. |
| Capital-intensive industrials | Kept lower | Heavy equipment spending consumes EBITDA, so less is left to repay debt. |
| Cyclical producers such as miners and automakers | Judge at the worst year | Earnings can halve in a downturn, which doubles the ratio overnight. |
| Buyout-owned companies | 4 to 6 | Lenders accept heavy debt in exchange for higher rates and strict loan conditions. |
| Capital-light software | Often negative | Many hold more cash than debt, so the ratio carries little information. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | Borrowing is the core business and EBITDA is not a meaningful measure for them. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless because each industry trades at a different multiple of earnings and needs a different amount of equipment spending. A capital-heavy business should carry a lower ratio than a capital-light one even at the same level of safety. Compare a company against direct peers and weight its worst year on the chart.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A flat low line means the company keeps debt small relative to its earnings power year after year. A flat high line means management runs the business with heavy debt on purpose, often after a buyout. The pattern that deserves the most attention is a steady climb. A rising ratio means debt is growing faster than earnings. The cause is usually acquisitions, buybacks, or earnings that shrink while the debt stays fixed.

A spike in a single year is often an earnings problem rather than a debt problem, because a weak EBITDA shrinks the bottom number. Earnings-driven spikes often recover with the business cycle, but the spike still reveals the worst-case level the debt must be sized against. A ratio that falls steadily usually means the company is repaying debt or growing earnings, both of which lower refinancing risk over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Net debt to EBITDA measures the size of the debt in years of earnings. Interest coverage measures whether earnings can pay the yearly interest on that debt. Size and affordability are different questions, and a complete view of debt risk needs both answers.

**What the combination tells you.** A high net debt to EBITDA ratio paired with strong interest coverage means a large debt load carried cheaply, which is still exposed to refinancing risk when rates rise. A high ratio paired with weak coverage means the company struggles on both the size and the cost of its debt. Reading the pair separates how much the company owes from whether it can afford the yearly bill.

### FCF / Total Debt

**Why read the two together.** Net debt to EBITDA uses EBITDA, which ignores the cash spent on equipment and taxes. FCF / Total Debt uses free cash flow, the cash left after that spending, and shows what share of the debt could actually be repaid each year. Free cash flow is a stricter measure of repayment power than EBITDA.

**What the combination tells you.** A low net debt to EBITDA ratio paired with weak FCF / Total Debt means the EBITDA is being consumed by equipment spending and taxes before any debt can be repaid. A close match between the two suggests the business converts earnings into real cash efficiently. Reading both shows whether the earnings that size the debt actually turn into cash that can clear it.

### EV/EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net debt to EBITDA measures the debt in years of earnings. EV/EBITDA measures the whole company's value in years of earnings, where enterprise value is the market value of equity plus net debt. Comparing the two shows how large the debt is relative to the total value of the business.

**What the combination tells you.** When the company's EV/EBITDA multiple is far above its net debt to EBITDA ratio, a large equity cushion sits above the debt, which protects lenders. When the two numbers are close, the debt makes up most of the company's value and little cushion remains. The gap between the two estimates how much asset value stands behind the borrowing.

### Net Debt / OCF

**Why read the two together.** Net debt to EBITDA uses EBITDA, a profit-based estimate of cash. Net Debt / OCF uses operating cash flow, the actual cash the business produced, which already reflects working capital swings and cash taxes. Operating cash flow is harder to flatter than EBITDA.

**What the combination tells you.** A net debt to EBITDA ratio that looks safe while Net Debt / OCF looks heavy means EBITDA is overstating the real cash the business throws off. A close match between the two confirms the earnings figure is converting into cash. Reading both guards against an EBITDA that has been inflated by adjustments or by ignored cash costs.

## Part 8: Limitations

The bottom number is the main weakness. EBITDA ignores the cash a company must spend on equipment, on interest, and on taxes. The ratio can therefore make an asset-heavy or highly taxed business look far safer than it is. Adjusted EBITDA figures that add back extra costs shrink the ratio further and reward aggressive reporting. The ratio also gives a single static snapshot and says nothing about when the debt actually comes due.

The cash subtraction can mislead. Cash that is trapped overseas or already committed to a project is not truly available to repay debt, yet the net figure treats all cash as free. The ratio ignores the interest rate on the debt, the loan conditions attached to it, and any foreign-currency exposure. The ratio is a strong first measure of debt size and a poor measure of debt timing or true cash availability.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio driven by the amount of debt, by the cash subtracted, or by an EBITDA that may be temporarily high? What does the ratio become if EBITDA returns to a normal level?
2. What was the ratio in the company's worst earnings year on the chart? Would the debt have looked manageable against that year's EBITDA?
3. Is the cash that lowers net debt genuinely free, or is it trapped overseas or already promised to a project?
4. Does the company report an adjusted EBITDA that adds back costs? What is the ratio using unadjusted EBITDA?
5. How much of the EBITDA is consumed by equipment spending and taxes before any debt can be repaid? Does free cash flow tell a harsher story?
6. How much of the debt comes due within three years? What would refinancing cost at current market rates?
7. How does the ratio compare with direct peers at the same point in the business cycle? Which peer would face refinancing trouble first?
8. Have recent acquisitions or buybacks raised net debt? Is management adding leverage at a risky point in the cycle?
9. What loan conditions reference the leverage ratio? How large an earnings decline would break them?
10. Is the business capital-heavy enough that even a modest ratio is risky, or capital-light enough to carry more debt safely?
