# Revenue

## Part 1: Definition

Revenue is the total value of goods sold and services delivered to customers during the period, counted before any cost is taken out. Revenue is the first line on the income statement. The line is also called sales or turnover. Revenue answers one question: how much business did the company actually do this period?

In the vertical analysis table this app shows, revenue is the base line. Every other income statement line is shown as a percentage of revenue, so revenue itself always reads 100 percent. A cost line that equals 30 percent means that cost consumed thirty cents of every revenue dollar. Reading the other lines as a share of revenue is what makes one year comparable with another and one company comparable with a rival of a different size.

Common readings of the revenue line:

| What the revenue line shows | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Steady growth year after year | Customers keep buying, and demand for the product is durable. |
| Flat revenue for several years | The company has stopped growing. Profit can still rise only by cutting cost, and cost cuts run out. |
| Falling revenue | Fewer customers, lower prices, or a shrinking market. No cost program fixes a shrinking top line for long. |
| A sudden jump in one year | Often an acquisition or a price increase rather than more units sold. The cause must be checked. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Revenue is built from the sales the company makes:

`Revenue = Units Sold × Price Per Unit`

Units sold is the quantity of product or service the company delivered. Price per unit is what each one sold for. A drinks company that sold ten cans at one dollar each reports ten dollars of revenue. Real companies sell many products at many prices, so the reported line adds up thousands of separate sales recorded across the period.

Revenue is recorded when it is earned, not when the cash arrives. Accounting calls this the accrual principle. A sale is counted once the company has delivered the goods or finished the service and the customer owes the money. The customer's unpaid bill becomes an account receivable on the balance sheet, which is the company's right to collect cash later. This timing rule matters because revenue can be booked in one period while the cash is collected in the next. A company that books sales far faster than it collects cash deserves a closer look.

One presentation choice can change the reported number without changing the business. A company that acts as a middleman can book the full sale price as revenue, called gross, or only the commission it keeps, called net. The gross choice makes the top line look much larger. The gross-versus-net choice makes it risky to compare two companies on revenue size alone.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Revenue is analyzed first because revenue is the hardest major line for management to manage upward. Costs can be trimmed and profits can be smoothed, but revenue is the customers' own verdict paid in cash. A company whose revenue stops growing has a ceiling on profit that no efficiency can lift for long.

Revenue sets the size of everything below it on the income statement. Gross profit, operating income, and net income are all carved out of revenue. If the top line is wrong, every line and every percentage beneath it is wrong too. That is why checking how real the revenue is comes before any other income statement work.

Revenue also drives the valuation tools on this page. The discounted cash flow value starts from a forecast of future revenue, and every margin assumption is applied to that revenue. A clear, honest, growing top line is the foundation the rest of the analysis stands on.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several business forces move revenue. Demand sets how many customers want the product. Price sets what each unit sells for, and general inflation can lift the reported number even when the number of units sold is flat. Mix is the blend of cheaper and more expensive products sold, and a shift toward pricier items raises revenue with no extra units. For companies that operate abroad, currency movement changes reported revenue when foreign sales are translated back into the home currency, with no change in the underlying business.

Two other forces can move the line in a large step. An acquisition adds the bought company's sales to the total at once, which is not the same as selling more of the existing product. Splitting growth into organic, meaning growth from the existing business, and acquired growth is the first task at any company that buys other companies. Accounting choices also move the line. Aggressive recognition can pull future sales into the current period, and the gross-versus-net presentation choice can double or halve the reported figure.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Revenue is graded on different quality questions in different sectors.

| Industry type | What to read in the revenue line | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription software | Share of revenue that recurs each year, and customer retention | Recurring revenue is more durable than one-time sales, so the recurring share is the quality measure. |
| Retail | Same-store sales separated from total sales | Total sales can grow just from opening new stores. Same-store growth shows whether existing stores are healthy. |
| Industrial and aerospace | Backlog and book-to-bill, the orders not yet delivered | Orders already signed make future revenue more visible than current sales alone. |
| Commodity producers | Price times volume, with price set by the market | The company controls volume but not price, so a revenue jump can be pure price and not effort. |
| Banks | Net interest income plus fees | A bank's revenue is built differently and cannot be compared with a product company's sales. |

Comparing the revenue size of two companies is meaningless when one books gross and the other books net, or when sectors differ. Compare growth rates and quality within the same sector, and strip out acquisitions first.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows revenue as a multi-year line, and revenue also appears as the 100 percent base in the vertical common-size table. Read the line two ways. First read the raw multi-year series for its slope and its smoothness. Steady compounding is the sign of a durable franchise. Acceleration demands an explanation. The cause could be price, more units, mix, or an acquisition, and each source carries a different quality. Deceleration at a large company is the maturity question that every fast grower eventually meets. Sharp swings up and down map how exposed the company is to the business cycle.

Reading revenue as a percent of revenue is not useful, because the base is always 100 percent. Instead, watch how the lines below revenue move as a share of revenue over the years. A cost line creeping up as a share of revenue means cost is growing faster than sales. A base effect can fake a trend. A weak prior year makes the next year's growth rate look large, and a one-time price spike can make a single year stand out. Cross-check growth against receivables. Revenue rising much faster than cash collection is a warning that sales are being booked before the cash is real.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Revenue tells how much the company sold. Gross margin tells how much of each sales dollar survives after the direct cost of making the product. Reading the two together separates growth that is earned at full price from growth that was bought by cutting prices. A rising top line means little if every extra sale comes at a thinner margin.

**What the combination tells you.** Revenue growing while gross margin holds steady is the healthy pattern, because the company is selling more without discounting. Revenue growing while gross margin falls means the company is buying its growth with price cuts, and the extra sales are worth less than they look. Falling revenue with steady margin points to a demand problem rather than a pricing problem. The pair shows whether growth is genuine or purchased.

### Revenue Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Total revenue can grow while the company quietly issues new shares to fund that growth. Revenue per share divides revenue by the share count, so the figure shows the sales attached to each share an owner holds. Reading the two together shows whether the owner's slice of the business is actually growing.

**What the combination tells you.** Total revenue and revenue per share rising together means growth is reaching shareholders. Total revenue rising while revenue per share stalls means new shares are diluting the gain, so each existing owner is standing still. The gap between the two lines measures how much of the headline growth is lost to a rising share count. The pair turns a company-level number into an owner-level number.

### Receivables Turnover

**Why read the two together.** Revenue is recorded when a sale is earned, not when the cash arrives. Receivables turnover measures how quickly the company collects the cash from those sales. Reading the two together checks whether reported revenue is converting into real money or piling up as unpaid customer bills.

**What the combination tells you.** Revenue rising while collection stays quick means the growth is real and turning to cash. Revenue rising while collection slows means sales are being booked faster than customers pay, which can signal channel stuffing or looser credit terms used to chase growth. A sharp fall in collection speed alongside a revenue jump is a classic warning that the top line was inflated. The pair confirms whether the revenue is trustworthy.

## Part 8: Limitations

Revenue collapses a great deal into a single number. The line does not show the split between price and volume, between organic and acquired growth, or between recurring and one-time sales, and those splits are where the real analysis lives. Recognition is full of timing judgment, so a company can advance future sales into the current period and make growth look stronger than it is. The gross-versus-net presentation choice makes revenue size treacherous to compare across companies in marketplace and agency businesses.

Revenue also says nothing about profit. A company can grow sales fast while losing money on every sale, and rapid growth at negative margins has destroyed many companies. Nominal revenue growth includes inflation, so part of a rising line can be price rather than real expansion. Currency translation moves the line for international companies with no change in the business. Revenue is the right first question in any analysis and never the last.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How much of the revenue growth came from price, how much from more units sold, how much from product mix, and how much from acquisitions? What does the growth look like with acquisitions stripped out?
2. What share of revenue is recurring, contracted, or subscription based, and how loyal is that base from year to year?
3. Does the receivables trend confirm that the reported growth is collecting as cash, or are unpaid customer bills growing faster than sales?
4. How concentrated is the revenue among the largest customers or segments? How durable is each large slice?
5. Where the choice exists, is the company booking revenue gross or net, and how would a comparison with rivals change if both used the same presentation?
6. How did revenue behave in the worst year on the chart? What does that history say about how the line would hold up in the next downturn?
7. Is the company's share of its market rising or falling? Is the growth coming from a growing market or from taking customers from rivals?
8. Where the company discloses forward indicators such as backlog, bookings, or order pipeline, what do they say against the trailing revenue being extrapolated?
9. Is any of the growth being bought through discounts, looser credit terms, or acquisition premiums? Which margin or balance sheet line records that cost?
10. What revenue path does the app's discounted cash flow value assume? How does that required path compare with the durable, organic portion of the company's revenue history?
