# EVA Margin

## Part 1: Definition

The EVA margin measures how much economic profit a company earns for every dollar of sales. EVA is short for economic value added. Economic value added is the profit that remains after the company pays every operating cost, the tax bill, and one more charge that the income statement never shows: a rent on the money tied up in the business. The income statement stops at accounting profit. The EVA margin keeps going and subtracts the cost of the capital the company uses, then states what is left as a percentage of revenue.

The margin answers one question: after charging the business for the money invested in it, does a dollar of sales still produce a profit? Capital here means the money supplied by lenders and shareholders to run the company. That money is not free. Lenders want interest and shareholders want a return, so the company owes a yearly charge on the capital it holds. The EVA margin reports the profit that survives after that charge.

| Margin level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | The company earns an accounting profit but does not cover the cost of its capital. The business is destroying value for its owners. |
| About zero | The company earns exactly the return its capital costs. Owners are no better off than they would be in a similar investment elsewhere. |
| 1 to 5 percent | A genuine surplus on each sales dollar after the capital charge. A solid result for most industries. |
| Above 5 percent | A wide surplus. The level usually points to strong pricing power or very efficient use of capital. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`EVA Margin = (NOPAT − Capital Charge) ÷ Revenue`

The top number is EVA, the economic value added in dollars. NOPAT is the first part of it. NOPAT is short for net operating profit after tax, which is the profit from normal operations after tax but before any interest on debt. The capital charge is the second part. The capital charge is the amount of capital invested in the business multiplied by the rate that capital costs. Subtracting the capital charge from NOPAT leaves the true surplus, the profit earned above what the capital itself demanded.

Revenue is the bottom number. Revenue is total sales for the year. Dividing the dollar surplus by revenue turns it into a margin, so a large company and a small company can be compared on the same scale. The capital charge is what separates this margin from an ordinary profit margin. An ordinary margin ignores the cost of the money invested. The EVA margin treats that cost as a real expense and deducts it before declaring any profit.

This app reports an EVA value for the company. The EVA margin expresses that same surplus as a share of sales.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A normal profit margin can look healthy while the business is still failing its owners. A company can report positive net income and yet earn less than the return its capital costs. The EVA margin catches that case. The margin charges the business for the capital it uses, so only profit above that hurdle counts as real.

The margin is also a clean way to judge whether growth is worth having. Growth that earns less than the cost of capital lowers EVA even as revenue rises. The EVA margin shows whether each sales dollar carries a true surplus or only an accounting one. A rising EVA margin means the company is widening the gap between what it earns and what its capital costs.

The margin sizes the result against revenue, so it can be tracked over time and set against peers. A higher EVA margin means more value created per dollar of sales. A lower one means the capital charge is eating most of the operating profit.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three forces move the margin. The first is operating profit, which rises and falls with prices, volumes, and costs. Stronger pricing power lifts NOPAT and widens the margin. The second is the amount of capital invested. A company that ties up more money in equipment, inventory, or acquisitions carries a larger capital charge, which lowers the margin even when sales hold steady. The third is the rate the capital costs. A higher cost of capital raises the charge and shrinks the margin.

Accounting choices can distort a single reading. Goodwill from past acquisitions inflates the capital base and enlarges the charge, so a serial acquirer often shows a thinner EVA margin. The cost of capital itself is an estimate, and a low estimate understates the charge and flatters the margin. Surplus cash held outside the operations can be removed from capital, which changes the charge. One-time gains or losses inside operating profit move the top number for reasons unrelated to the ongoing business.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The level of the margin depends on how much capital an industry needs and how much pricing power it holds. Capital-heavy industries carry large charges and tend to show thin EVA margins even when run well.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and asset-light services | Often high | Little capital is tied up, so the capital charge is small and most operating profit survives as surplus. |
| Consumer brands with pricing power | Moderate to high | Strong margins clear the capital charge with room to spare. |
| Industrial and manufacturing | Thin and cyclical | Heavy equipment means a large capital charge, and profit swings with the economy. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Thin by design | Enormous capital bases produce large charges, and regulated prices cap the surplus. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Capital and financing are the raw material of the business, so the standard capital charge does not apply. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when two companies need very different amounts of capital to operate. Compare a company against others in the same industry.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in the EVA margin means the company is earning a wider surplus over its capital cost each year. The rise can come from better operating profit or from using less capital to produce the same sales. A steady decline is a warning. A falling margin means the capital charge is claiming a larger share of operating profit, often because the company is investing heavily without the profit to match.

A step change usually marks an event rather than a trend. A large acquisition can drop the margin in one year by loading the capital base with goodwill. A change in the assumed cost of capital can move the whole line without anything happening inside the business. High volatility points to a cyclical company whose operating profit swings with the economy. Read the margin next to the company's revenue and capital, because a margin that climbs only because the company is shrinking its asset base is a different story from one that climbs on real operating gains.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC − WACC Spread

**Why read the two together.** The EVA margin and the value-creation spread measure the same idea in two units. The spread is the return on invested capital minus the cost of that capital, stated in percentage points. The EVA margin is that surplus expressed against sales. A positive spread and a positive EVA margin both say the company earns more than its capital costs.

**What the combination tells you.** The spread shows whether value is being created per dollar of capital. The EVA margin shows how much of each sales dollar that creation amounts to. A wide spread with a thin EVA margin points to a low-margin, high-turnover business that still creates value. A narrow spread with a healthy EVA margin points to a high-margin business that uses a lot of capital. Reading both separates the return on capital from the profitability of sales.

### NOPAT

**Why read the two together.** NOPAT is the operating profit after tax that forms the top half of the EVA margin. Reading NOPAT on its own shows the raw profit before any capital charge is applied. The EVA margin then subtracts that charge.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising NOPAT with a falling EVA margin means the capital charge is growing faster than profit, so the extra profit is not creating value. A rising NOPAT alongside a rising EVA margin means growth is genuinely profitable after the cost of capital. Watching both shows whether reported profit growth is the kind that rewards owners.

### Sales / Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Sales to invested capital measures how much revenue each dollar of capital produces. The EVA margin measures how much surplus each dollar of sales produces after the capital charge. The two cover the two halves of value creation: capital efficiency and profitability.

**What the combination tells you.** A high sales-to-invested-capital figure paired with a positive EVA margin means the company turns capital into sales efficiently and earns a surplus on those sales. A low figure paired with a thin EVA margin means the business needs a lot of capital and earns little above its cost. Together the two show whether value comes from working capital hard or from pricing it well.

## Part 8: Limitations

The EVA margin rests on an estimated cost of capital. That cost is not observed directly, so a small change in the estimate moves the capital charge and the margin. A low cost-of-capital estimate flatters the margin, and a high one punishes it, with no change in the business itself. The capital base is also an accounting figure. Goodwill from acquisitions can swell the base and depress the margin, so a single low reading may reflect an old deal rather than a weak operation.

The margin also reflects the choices made when computing NOPAT and capital, and different analysts make different adjustments for leases, research spending, and one-time charges. Two analysts can produce two different EVA margins for the same company. The margin is a backward-looking measure of value created on existing assets, and it says nothing on its own about future investment opportunities. A negative margin in one year does not prove the business is failing if the company is early in a heavy investment phase that has not yet produced its returns.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current margin driven by strong operating profit, a low capital base, or a low assumed cost of capital? What does the margin become if the cost of capital is set higher?
2. How much of the invested capital is goodwill from past acquisitions? What is the margin on the operating assets alone?
3. Has the margin been positive across the worst year on the chart, or only in good years?
4. Does the EVA value the app reports agree with the trend in this margin, or do the two diverge?
5. Is operating profit being lifted by one-time gains that will not repeat? What is the margin without them?
6. Is the company growing revenue while the EVA margin falls? Is that growth earning less than the cost of capital?
7. How does the margin compare with direct competitors that need a similar amount of capital to operate?
8. Is surplus cash being counted inside the capital base, and would removing it change the charge and the margin?
9. If the margin is negative, is the business early in an investment phase, or is it a mature operation that simply does not cover its capital cost?
10. What would the margin be if the assumed cost of capital rose by a few points at the next refinancing?
