# SG&A To Revenue

## Part 1: Definition

The SG&A to revenue ratio measures how many cents of every revenue dollar the company spends on selling, general, and administrative costs. SG&A is the standard abbreviation for selling, general, and administrative expenses. Those costs include sales commissions, advertising and distribution. Office salaries, executive pay and legal fees belong there too. The ratio answers one question: how much overhead does the company need to carry in order to sell what it sells?

The ratio is shown as a percentage of revenue. A reading of 20 percent means twenty cents of every sales dollar is consumed by selling and administrative costs before any operating profit is left. SG&A sits below gross profit on the income statement, so the ratio measures the second layer of cost rather than the direct cost of making the product.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 10 percent | A lean cost base. Typical where products sell themselves or where the customer base is small and concentrated. |
| 10 to 20 percent | A normal range for manufacturers and many established businesses. |
| 20 to 35 percent | Heavy selling effort. Common in consumer brands, specialty retail, and companies with large sales forces. |
| Above 35 percent | Either a very high gross margin business that can afford the spending, or a business whose overhead is out of control. |

The level alone proves nothing. A high ratio is acceptable when gross margin is high, and a low ratio is unremarkable when gross margin is thin.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`SG&A To Revenue = Selling, General And Administrative Expenses ÷ Revenue`

Selling, general, and administrative expenses form the top number. The line combines two different kinds of cost. Selling costs rise and fall with sales activity, and advertising and sales commissions are the main examples. General and administrative costs are largely fixed. Head office salaries, insurance and audit fees are the main examples.

Revenue is the bottom number. Revenue is the year's total sales recognized under accounting rules. Revenue belongs in the bottom because the question is how much overhead the company needs per dollar of business, not the absolute size of the overhead.

The treatment of research and development varies:

| Version | What is included on top | When the version is used |
|---|---|---|
| SG&A only | Selling, general, and administrative expenses as reported | The standard version, and the one this app displays. |
| SG&A plus research and development | Adds research and development spending | Used when comparing companies that report the two lines differently, since some firms bundle them. |

This app reads the reported SG&A line directly, so check how a competitor splits its operating costs before comparing.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The ratio measures cost discipline in the part of the business management controls most directly. Direct production cost is largely set by suppliers and by product design. Overhead is set by decisions about headcount, offices and sales structure. A management team that holds the ratio steady while revenue grows is demonstrating control over exactly the costs it can control.

The ratio also reveals operating leverage. Operating leverage means the tendency of profit to move more sharply than revenue because part of the cost base does not change with sales. Most general and administrative cost is fixed in the short run, so a falling ratio during growth shows the fixed portion being spread over more revenue.

A third use is competitive comparison. Two companies selling similar products at similar gross margins will earn very different operating margins if one carries much more overhead. The ratio isolates that difference in a single number and makes the cost advantage visible.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Revenue growth is the first influence, and the influence works through the denominator. Fixed overhead spread over rising revenue lowers the ratio without any cost being cut. Falling revenue raises the ratio for the same reason, which is why a rising ratio in a weak year is often a sales problem rather than a spending problem.

Deliberate spending decisions are the second influence. Launching a product, entering a new country, or expanding a sales force all raise the ratio in the near term in exchange for revenue later. Cutting advertising lowers the ratio immediately and can lower revenue later, so a sudden improvement deserves scrutiny.

Accounting and structural choices move the ratio without changing the business. Companies differ in whether distribution and warehousing sit inside cost of revenue or inside SG&A, which shifts cost between gross margin and this ratio. Stock based compensation for office staff is recorded inside SG&A, so a change in grant policy moves the line. One-time items such as restructuring charges, legal settlements, and acquisition fees are frequently reported here and can distort a single year badly.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level tracks the gross margin of the industry and the amount of selling effort each sale requires.

| Industry type | Typical range | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Grocers, distributors, and wholesalers | 5 to 15 percent | Gross margins are thin, so overhead must stay small or no profit remains. |
| Industrial manufacturers | 10 to 20 percent | Products are sold to a limited number of business buyers. |
| Consumer packaged goods | 20 to 35 percent | Advertising is the main way brands defend shelf space and pricing. |
| Specialty retail | 20 to 30 percent | Store staff and occupancy costs are often reported inside this line. |
| Software and internet businesses | 30 to 60 percent | Gross margins near ninety percent fund very heavy sales and marketing spending. |
| Pharmaceuticals | 25 to 40 percent | Large field sales forces and regulatory affairs teams are required. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Cost is measured against net interest income or premiums rather than against revenue. |

A cross-sector comparison of this ratio is meaningless on its own. The ratio must always be read against gross margin, because the gross margin sets how much overhead the business can afford. A software company at 40 percent may be far more profitable than a distributor at 12 percent.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steadily falling ratio during a period of revenue growth is the most favorable pattern, because the pattern shows fixed overhead being spread across a larger business. The improvement is genuine only while revenue is actually rising. Check the revenue line beside the ratio before crediting management with cost control.

A steadily rising ratio is a warning in most cases. Overhead growing faster than sales means either that the company is buying growth that has not arrived, or that costs have been allowed to build. A rise concentrated in one year usually points to a specific event such as a restructuring charge or a legal settlement, and those should be identified and set aside.

Two base effects can fake a trend. A collapse in revenue during a downturn lifts the ratio sharply even when spending was flat, and the ratio falls back on its own during recovery. A reclassification of costs between cost of revenue and SG&A creates a permanent step in the line, and the step is visible as an equal and opposite move in gross margin during the same year.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross margin is revenue less the direct cost of producing goods, divided by revenue. SG&A is the next layer of cost subtracted after gross profit. Gross margin therefore sets the budget that overhead spending has to fit inside, and neither number can be judged without the other.

**What the combination tells you.** A high SG&A ratio funded by a very high gross margin is a normal and sustainable structure, which is why software companies can spend heavily on sales. A high SG&A ratio against a thin gross margin leaves almost nothing for operating profit and is fragile in a downturn. When gross margin falls while the SG&A ratio holds, the company is being squeezed from both sides and operating profit will fall faster than revenue.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT means earnings before interest and taxes, so the EBIT margin is what remains of revenue after both direct cost and overhead. Gross margin and the SG&A ratio are the two components that produce it. Reading the pair shows which of the two layers is responsible for a change in operating profitability.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling EBIT margin with a stable SG&A ratio means the problem lies in pricing or in production cost rather than in overhead. A falling EBIT margin driven by a rising SG&A ratio means the overhead base is the cause, and that cause is usually within management's control. An improving EBIT margin built only on overhead cuts is worth questioning, since cuts to advertising and sales capacity can borrow profit from future revenue.

### Operating Leverage

**Why read the two together.** Operating leverage measures how sharply operating income moves when revenue moves. The fixed portion of general and administrative cost is one of the main sources of that sensitivity. The SG&A ratio shows the size of the cost layer, and operating leverage shows how much profit swing that layer creates.

**What the combination tells you.** A large SG&A ratio with high operating leverage means most of the overhead is fixed, so profit will rise quickly in an upturn and fall quickly in a downturn. A large SG&A ratio with low operating leverage means the spending is mostly variable, such as commissions that shrink automatically when sales fall. Knowing which structure applies tells you how much of today's operating margin would survive a bad year.

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Revenue is the denominator of the ratio, so every movement in the ratio has to be traced to either the cost line or the revenue line. A ratio read without the revenue trend can invert the conclusion completely. Improvement and deterioration can both come from the denominator alone.

**What the combination tells you.** Falling ratio with rising revenue means scale is working, and the company is growing without adding proportionate overhead. Falling ratio with flat revenue means costs were genuinely cut, and the next question is which activities were stopped. Rising ratio with falling revenue is usually a demand problem rather than a cost problem, and the ratio will correct on its own if sales recover.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio is not comparable across companies without checking what each company puts in the line. Distribution, warehousing and store occupancy can each sit in cost of revenue at one company and in SG&A at another. The split between SG&A and research and development also varies. Any comparison that skips this check can produce a confident conclusion in the wrong direction.

The ratio also mixes two costs with opposite behavior. Selling cost moves with sales volume, and administrative cost mostly does not, so a single number hides the split that determines downside risk. One-time charges reported inside SG&A distort single years. A low ratio can reflect chronic underinvestment in sales capacity rather than efficiency, and the damage from that underinvestment appears in revenue several years later. The ratio measures spending, not the return on that spending, so the ratio cannot tell you whether the advertising worked.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Did the ratio move this year because spending changed or because revenue changed? Which of the two moved first?
2. Which costs does this company report inside SG&A, and do the closest competitors classify the same costs the same way?
3. How much of the SG&A line is fixed administrative cost, and how much varies directly with sales volume?
4. Were any one-time items such as restructuring charges, legal settlements, or acquisition fees reported inside SG&A this year?
5. How much stock based compensation sits inside the line, and how has the share count changed over the same years?
6. If revenue fell by twenty percent, how much of the current SG&A spending could actually be removed within one year?
7. Is any recent improvement in the ratio the result of cutting advertising or sales headcount, and has revenue growth slowed since?
8. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors at similar gross margins, and what explains a persistent gap?
9. Has the company entered new markets or launched new products that raised the ratio, and is the promised revenue arriving on schedule?
10. Does the gross margin chart show an equal and opposite move in any year the SG&A ratio stepped, which would indicate a cost reclassification rather than a real change?
