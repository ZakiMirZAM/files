# Return On Equity

## Part 1: Definition

Return on equity measures how much profit a company earns each year for every dollar of capital its shareholders have invested and left in the business. Net income is the profit left after every expense has been paid. Operating costs, interest, and tax are all subtracted before net income. Shareholders' equity is the book value of the owners' stake in the company. Book value means the accounting value recorded on the balance sheet rather than the market price of the shares. Return on equity is usually shortened to ROE.

An ROE of 20 percent means the company earned twenty cents of net income during the year for each dollar of shareholders' equity. ROE is the broadest single measure of how productively management uses the owners' money. The number sits at the bottom of the income statement and the equity section of the balance sheet at the same time. For that reason ROE absorbs operating performance, financing choices, and tax outcomes all at once.

Common readings of the ratio:

| ROE level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0 | The company lost money during the year. A loss reduces shareholders' equity rather than adding to it. |
| 0 to 8 percent | Weak. The business earns less on the owners' capital than a safe bond would pay. |
| 8 to 15 percent | Average for an established company. Acceptable but not a sign of unusual quality. |
| 15 to 25 percent | Strong. This app's quality score awards a pass at 15 percent or higher. |
| Above 25 percent | Excellent, if the figure is durable and not produced by heavy debt or a tiny equity base. |
| Very high from tiny equity | Buybacks or past losses have shrunk equity close to zero. The ratio then says little about the real business. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity`

Net income is the top number. Net income is the earnings stream that belongs entirely to common shareholders, because lenders and the tax authority have already been paid. Where preferred stock exists, the preferred dividends are subtracted first, since ROE measures the return to common shareholders alone. Preferred stock is a senior class of shares that receives a fixed dividend before common shareholders.

Shareholders' equity is the bottom number. Shareholders' equity is the capital the owners originally contributed plus all retained earnings, minus buybacks and accumulated losses. Retained earnings are the profits the company kept and reinvested rather than paying out as dividends. Dividing profit by this figure gives a rate of return on the capital shareholders have actually committed.

Two conventions exist for the denominator:

| Version | What the version uses | What the version changes |
|---|---|---|
| Period-end equity | The equity balance on the last day of the year | Simple to read from one balance sheet. This app's headline ROE uses this version. |
| Average equity | The average of beginning and ending equity | Smooths the effect of buybacks and share issuance during the year. The DuPont section in this app recomputes ROE on average equity. |

Because the two versions use a different denominator, the headline figure and the DuPont figure can differ slightly. This app's quality score awards a pass when ROE reaches 0.15, meaning 15 percent.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Over long holding periods the return an owner earns tends to converge toward the return the business itself earns on its retained capital. A company that compounds equity at 18 percent and reinvests most of its earnings builds wealth faster than a company earning 6 percent on its capital. ROE states that compounding rate as one number, which is why long-term investors watch it closely.

ROE also feeds directly into valuation. Residual income and justified price-to-book frameworks compare ROE against the cost of equity. The cost of equity is the return shareholders require for the risk they take. When ROE sits above the cost of equity, the company tends to trade above book value. When ROE sits below the cost of equity, the company tends to trade below book value.

The gap between ROE and the cost of equity is the engine of those valuation models. A wide positive gap signals value creation. A negative gap signals value destruction, because the company earns less than shareholders could get elsewhere at the same risk.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three forces move ROE, and only one of them is unambiguously good. The first is operating improvement, meaning higher profit margins or faster asset turnover. Asset turnover is the revenue a company generates per dollar of assets. Operating improvement raises the top number honestly. The second force is financial leverage. Leverage means funding the business with more debt and less equity. More debt shrinks the equity base, so ROE rises with no change in operating skill while risk increases.

The third force is accounting and capital actions. Large buybacks shrink book equity and mechanically inflate the ratio. A heavy buyback program can push equity near zero or negative, at which point ROE becomes meaningless. One-time gains, unusually low tax years, and non-operating income all lift the top number for reasons unrelated to the core business. A large writedown shrinks equity and flatters every later year's ROE. A writedown is an accounting charge that lowers the recorded value of an asset.

A large cash pile works the other way. Cash earns little while it sits inside the equity base, so a company hoarding cash will show a lower ROE. The first question to ask about any ROE is which of these forces produced it.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

A good ROE is sector-relative. The acceptable level depends on how much capital the business needs and how it is financed.

| Industry type | Typical ROE range | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Branded consumer, software, and asset-light services | Above 20 percent | These businesses need little capital, so profit is large relative to a small equity base. |
| Banks and insurers | Low teens is strong | Leverage is the business model for these firms, so ROE is judged only against other financials. |
| Utilities and regulated infrastructure | 9 to 11 percent | Regulators set an allowed return on equity, and the company cannot durably exceed it. |
| Capital-intensive cyclicals such as autos and commodity producers | High to negative | Earnings swing with the cycle, so a single year shows mostly where the cycle stands. |

Compare a company against its industry peers and against its own ten-year history. Comparing ROE against the all-market average mixes businesses that need very different amounts of capital, so the comparison carries little meaning.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A stable or gently rising ROE over many years is the accounting signature of a durable competitive advantage. High returns that are not protected get competed away, so a high ROE that lasts implies a moat. A moat is a structural barrier that keeps competitors from copying the business.

A rising trend deserves decomposition before applause. The DuPont section splits ROE into margin, turnover, and leverage. DuPont decomposition is a method that breaks ROE into those three drivers. A gain that comes from margin or turnover means the business is improving. A gain that comes from the leverage factor means the company is borrowing its improvement and adding risk.

A steadily declining ROE usually signals either fading pricing power or an equity base growing faster than profits. A growing equity base often follows large acquisitions that have not yet paid off. A sudden spike is more often an accounting event than an operating one, such as a writedown shrinking equity or a one-time gain. A wildly volatile ROE marks a cyclical business, where the average across the cycle matters more than any single year.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DuPont ROE Decomposition

**Why read the two together.** ROE is a single number that hides how the return was produced. DuPont decomposition splits that number into profit margin, asset turnover, and leverage. The split shows whether the return comes from operating skill or from debt. Reading the two together turns one figure into a diagnosis.

**What the combination tells you.** A high ROE built on strong margin and turnover reflects a genuinely good business. A high ROE built mostly on the leverage factor reflects borrowed performance and added risk. Watching the three drivers over time shows which one is changing. A rising ROE driven only by rising leverage is a warning rather than a sign of progress.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC means return on invested capital. ROIC measures the return on all the money in the business, both debt and equity. ROE measures the return on equity alone. Comparing the two isolates the effect of debt on the headline ROE.

**What the combination tells you.** A high ROE next to a much lower ROIC means leverage is doing the work rather than the underlying business. A high ROE next to a similar ROIC means the business earns well on all its capital, which is the stronger result. When the two figures track each other closely, the company uses little debt. A wide gap between them measures how much of the ROE is borrowed.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity sizes the leverage directly. The ratio compares total debt against shareholders' equity. ROE rises with leverage, so a high ROE alongside a high debt-to-equity ratio confirms that debt is part of the result. The pair separates operating strength from financing.

**What the combination tells you.** A strong ROE with a low debt-to-equity ratio means the return is earned with little borrowing, which is the safest combination. A strong ROE with a high debt-to-equity ratio means the return depends on debt and would fall if the company deleveraged. Reading the two together confirms or clears the suspicion that leverage flatters the ROE. The same operating business shows a very different ROE at different debt levels.

### Income Quality (CFO / NI)

**Why read the two together.** ROE uses accrual net income in the top number. Accrual net income can include profit the company has booked but not yet collected as cash. Income quality compares cash flow from operations against net income. The comparison tests whether the reported profit is backed by real cash.

**What the combination tells you.** A high ROE backed by strong operating cash flow is trustworthy. A high ROE built on accrual earnings that cash flow does not support is far weaker. A persistent gap, where net income runs well above operating cash flow, suggests aggressive accounting. Reading the two together shows whether the profit behind the ROE is real.

## Part 8: Limitations

ROE inherits every flaw of book equity. Decades of buybacks, intangible-heavy business models, and past writedowns can shrink equity so far that ROE looks astronomically high yet means little. A company with negative book equity has no usable ROE at all, even when the business is healthy. ROE also rewards leverage without charging for the added risk, so two companies with identical operations but different debt loads report very different returns. The top number is accrual net income. Accrual net income is exposed to one-time items, tax noise, and outright manipulation.

ROE says nothing about the price an investor pays. A wonderful ROE bought at many times book value can still produce a poor return. Book value is the equity figure used in the denominator, and the market price can sit far above it. A single year also proves little, because one strong year can come from luck or an accounting event. Persistence across a full business cycle is the real signal, so ROE should always be read as a multi-year pattern rather than a single reading.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Using the DuPont section, how much of the current ROE comes from net margin, how much from asset turnover, and how much from financial leverage? Which of the three drivers changed most over the last five years?
2. Is the equity denominator economically meaningful for this company? Or have buybacks and writedowns shrunk it to the point where the ratio overstates the real business return?
3. Would the ROE still clear the 15 percent quality threshold using net income averaged over the last five years rather than the latest year alone?
4. How much of the latest net income is non-operating, such as investment gains, one-time items, or unusual tax benefits? What is the ROE on operating earnings alone?
5. What specific competitive advantage explains why this ROE has not been competed down toward the cost of equity? What evidence in the five-year history would warn that the protection is weakening?
6. If the company moved to its industry's typical debt-to-equity ratio, what would the ROE become? Does the comparison still favor this company after adjusting for leverage?
7. What does management do with retained earnings? Has the equity retained over the past five years earned a return near the headline ROE or visibly below it?
8. How does the company's ROE compare with its three closest competitors using the same accounting conventions? What operating difference explains the gap?
9. Is the income behind the ROE backed by operating cash flow in the income quality figure? Or do accruals account for a meaningful share of it?
10. At the current price-to-book multiple, how much of this ROE is already paid for? What ROE would the company need to sustain to justify the multiple?
