# Executive Compensation

## What it is

Executive compensation is the total pay package a company gives its most senior managers, above all the chief executive officer. Analyzing this pay means asking whether the amount is defensible and whether the design pushes managers to build lasting value for shareholders. A pay package usually has three parts. Salary is fixed cash paid every year. A cash bonus is a yearly reward earned by hitting short-term targets. Equity is an ownership stake in the company, granted either as shares or as options to buy shares later at a price fixed today. Stock-based compensation means pay handed to managers as company shares or share options instead of cash. Well-designed equity vests over several years and pays out only when long-term goals are met. Vesting is the waiting period a manager must serve before granted shares or options actually become his to keep.

## Why it matters for valuation

Executive pay affects value in two ways. First, pay is a real cost that comes straight out of what owners would otherwise keep. Cash salary and bonuses reduce profit directly. Stock-based compensation reduces each owner's share of the company through dilution. Dilution is the drop in an existing owner's share of the company when new shares are created and handed out. Second, the design of pay predicts behavior. Managers work toward whatever the pay plan rewards. A plan tied to durable measures pushes managers to build lasting value. Return on invested capital is the profit a company earns for every dollar of capital put into the business. Free cash flow is the cash left after the company covers running costs and the spending needed to keep operating. A plan tied to measures that are easy to manage invites managers to flatter short-term figures instead. Adjusted earnings is one such measure, a profit figure the company reports after removing costs it labels unusual. Management decides what counts as unusual, so the reported figure can be shaped. For that reason, the pay plan reveals a great deal about how a company will really be run.

## What a strong answer looks like versus a weak one

| Question in view | A strong answer | A weak answer |
|---|---|---|
| Level of pay | Pay looks modest against company size and peers, and it rises only when results improve. | Pay sits far above peers and keeps rising while results and the share price fall. |
| Structure | Most pay is equity that vests over many years and depends on long-term goals. | Most pay is fixed salary plus a large cash bonus, with little tied to the long run. |
| Bonus metrics | Bonuses track durable measures such as return on invested capital and free cash flow. | Bonuses track adjusted earnings that management can reshape at will. |
| Dilution | Share and option grants are small and steady, so owners keep their share. | Grants are large every year, and each owner's stake shrinks steadily. |
| Red flags | Bonuses must be earned, options keep their original price, and exit pay stays limited. | Bonuses are guaranteed, underwater options are repriced, and failure still pays out richly. |

## Common traps

1. **Judging pay by the headline number alone.** A large number can be fair when results are strong, and a small number can be generous when results are poor. The real test is pay measured against size, peers, and outcomes together.
2. **Treating stock-based compensation as free.** Equity pay uses no cash, so it can feel costless. Dilution still passes real value from owners to managers.
3. **Accepting a guaranteed bonus.** A bonus is meant to move up or down with performance. A guaranteed bonus pays out whatever happens, which drains the word bonus of meaning.
4. **Excusing repriced options.** When the share price falls below an option's fixed buy price, the option is worthless for the moment. Repricing resets that fixed price lower so managers gain again, which removes the loss they were meant to share with owners.
5. **Overlooking golden parachutes that reward failure.** A golden parachute is a large payout promised to an executive if the company is sold or the executive is removed. A parachute that pays richly even after poor performance rewards the very outcome shareholders least want.
