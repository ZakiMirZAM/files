# The Reinvestment Rate

## Part 1: Definition

The reinvestment rate is the share of a company's after tax operating profit that goes back into the business rather than out to the people who funded it. It answers one question: of the money the operations produced, how much was put back to work?

It is the engine of growth in a valuation. A company that reinvests nothing cannot grow from reinvestment, whatever else it reports.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Reinvestment rate = (Capital expenditure − Depreciation + Change in working capital) ÷ After tax operating profit`

Depreciation is subtracted because that portion of capital spending is replacing assets already in use rather than adding new ones. The change in working capital is included because growth ties up money in inventory and in amounts owed by customers, and that money is as committed as a factory.

This app reports the current year beside the five year average and the five year median. The three are shown together rather than one being chosen for you, because a single year of reinvestment is often distorted by the timing of one project.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used

Growth is not free. A company promising to grow must fund the growth, and the reinvestment rate is where that funding shows up. A growth forecast attached to a company that is not reinvesting is a forecast with nothing behind it.

The rate is also the clearest statement of what management believes. A company reinvesting heavily believes it has opportunities. A company reinvesting nothing and returning cash instead believes it does not, and that belief is worth taking seriously.

## Part 4: What influences the output

A negative rate means capital spending fell below depreciation. The company is consuming its asset base rather than renewing it, and current profit is being supported by assets that will eventually need replacing.

Depreciation is an accounting estimate and can understate what replacement truly costs, especially where the assets were bought years ago at lower prices. A company that appears to be reinvesting at replacement level may be falling behind it.

Research spending distorts the rate in the opposite direction. Research is charged against profit in the year it happens even though the benefit arrives later, so a research heavy company appears to reinvest less than it does. Acquisitions distort it too, because a company can buy growth rather than build it, and the money spent sits outside the usual capital spending line.

A company that holds cash it cannot deploy at good returns will watch its return on capital fall for no reason other than a growing denominator.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

One low year is a project that slipped. Five low years is a policy. Read the rate across the window and against capital spending divided by depreciation, which shows directly whether the asset base is being renewed.

A rate that has drifted down across a decade while the company still projects the same growth is the clearest version of the contradiction this metric exists to catch.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** This ratio isolates the asset renewal half of the reinvestment rate and removes the working capital swing that makes the headline rate jump around. It says plainly whether the company is spending enough to stand still.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio below one means the asset base is shrinking in real terms. Sustained below one, alongside a growth forecast, the two numbers contradict each other and the forecast is the one that is wrong.

### Sales / Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** This ratio says how much revenue each unit of capital produces, which is what decides how much reinvestment a given amount of growth actually requires. It is the bridge the model uses when a return on capital cannot be computed.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with high sales to capital needs less money to grow than a company with low sales to capital, so an identical reinvestment rate buys more growth at the first company than at the second.

## Part 8: Limitations

The rate divides by after tax operating profit, so it becomes unstable as profit approaches zero and meaningless once profit turns negative. For those companies the sales to capital bridge replaces it.

No industry average reinvestment rate and no economy-wide rate are available yet, so the benchmark questions beneath this definition currently have no figure behind them. Sales to invested capital is the only reinvestment benchmark the engine carries against the industry today.
