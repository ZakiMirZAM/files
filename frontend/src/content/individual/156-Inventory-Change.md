# Inventory Change

## Part 1: Definition

Inventory change is the line on the cash flow statement that shows how much cash moved in or out because the company's stock of unsold goods grew or shrank during the year. Inventory is the goods a company holds to sell. Inventory covers raw materials, parts, and finished products waiting on the shelf. The line answers one question: did building or running down inventory release cash or absorb cash this year?

Inventory sits inside the operating section of the cash flow statement. The operating section converts accounting profit into actual cash. Inventory is bought with cash before it is sold, so a rise in inventory ties up cash and a fall in inventory frees cash. The line records that swing, not the inventory balance itself.

Common readings of the line:

| Line reading | What that reading usually means |
|---|---|
| Large negative number | Inventory grew. The company spent cash to stock up. Cash was used. |
| Small number near zero | Inventory was roughly flat. Stock levels matched the year before. |
| Large positive number | Inventory shrank. The company sold from existing stock or cut purchases. Cash was released. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The line is the change in the inventory balance between two balance sheet dates, with the sign flipped for the cash statement:

`Inventory Change (cash) = Inventory at start of year − Inventory at end of year`

The inputs are the inventory figure on last year's balance sheet and the inventory figure on this year's balance sheet. The balance sheet is the snapshot of what a company owns and owes on one date. Inventory appears there as a current asset, meaning an asset expected to turn into cash within a year.

The sign rule is the key part. When ending inventory is larger than starting inventory, the subtraction gives a negative number, which lowers cash. The company paid suppliers for goods that have not been sold yet. When ending inventory is smaller, the result is positive, which raises cash. The company turned stock into sales without buying as much to replace it.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Profit on the income statement records a sale when the goods leave the door, not when cash arrives, and it ignores the cash buried in unsold stock. Inventory change closes that gap. The line shows whether reported profit was funded by real cash or quietly consumed by a growing pile of goods.

A company can report rising profit while cash drains into inventory year after year. Reading the inventory change line catches that drain early. The line tells an investor whether growth is paying for itself in cash or living on borrowed time.

The line also flags demand problems. Inventory that swells faster than sales often means goods are not selling. Inventory that falls while sales hold steady often means the company is running its operations more tightly.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Sales growth is the first driver. A company that is genuinely growing must hold more stock to serve more customers, so a growing business often shows cash used by inventory. The use of cash is normal here as long as inventory grows in line with sales and not faster.

Buying and production decisions move the line next. Stocking up ahead of a busy season, buying early to beat a price rise, or producing for a large order all raise inventory and use cash. Selling down old stock or cutting orders lowers inventory and frees cash.

Accounting choices can distort the line. The method used to value inventory, such as costing the oldest goods first or the newest goods first, changes the reported balance when prices move. A write-down, meaning a forced reduction in the recorded value of stock that will not sell at full price, also shrinks inventory without any real cash coming in. A one-time write-down can make a bad year look like a cash release.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a normal inventory swing depends on whether the company holds physical goods at all.

| Industry type | Typical behavior of the line | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Retailers and wholesalers | Large swings, often seasonal | These businesses live on stock, and the line moves sharply before and after peak selling periods. |
| Manufacturers | Steady use of cash while growing | Raw materials and finished goods both must be funded, so growth ties up cash in inventory. |
| Software and digital services | Near zero | There is little or no physical stock to buy, so the line barely moves. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | These firms hold financial assets rather than goods, so inventory is not a meaningful line. |
| Commodity producers | Large swings tied to prices | Stockpiles rise and fall with output and with the market price of the commodity. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is meaningless once one company holds physical goods and the other does not. Compare a retailer only against other retailers, and judge the line next to sales growth in the same year.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows this line as a multi-year series and as a percent of revenue. Reading the line as a percent of revenue removes the effect of the company simply getting bigger, so a rise or fall reflects how efficiently inventory is managed rather than raw size. A line that uses a steady, modest share of revenue each year points to inventory growing in step with sales.

A steady rise in cash used by inventory, growing as a percent of revenue, is a warning. Inventory is building faster than sales, which can mean goods are not moving or the company is overbuying. A steady fall in cash used can mean tighter management. The less happy version is a company starving itself of stock and risking lost sales.

A single large swing is often seasonal timing or a one-off stock build for a big contract. Watch for base effects. A year that released cash because of a deep write-down will look efficient on the chart even though the cause was unsold goods, so check whether a positive number came from real selling or from a markdown.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Inventory Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Inventory change shows the cash effect of the stock pile moving in dollars. Days Inventory Outstanding shows how many days of sales are sitting in inventory. The cash line tells how much moved, and the days figure tells whether the level of stock is healthy. Reading both separates the size of the swing from the efficiency behind it.

**What the combination tells you.** Cash used by inventory while days inventory holds flat means the build is simply growth, not a slowdown. Cash used while days inventory climbs means goods are piling up and selling more slowly, which is the worrying case. Cash released while days inventory falls confirms genuinely tighter stock management rather than a one-off sell-off.

### COGS / Inventory

**Why read the two together.** COGS divided by inventory measures how many times a year the company sells and replaces its entire stock, a measure of how fast goods turn over. The inventory change line shows the cash consequence of that stock growing or shrinking. One measures speed, the other measures the cash tied up at that speed. Together they show whether a cash drain comes from slow turnover.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling turnover rate alongside steady cash use by inventory points to a demand problem hiding inside a growing balance. A rising turnover rate alongside cash released shows the company is selling stock faster and freeing the cash that was locked in it. Stable turnover with modest cash use is the healthy state, where inventory and sales grow together.

### Operating Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Inventory change is one of the working-capital lines that builds up the operating cash flow total. Operating cash flow is the cash a business generates from its core operations in a year. Reading the inventory line inside that total shows how much of the cash result came from stock movements rather than from profit. The pairing reveals what is propping up or dragging down the headline cash number.

**What the combination tells you.** Strong operating cash flow helped by inventory falling can be a one-time release that will not repeat, so the underlying cash power is lower than it looks. Weak operating cash flow caused by heavy inventory building may be fine if the company is growing and the stock will sell. Reading the two together shows whether the cash result is durable or borrowed from a swing in stock.

## Part 8: Limitations

The inventory change line records a swing, not a level, so it says nothing about whether the company is over- or under-stocked to begin with. A small, calm line can sit on top of a dangerously bloated inventory balance that simply did not grow this year. The line must be read next to the inventory balance and the days-of-inventory figure to mean anything.

Accounting choices muddy the line further. The inventory valuation method changes the reported figure when input prices move, so two identical companies can show different lines. A write-down releases a positive number that looks like efficient selling but really marks failed stock. The line also blends raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods into one number. The blend hides whether a build is healthy production or unsold output stacking up.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Did inventory grow faster or slower than sales this year? If faster, what explains the extra stock?
2. Is any positive number on this line the result of real selling, or of a write-down that marked stock to a lower value?
3. How does the cash used by inventory compare with the days-of-inventory trend over the same years?
4. Is the build concentrated in raw materials, work in progress, or finished goods? Which one is rising?
5. Was a large swing caused by seasonal timing or a one-off stock build for a specific contract?
6. What inventory valuation method does the company use, and how much did changing input prices move the reported balance?
7. Does the inventory line repeatedly drain cash while reported profit rises? If so, where is the profit going?
8. How does this line behave in the company's weakest sales year on the chart compared with its strongest?
9. Has management written down inventory more than once in recent years? What does that say about demand forecasting?
10. If inventory keeps building, can the company fund the stock from its own cash, or does it need to borrow to carry it?
