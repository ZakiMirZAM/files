# Equity Per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Equity per share measures the net worth recorded on the company's books for each share of stock. Shareholders' equity is that net worth. Shareholders' equity equals total assets minus total liabilities. Total assets are everything the company owns at its recorded value. Total liabilities are everything the company owes. Shareholders' equity is the residual amount, meaning the amount the owners would keep if every asset were sold at its recorded value and every debt were repaid. Shares outstanding is the total number of ownership units currently held by investors. Equity per share states each share's claim on that net worth. The metric answers one question. What is the accounting net worth standing behind a single share?

In this app's data, equity per share usually prints the same number as book value per share. That match is expected and is not an error. Book value per share measures the same residual, shareholders' equity divided by shares. The two names come from different traditions. Equity per share comes from balance-sheet vocabulary. Book value per share comes from the valuation tradition that compares a stock's price against the value on the books. The only common reason the two diverge is preferred stock. Preferred stock is a senior class of shares that is paid before common shareholders. Some versions of the calculation subtract preferred stock to isolate the claim of common shareholders alone.

| Equity per share level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | Recorded liabilities exceed recorded assets. Past losses or buybacks above book value have erased the equity. The figure needs context before it condemns the business. |
| Low relative to the share price | Investors pay well above book net worth. The market expects earning power that the recorded assets do not show. Common for capital-light businesses. |
| Near the share price | The market values the company close to its recorded net worth. Common for asset-heavy businesses with steady but unexciting returns. |
| High relative to the share price | The stock trades below recorded net worth. Either the market doubts the assets are worth their recorded value, or the stock is overlooked. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Equity Per Share = Total Shareholders' Equity ÷ Shares Outstanding`

Total shareholders' equity is the top number. Total shareholders' equity is the bottom section of the balance sheet. The section holds common stock and paid-in capital, which is the money shareholders contributed. The section also holds retained earnings, which is the profit the business earned and kept rather than paid out. The section subtracts treasury stock, which is the cost of shares the company bought back. Each part belongs because each part is something the owners collectively have at stake.

Shares outstanding is the bottom number. Shares outstanding is the count of ownership units held by investors right now. Dividing the total equity by this count spreads the net worth across every share. A larger share count lowers the figure even when total equity does not change.

One variant adjusts the top number when preferred stock exists.

| Version | What the version changes |
|---|---|
| Total equity | Uses the whole equity section. This is what equity per share normally reports. |
| Common equity | Subtracts preferred stock first, so the figure reflects only the claim of common shareholders. Book value per share often uses this stricter version. |

When the company has no preferred stock, both versions give the same answer, and equity per share matches book value per share exactly.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Equity per share gives a recorded floor of value for each share. Shareholders' equity is the audited net worth, so the figure is harder to dispute than a forecast of future profit. An investor uses the figure as an anchor when judging whether a stock's price is rich or cheap against the value already on the books.

Equity per share also records what the owners have built over time. Retained earnings accumulate inside the equity section as the company keeps profit year after year. A rising equity per share shows the business is adding net worth for each share. The figure answers a question that earnings alone do not. How much owned value stands behind the share today, regardless of this year's profit?

Equity per share serves as the base for several other measures. Return on equity compares profit against this equity. The price-to-book ratio compares the share price against this figure. Reading the level first makes those comparisons meaningful.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Retained profit is the main builder. The company earns a profit, keeps part of it, and the kept part lifts shareholders' equity and the figure with it. Losses do the reverse and drain the equity. Dividends reduce the equity because dividends pay cash out to shareholders. Buybacks reduce the equity by the full price paid for the shares, which can lower the figure when shares are bought above book value.

Several events move the figure without the business truly changing. Issuing new shares raises both the equity and the share count, and the net effect on the per-share figure depends on the price. A writedown, which is a charge that lowers the recorded value of an asset, cuts the equity in one step. Currency translation can swing the equity at companies with foreign operations for reasons unrelated to operations. A reader who knows which item moved knows what kind of year the equity had.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Equity per share carries very different weight across sectors. The figure is most meaningful where recorded assets reflect real value and least meaningful where value sits off the balance sheet.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Banks and insurers | Equity is watched closely | Equity is regulated capital that supports the firm against losses. Regulators set minimum levels, so the figure is central. |
| Capital-light technology | Low equity per share | Most value comes from brands, software, and people, which are not recorded as assets. The metric understates these companies. |
| Heavy industry, utilities, real estate | Large and meaningful equity | These companies own factories, networks, and property recorded near real value. The figure tracks genuine net worth. |
| Companies that bought back stock above book | Falling or negative equity per share | Each buyback above book value removes more equity than it removes shares. Heavy buybacks can drive the figure down or below zero while the business stays healthy. |

Comparing equity per share across different sectors is meaningless. A bank and a software firm are measured on different things. Compare a company only against peers in the same industry with similar asset structures.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise is the healthy default. A rise that comes from retained profit means the business keeps adding net worth for each share. Read the slope against dividends, because a company can grow equity slowly while paying most profit out, and that can still be the right choice.

A decline deserves attention. A fall can come from losses, from writedowns that lower recorded asset values, from buybacks made above book value, or from large dividends paid out faster than the company earns. A step change usually marks a one-time event. A sudden jump in the share count points to a share issuance worth checking against what the raised money bought. As with every per-share equity line, heavy buybacks can eventually detach the figure from the underlying business. At that point the trend records capital returned to owners rather than net worth accumulated, which is a different but still useful story.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Equity per share and book value per share measure the same residual, shareholders' equity divided by shares. In this app the two usually print the same number. The only common reason they differ is preferred stock, which book value per share often subtracts to isolate the common shareholder's claim.

**What the combination tells you.** When the two figures match, no senior equity claim sits between the company and its common shareholders. When the two diverge, the gap flags preferred stock standing ahead of common shareholders. Reading both confirms whether the headline equity belongs fully to common owners or is shared with a senior class first.

### P/B

**Why read the two together.** P/B is the price-to-book ratio, the share price divided by book net worth per share. Equity per share supplies that book net worth. The ratio cannot be read without knowing the equity figure underneath it.

**What the combination tells you.** A high P/B means the market pays well above recorded net worth, which signals expected earning power the assets do not show. A low P/B means the price sits near or below recorded net worth, which signals either doubt about the assets or a stock the market has overlooked. Pairing the two separates the recorded value from what the market will pay for it.

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** ROE is return on equity, the profit earned for each dollar of shareholders' equity. Equity per share measures that equity base for each share. ROE shows the return earned on the very base that equity per share counts.

**What the combination tells you.** A growing equity per share earning a high ROE means the business compounds owner value at an attractive rate. A growing equity per share earning a low ROE means the equity is accumulating without producing much return, so the growth creates little value. Reading the pair shows whether the rising net worth is working hard or merely sitting.

### Tangible Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Tangible book value per share removes intangible assets such as goodwill from the equity before dividing by shares. Goodwill is the premium paid above recorded value in past acquisitions. Equity per share keeps those intangibles in, so comparing the two shows how much of the net worth is intangible.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide gap between equity per share and the tangible figure means much of the recorded net worth is goodwill and other intangibles that could be written down later. A narrow gap means the equity is backed mostly by hard assets. Reading both shows how solid the net worth behind the share really is.

## Part 8: Limitations

Equity per share rests on book value, which is historical cost rather than market value. Assets sit on the books near what the company paid, so the figure can be far from what the assets would fetch today. Internally built brands, software, and customer relationships are not recorded as assets at all, so the figure ignores intangible and off-balance-sheet value and understates capital-light businesses.

Equity per share can also mislead on its own terms. The figure can turn negative after heavy buybacks while the business earns fine, because each buyback above book value removes equity faster than shares. Accounting choices and writedowns distort the figure, and a writedown often arrives in a downturn just as confidence is already low. Equity per share says nothing about earning power. A high figure on weak assets can be worth less than a low figure on a business that compounds.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Does equity per share match book value per share in this company's data? If not, what preferred stock explains the gap and stands ahead of common shareholders?
2. What part of total equity is retained earnings versus paid-in capital? Does that mix show a business that earned its net worth or one that was handed the money by shareholders?
3. How has equity per share moved across the years shown? Is the change driven by retained profit, by share issuance, or by buybacks?
4. If equity per share fell, was the cause losses, writedowns, large dividends, or buybacks made above book value?
5. How wide is the gap between equity per share and tangible book value per share? How much of the net worth is goodwill that could be written down later?
6. Has the share count risen over the period? If so, what did the company buy with the money raised from issuing the new shares?
7. What return on equity is the company earning on this equity base? Does that return justify keeping the profit that grows the figure?
8. Does this company's sector make equity per share meaningful, or does most of its value sit off the balance sheet?
9. If equity per share is negative or barely positive at a mature company, what consumed the accumulated profit, and is the business still healthy underneath?
10. How does equity per share compare against direct peers with similar assets? Does the share price stand at a fair distance from the recorded net worth?
