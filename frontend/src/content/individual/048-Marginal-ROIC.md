# Marginal ROIC

## Part 1: Definition

Marginal ROIC measures the return a company earned on the new money it put into the business over a period. ROIC stands for return on invested capital, the after-tax operating profit a company earns for each dollar of capital tied up in the business. Invested capital is the total money funding the operations, counting both debt and shareholder equity used to run the company. The word marginal means the focus is only on the change, the extra profit produced by the extra capital. The metric answers one question: did the latest round of investment earn a good return?

Ordinary ROIC blends every dollar ever invested into one average. Marginal ROIC strips out the old base and looks only at the recent additions. A company can show a healthy average ROIC while its newest investments earn almost nothing. Marginal ROIC catches that decay early because the metric reacts to fresh spending before the average does.

| Marginal ROIC level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below the cost of capital | New investment is destroying value. Each new dollar earns less than the company pays to raise it. |
| Roughly equal to the cost of capital | New investment is neutral. Growth adds size but not value. |
| Above the cost of capital | New investment is creating value. Growth is worth funding. |
| Far above the average ROIC | The newest projects are the best ones. Returns are improving as the company expands. |
| Far below the average ROIC | The newest projects are the worst ones. Returns are decaying as the company expands. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Marginal ROIC = Change in NOPAT ÷ Change in Invested Capital`

The top number is the change in NOPAT. NOPAT stands for net operating profit after tax, the profit the business earns from operations after tax but before any interest on debt. The change is the difference between this year's NOPAT and an earlier year's NOPAT. The metric uses operating profit before interest because the goal is to judge the business itself rather than how the business is financed.

The bottom number is the change in invested capital. Invested capital is total debt plus equity minus the cash the company does not need to run day to day. The change is the difference between invested capital now and invested capital in the earlier year. The bottom number captures how much new money the company committed. Dividing the extra profit by the extra capital gives the return on just those new commitments.

The change is often measured over several years rather than one. A single year is noisy because new projects take time to reach full profit. The app reports the ratio of the change in operating profit to the change in invested capital across the periods it tracks.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Growth is only worth funding when the return on the new money beats the cost of the new money. Growth that earns exactly the cost of capital adds revenue and assets but no value for shareholders. Growth that earns below the cost of capital actively destroys value even as the company gets bigger. Marginal ROIC is the test that separates the three cases.

Average ROIC hides this distinction. A strong legacy business can carry a high average for years while management plows new cash into weak projects. Marginal ROIC exposes the weak projects sooner because the metric ignores the legacy base and reads only the recent additions.

The metric also frames the reinvestment decision. A company with high marginal ROIC should reinvest heavily and grow. A company with low marginal ROIC should return cash to shareholders instead of expanding. Reading marginal ROIC tells the investor which path management should be taking.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The first driver is the quality of recent projects. New factories, acquisitions, and product lines that earn strong margins lift the top number and raise marginal ROIC. Weak acquisitions and low-return expansion drag the number down. The metric is the cleanest read available on whether management is allocating new capital well.

The second driver is timing. New capital often lands before the profit it will produce. A company that just spent heavily on a plant that is not yet running will show a depressed marginal ROIC until the plant ramps up. The metric can understate true returns during a heavy investment phase. The metric can overstate returns when spending pauses while past projects mature.

One-off items distort the metric badly because both the top and bottom numbers are small differences between large figures. A single acquisition can swell invested capital in one year. An asset sale can shrink it. A restructuring charge can crush NOPAT for a year. Any of these can push a single-year reading to an extreme or even turn it negative when invested capital barely moved.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a good marginal ROIC depends on how much capital the business needs to grow and what the business pays for that capital.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-light software and services | Very high or volatile | New growth needs little new capital, so small profit changes against a tiny capital change produce large and jumpy readings. |
| Capital-heavy industrials and utilities | Lower and steadier | Growth requires large plants and networks, so the bottom number is large and the ratio is more stable. |
| Commodity producers | Swings with prices | Profit on new mines and wells tracks the commodity price, so marginal ROIC rises in booms and falls in busts regardless of project quality. |
| Acquisitive companies | Distorted by deals | Each acquisition adds a large lump of invested capital, so the metric reflects deal prices and timing as much as underlying returns. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Capital is regulated and the operating profit definition does not apply cleanly, so the standard ratio misleads. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two businesses need very different amounts of capital to grow. Compare marginal ROIC against the same company's own cost of capital and its own history first, and only then against direct competitors.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The most important comparison is marginal ROIC against the company's average ROIC over the same years. A marginal line sitting above the average means the newest investments are better than the old base. A marginal line sitting below the average means the newest investments are worse than the old base, and growth is slowly diluting quality.

A steady decline in marginal ROIC is an early warning that the company is running out of good projects. A steady rise suggests the company is finding better uses for capital as it expands. High volatility usually reflects the lumpiness of large projects and acquisitions rather than a real change in project quality, so smooth the reading across several years. A single negative print is common after a big acquisition or a heavy build year. A negative print alone proves nothing until the spending matures.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC is the average return across all capital ever invested. Marginal ROIC is the return on just the recent additions. The average shows what the whole business earns. The marginal shows what the latest decisions earned. Reading only the average hides whether new spending is as good as the old base.

**What the combination tells you.** A high average ROIC with a falling marginal ROIC means a strong legacy business is funding weak new projects, and the average will drift down over time. A low average with a rising marginal means a turnaround may be underway. When both move together, the company is reinvesting at the same quality it already has, and the average is a fair guide to the future.

### Reinvestment Rate

**Why read the two together.** The reinvestment rate is the share of profit the company puts back into the business rather than paying out. Marginal ROIC is the return that reinvestment earns. Growth in value is the reinvestment rate multiplied by the return on that reinvestment, so the two numbers together explain how much value future growth will add.

**What the combination tells you.** A high reinvestment rate paired with a high marginal ROIC is the best combination, because the company is plowing a lot of money into projects that pay off well. A high reinvestment rate paired with a low marginal ROIC is the worst, because management is funding value-destroying growth at scale. A low reinvestment rate with a high marginal ROIC suggests the company should be investing more than it is.

### ROIC − WACC Spread

**Why read the two together.** WACC is the weighted average cost of capital, the blended rate a company pays on its debt and equity. The spread is the company's ROIC minus that cost. Marginal ROIC should be judged against the same cost, because new investment only creates value when its return clears the cost of the new money.

**What the combination tells you.** A positive spread on the average combined with a marginal ROIC that has fallen below WACC means the value creation is in the past, not the present. A marginal ROIC well above WACC confirms that current investment is still adding value, even when the average spread looks ordinary. Reading the marginal return against the cost of capital tells the investor whether to want more growth or less.

### NOPAT

**Why read the two together.** NOPAT is the operating profit after tax that sits in the top of the marginal ROIC formula. Reading the NOPAT series shows whether the change driving the marginal ratio came from genuine profit growth or from a one-off swing. The ratio can be high simply because invested capital barely moved while profit jumped for a temporary reason.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising marginal ROIC backed by a steady climb in NOPAT is real improvement in returns on new capital. A rising marginal ROIC driven by a one-year NOPAT spike against flat capital is likely a temporary distortion. Watching the two together separates durable gains in capital efficiency from accounting noise.

## Part 8: Limitations

Marginal ROIC is the difference between two large numbers divided by the difference between two other large numbers, so the metric is fragile. Small measurement errors in NOPAT or invested capital are amplified into large errors in the ratio. A single acquisition, asset sale, or write-down can dominate a year's reading and reveal nothing about the underlying business. A single-year figure should never be trusted on its own.

The metric also suffers from a timing mismatch. New capital is spent before the profit it produces arrives, so a company in a heavy investment phase will look worse than the projects truly are. The definition of invested capital varies between sources, and so does the tax rate used to compute NOPAT, which makes cross-company comparison harder. The metric works best as a multi-year trend read against the company's own average ROIC and its own cost of capital, not as a precise single number.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current marginal ROIC above or below the company's own average ROIC? What does the gap say about whether new investment is better or worse than the existing base?
2. Is the current marginal ROIC above the company's cost of capital? If not, why is management still funding growth that earns less than the cost of the money?
3. Was the latest reading distorted by a single large acquisition or asset sale? What does the ratio look like with that one item removed?
4. How much of the recent change in invested capital is still ramping up and has not yet produced its full profit? What would the return be once those projects mature?
5. Over the full chart, is marginal ROIC trending up, trending down, or just noisy? Does the trend point to improving or decaying project quality?
6. Where is the new capital actually going: organic expansion, acquisitions, or working capital? Which of those uses is earning the return?
7. Does management's reinvestment rate match the marginal return? Is a high-return company reinvesting enough, or a low-return company reinvesting too much?
8. How does the marginal ROIC compare with direct competitors investing in the same markets at the same time? Who is allocating new capital best?
9. Has the tax rate or the definition of invested capital changed in a way that moved the ratio without any change in the business?
10. If marginal ROIC has fallen below the cost of capital, is management slowing investment and returning cash, or continuing to expand?
