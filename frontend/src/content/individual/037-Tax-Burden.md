# Tax Burden

## Part 1: Definition

The tax burden ratio measures how much of a company's pre-tax profit survives income tax and reaches shareholders. Pre-tax profit is the income the company earns after every operating cost and after interest, but before income tax is taken out. Accountants also call pre-tax profit earnings before tax, shortened to EBT. The ratio answers one question: of each dollar the company earned before tax, how much does the company keep?

A ratio of 0.80 means the company keeps eighty cents of every pre-tax dollar and pays twenty cents in tax. The kept fraction and the tax rate always add to one, so a tax burden of 0.80 is the same as an effective tax rate of 20 percent. The effective tax rate is the actual tax paid divided by pre-tax profit. The name of the ratio sounds negative, but a higher number is the better reading. The ratio counts what the company keeps, not what the government takes. The tax burden is one of the five levers in the DuPont ROE Decomposition, the breakdown that splits return on equity into its causes.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| 1.00 | The company paid no income tax this year. Losses carried from earlier years or special tax breaks are usually the cause. |
| 0.85 to 0.95 | A light tax charge. The company benefits from low-tax foreign income, tax credits, or favorable rules. |
| 0.70 to 0.85 | A normal range for most companies in most countries. |
| Below 0.70 | A heavy tax charge. High statutory rates or one-off tax items are usually the cause. |
| Above 1.00 | A tax benefit was recorded rather than a tax cost. The company booked income from reversing past taxes. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Tax Burden = Net Income ÷ Pre-Tax Income`

Net income is the top number. Net income is the final profit left after every cost, including income tax. Net income is the figure that belongs to shareholders and feeds earnings per share. The top number is placed after tax on purpose, because the ratio is measuring the size of the tax bite.

Pre-tax income is the bottom number. Pre-tax income is the same profit measured one step earlier, before income tax is subtracted. The two numbers differ only by the income tax line. Dividing the after-tax figure by the before-tax figure isolates the effect of tax alone and removes every operating and financing effect, because those effects already sit in both the top and the bottom number.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Two companies can earn the same operating profit and still deliver different returns to shareholders, and tax is often the reason. The tax burden ratio strips out the tax effect so an investor can see it on its own. The DuPont ROE Decomposition separates tax from operations and from financing so that no single strong year hides a weakness in another lever.

The ratio answers a question that profit margins do not. A margin shows how well the business runs. The tax burden shows how much of that result the company actually keeps after the government's claim. A company can run a strong business and still hand an unusual share of its profit to the tax authority.

The ratio also flags how durable a high return really is. A return on equity lifted by an unusually low tax charge can fall the moment the tax break expires. Reading the tax burden on its own tells the investor whether good results rest on the business or on a tax position that may not last.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The statutory tax rate is the first driver. The statutory rate is the headline rate the government sets for company profits. A cut in that rate lifts the tax burden ratio for every company at once, which can make a year look better without any change in the business.

Where the company earns its profit is the second driver. Income earned in low-tax countries is taxed less, so a company that shifts sales abroad can raise its tax burden ratio. Tax credits, deductions, and prior-year losses carried forward are the third driver. A loss carried forward lets a company offset current profit and pay little or no tax until the stored loss runs out.

One-off items move the ratio for reasons unrelated to the ongoing business. A large legal settlement, an asset write-down, or a change in tax law can push the effective tax rate far from normal in a single year. A single-year tax burden near 1.00 or above it almost always reflects one of these one-off items rather than a permanently low tax position.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level depends mostly on where a company operates and what tax rules apply to its industry. A cross-country comparison is only fair after accounting for different national tax rates.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic-only companies | Close to the local statutory rate | Profit is taxed mostly in one country at one rate, so the ratio is stable and predictable. |
| Global technology and pharmaceutical companies | Often high, above 0.80 | Profit is booked in low-tax countries and intellectual property is held in favorable locations. |
| Capital-heavy industrials and utilities | Variable | Large depreciation deductions and investment tax credits can lower the tax charge for years at a time. |
| Companies recovering from losses | Near 1.00 for several years | Losses carried forward shield profit from tax until the stored losses are used up. |
| Energy and mining | Variable | Special resource taxes, royalties, and depletion allowances make the tax charge swing with policy and prices. |

A cross-sector comparison of the tax burden alone is rarely meaningful, because tax differences reflect geography and tax law rather than business quality. Compare a company against direct peers that operate in the same countries.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady tax burden ratio means a stable tax position, which is the normal and healthy pattern. A steady level near the home country's statutory rate suggests the company is not relying on fragile tax structures. A steady rise in the ratio means the company is keeping more of its pre-tax profit over time, often through more foreign income or new tax credits.

A steady decline means a heavier tax charge year after year, which slowly drags down return on equity even when the business is unchanged. A step change in a single year usually marks a tax law change or a one-off tax item, not a lasting shift. The base effect to watch is a year with very low pre-tax income. When the bottom number is small, an ordinary tax amount produces a wild ratio, so a tax burden far from its usual level deserves a check of the pre-tax profit that produced it.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Margin

**Why read the two together.** Net margin is net income divided by revenue, the share of each sales dollar left after every cost including tax. The tax burden is one of the pieces hidden inside net margin. A change in net margin can come from the business or from tax, and reading the two together separates the two causes. The tax burden isolates the tax part so the investor can see whether a margin move is operational or fiscal.

**What the combination tells you.** A net margin that improves while the tax burden is flat means the gain came from the business itself, which is the durable kind. A net margin that improves only because the tax burden rose means the gain came from a lighter tax charge, which can reverse. Reading the pair shows whether better bottom-line profit reflects a stronger company or a temporary tax advantage. The combination is the quickest way to test the quality of a margin improvement.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is operating income divided by revenue, the profit from running the business before interest and tax. The tax burden sits two steps further down the income statement, after interest and at the tax line. Placing the operating lever next to the tax lever shows where a company's return is actually made. Strong operations with a heavy tax charge tell a very different story from weak operations with a light tax charge.

**What the combination tells you.** A high EBIT margin paired with a low tax burden means a strong business that loses an unusual share to tax, so the after-tax return understates the operating quality. A modest EBIT margin paired with a high tax burden means a plainer business that happens to keep most of what it earns. Reading both reveals whether the company's return on equity is driven by how it operates or by how it is taxed. The pairing keeps an investor from crediting the business for a result that the tax code produced.

### DuPont ROE Decomposition

**Why read the two together.** The DuPont ROE Decomposition splits return on equity into five levers, and the tax burden is the lever closest to the bottom line. Reading the tax burden inside the full decomposition shows its weight relative to operations, efficiency, and leverage. A single lever means little until it is placed beside the other four. The decomposition supplies that context.

**What the combination tells you.** When the decomposition shows return on equity rising mainly because the tax burden rose, the improvement is at risk of reversing if tax conditions change. When the tax burden is flat and the operating levers are doing the work, the return is built on the business and is more durable. Reading the tax burden within the decomposition tells the investor which engine is driving the return. The full split prevents a tax-driven gain from being mistaken for a business-driven one.

## Part 8: Limitations

The tax burden ratio measures the accounting tax charge, not the cash tax paid. A company can report a high tax burden while deferring much of the actual cash payment to future years, so the ratio can overstate how much tax really left the business this year. The ratio also says nothing about why the tax charge is high or low, so a number on its own cannot tell durable tax planning from a one-off item.

The ratio breaks down at the extremes. When pre-tax income is near zero, a small tax amount produces a huge or negative ratio that carries no real meaning. When the company records a tax benefit rather than a tax cost, the ratio rises above 1.00 and no longer behaves like a fraction kept. A very low tax burden may reflect smart, lasting tax structure or a single fragile loophole, and the ratio alone cannot tell which. The tax burden is a useful lever inside the DuPont ROE Decomposition and a weak standalone judge of company quality.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current tax burden close to the statutory rate in the company's main country, or far from it? What explains the gap?
2. How much of the company's profit is earned abroad in low-tax countries? Would the ratio fall sharply if those tax advantages ended?
3. Is the company using losses carried forward from earlier years to lower its tax? How many more years can that shield last?
4. Did any one-off tax item, such as a law change or a settlement, move the ratio this year? What is the tax burden with that item removed?
5. Does the cash tax actually paid match the accounting tax charge, or is a large share of the tax being deferred?
6. How does the tax burden compare with direct competitors that operate in the same countries? Is this company taxed unusually lightly or heavily?
7. If return on equity rose this year, how much of the rise came from a lower tax charge rather than from the business?
8. Are any tax credits or special incentives set to expire soon? What would the ratio become once they end?
9. Has management described its tax position as sustainable, or does it depend on structures that regulators are challenging?
10. Over the multi-year chart, is the tax burden stable, or does it swing with one-off items that make the trend hard to trust?
