# Sales And Maturities Of Investments

## Part 1: Definition

Sales and maturities of investments is the cash a company received during the year from securities that were sold or that matured. A security is a financial holding such as a government bond, a money-market fund, or shares of another company. A security matures when it reaches its end date and the issuer pays the holder back. The line answers one question: how much cash came back into the company from its securities this year?

Sales and maturities of investments sits in the investing section of the cash flow statement. The investing section records cash spent to buy long-term assets and cash received from selling them. Selling or redeeming securities brings cash in, so this line is always an inflow. An inflow raises the company's cash. The line is reported as a positive number.

This line is a gross figure. Gross means it shows only the cash coming back, with no buying subtracted. The matching line for buying is reported separately as purchases of investments. The two lines are a matched pair. Reading them together shows the full round trip of the company's securities.

| What the line shows | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Large inflows every year | Securities are maturing or being sold and rolled into new ones, a normal sign of an active securities pile. |
| Inflows above purchases | The company is pulling cash back out of securities, often to fund spending elsewhere. |
| Inflows near zero | The company holds few securities, or holds long-term ones that have not yet matured. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Sales and maturities of investments is not a ratio. The line is a direct cash total taken from the company's records.

`Sales And Maturities Of Investments = total cash received from selling or redeeming securities during the year`

The number counts cash from two sources. The first is sales, where the company sells a security to another party before its end date. The second is maturities, where a security reaches its end date and the issuer pays the holder back. Both bring cash into the company, so both belong on this line.

The line shows cash only. A security that rises in market value but stays in the pile does not appear here, because no cash changed hands. Only an actual sale or maturity paid in cash is counted. The cash-only rule keeps the line tied to real cash movement.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Sales and maturities of investments shows cash returning from the company's securities. A company that parks spare cash in securities will see that cash come back when the securities sell or mature. The line measures the size of that return flow.

The line also explains where a company found cash in a given year. A company short on operating cash can raise money by selling securities instead of borrowing. The line reveals when the company tapped its securities pile to fund spending, dividends, or debt repayment.

Reading sales and maturities together with the buying line shows the company's true direction with its securities. Cash coming back faster than cash going out means the pile is shrinking. Cash going out faster than cash coming back means the pile is growing.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The mix of securities the company holds is the main driver. Short-term securities mature quickly, so a company holding mostly short-term holdings shows a large maturities figure every year as old holdings pay back. A company holding long-term securities shows a smaller figure, because each holding stays in place for years before it matures.

The company's need for cash also moves the line. A company that runs short of operating cash will sell securities to cover the gap, pushing this line up. A company flush with cash has no reason to sell, so the line reflects only natural maturities.

A one-time event can spike the line. The company may liquidate a large block of securities to fund an acquisition, a big dividend, or a debt repayment. The sales figure jumps that year for a reason unrelated to normal operations.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as normal depends on the size and mix of the company's securities pile.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cash-rich technology and pharmaceutical companies | Large inflows | Big short-term securities piles mature and roll over constantly, producing large maturities figures. |
| Insurers and banks | Enormous inflows | Selling and redeeming securities is the core of the business, so the line is huge by design. |
| Capital-heavy manufacturers and utilities | Small inflows | These companies hold few securities, so little cash returns from them. |
| Companies funding a big outlay | A one-year spike | Securities are liquidated to pay for an acquisition, a dividend, or a debt repayment. |
| Young or unprofitable companies | Inflows near zero | These companies hold no meaningful securities pile to draw cash from. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is meaningless. The line measures cash-management style, not business quality. Compare a company only against its own history and against direct peers with similar securities holdings.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read this line as a percent of revenue across the multi-year chart, because revenue scales the company and makes the years comparable. A steady level year after year means the company runs a stable securities program. Holdings mature and roll over at a constant pace.

The key reading is sales and maturities against purchases in the same years. When the inflow steadily exceeds the buying, the securities pile is shrinking and cash is being pulled back into the business. When the inflow steadily trails the buying, the pile is growing. When the two move together at a high level, the company is simply rolling short-term securities with little change in the pile.

A single-year spike usually marks a one-time liquidation to fund a large outlay. High year-to-year volatility is normal when the company holds short-term securities, because the maturities total swings with the size and timing of the pile. Always read this line beside the purchases line. The inflow alone tells half the story. The net of the two tells the real direction.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Purchases Of Investments

**Why read the two together.** Sales and maturities of investments shows only the cash coming back from securities. Purchases of investments shows the cash going out to buy them. The two lines are a matched pair. Reading the inflow alone hides whether the securities pile is growing or shrinking.

**What the combination tells you.** Sales above purchases means the company is pulling cash out of securities, often to fund spending elsewhere. Purchases above sales means the company is building its pile. Two large lines that roughly cancel mean the company is simply rolling short-term securities with little change in the pile. The net of the two is the figure that shows the company's real direction.

### Net Cash Used For Investing Activities

**Why read the two together.** Sales and maturities of investments is one line inside the investing section. Net cash used for investing activities is the total of every line in that section. A large securities inflow can pull the whole investing total toward positive even when the company is also spending heavily on equipment.

**What the combination tells you.** A positive investing total driven mainly by securities sales is not the same as a company that genuinely needs no investment. Reading the securities inflow against the section total separates one-time securities liquidation from a real pullback in business investment. The split keeps a busy securities program from disguising the company's true spending on long-term assets.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow measures the cash a company generates from operations after spending to maintain the business. Sales and maturities of investments is cash raised by selling securities, not cash earned by operating. A company with weak free cash flow can still show plenty of cash coming in if it is selling down its securities pile.

**What the combination tells you.** Strong free cash flow with modest securities sales means the business funds itself from operations. Weak free cash flow alongside heavy securities sales means the company is leaning on its securities pile to cover a shortfall. Reading both shows whether incoming cash is earned by the business or drawn from savings that will eventually run dry.

## Part 8: Limitations

Sales and maturities of investments tells you nothing on its own. The line shows only the cash coming back from securities. The buying line must be read beside it before any judgment about direction is possible. A huge inflow can mean a shrinking pile or simply a fast-rolling short-term pile that is fully replaced the same year.

The line also hides the reason cash came back. A maturity that the company will roll into new securities looks identical to a forced sale to cover a cash shortage. The cash total does not reveal whether the company sold securities by choice or by need, and it does not reveal whether the sale produced a gain or a loss. The line is a record of cash movement, not a measure of why the cash moved.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How does the sales and maturities line compare with the purchases line in the same year? Is the securities pile growing, shrinking, or simply being rolled?
2. Is the cash coming back from natural maturities, or from selling securities the company would rather have kept?
3. Did the company sell a large block of securities this year to fund an acquisition, a dividend, or a debt repayment?
4. Does the company hold mostly short-term or long-term securities? Does the large inflow reflect frequent maturities of a steady pile?
5. Is the company leaning on its securities pile to cover weak operating cash flow? How many more years could the pile fund such gaps?
6. Did the securities sales produce gains or losses? Does the company disclose the outcome?
7. How does the inflow move across the multi-year chart as a percent of revenue? Is the pattern steady, rising, falling, or driven by one-time events?
8. Is the securities pile shrinking year after year? If so, where is the cash going, and is the company running down a buffer it may later need?
9. Does the company explain its securities strategy in its filings? Does the stated strategy match what the purchases and sales lines actually show?
10. If the company stopped selling securities, would operating cash flow alone cover its spending, dividends, and debt payments?
