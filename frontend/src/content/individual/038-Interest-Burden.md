# Interest Burden

## Part 1: Definition

The interest burden ratio measures how much of a company's operating profit survives the cost of its debt. Operating profit is the income the business earns from normal operations before interest and tax. Analysts also call operating profit EBIT, short for earnings before interest and taxes. The ratio answers one question: of each dollar of operating profit, how much is left after interest is paid?

A ratio of 0.95 means that after interest, ninety-five cents of every operating dollar remains for tax and shareholders. A ratio of 0.70 means lenders take nearly a third of operating profit before anyone else is paid. The name sounds negative, but a higher number is the better reading. A ratio of 1.00 marks a company whose financing costs its operating profit nothing at all, usually because the company carries little or no debt. The interest burden is one of the five levers in the DuPont ROE Decomposition, the breakdown that splits return on equity into its causes.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| 1.00 | The company pays no net interest. Debt is tiny or interest earned on cash offsets interest paid. |
| 0.90 to 1.00 | A light debt cost. Borrowing trims only a small slice of operating profit. |
| 0.75 to 0.90 | A moderate debt cost typical of companies that use some borrowing. |
| 0.50 to 0.75 | A heavy debt cost. Interest consumes a large share of operating profit. |
| Below 0.50 | Interest claims most of operating profit. The company has little room before a weak year wipes out the rest. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Interest Burden = Pre-Tax Income ÷ Operating Income`

Pre-tax income is the top number. Pre-tax income is the profit left after interest is subtracted but before income tax is taken out. Accountants also call pre-tax income earnings before tax, shortened to EBT. The top number sits after interest on purpose, because the ratio is measuring the size of the interest bite.

Operating income is the bottom number. Operating income is the profit from running the business, measured before interest and tax. The two numbers differ mainly by the interest line, so dividing the after-interest figure by the before-interest figure isolates the effect of interest alone. The ratio strips out operations and tax, because operating profit is fixed in the denominator and tax has not yet been charged.

Some companies report non-operating items, such as gains on investments, between operating income and pre-tax income. When those items are large, the ratio reflects more than interest alone, which is a point to watch when reading the number.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The interest burden ratio isolates the cost of debt within a company's profit chain. Two companies can run equally strong operations and still deliver different returns to shareholders because one carries more debt than the other. The DuPont ROE Decomposition separates this financing effect from operations and from tax so an investor can see each force on its own.

The ratio answers a question that operating margins do not. A margin shows how well the business runs. The interest burden shows how much of that operating result the lenders take before tax and shareholders are served. A strong business loaded with debt can still leave little for its owners.

The ratio also signals risk. A low interest burden means interest already claims a large share of operating profit, so a small drop in operations can push pre-tax profit toward zero. Reading the interest burden tells the investor how much cushion sits between today's operating result and the point where debt costs overwhelm it.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The amount of debt is the first driver. More borrowing raises the yearly interest bill and lowers the ratio. Borrowing to fund acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends pushes the interest burden down even when the business itself is unchanged.

Interest rates are the second driver. Debt with a floating rate costs more as soon as market rates rise, which lowers the ratio quickly. Debt with a fixed rate costs more only at refinancing, when an old loan is replaced with a new one at current rates. A ratio that looks stable may rest on old cheap loans that disappear at the next refinancing.

The level of operating profit is the third driver, because operating profit is the denominator. When operating profit falls, the same interest bill consumes a larger share, so the ratio drops even though no new debt was added. Non-operating gains and losses are a further influence. A large one-off gain booked below operating income can lift pre-tax profit and push the ratio above what interest alone would produce, which flatters the reading for a single year.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level depends on how much debt an industry typically carries and how steady its operating profit is. Steady operating profit makes a low ratio safer than the same ratio in a volatile business.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and other low-debt companies | Near 1.00 | Many carry little debt, so interest barely touches operating profit. |
| Established industrials | 0.80 to 0.95 | Moderate borrowing trims a predictable slice of operating profit. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | 0.65 to 0.85 | Heavy borrowing is normal, but stable regulated revenue makes the lower ratio safe. |
| Real estate and infrastructure | 0.50 to 0.75 | Assets are funded with large amounts of debt, so interest takes a big share of operating profit. |
| Companies owned by buyout firms | Often below 0.60 | Debt is loaded on deliberately, so interest consumes much of operating profit. |
| Banks | Not applicable | Interest is the core cost of the banking business rather than a financing expense, so the ratio is not meaningful. |

A cross-sector comparison of the interest burden is rarely fair, because a low ratio reflects a heavily financed industry rather than a weak company. Compare a company against direct peers with similar debt structures.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady interest burden near 1.00 means debt never weighs much on the business. A steady level well below 1.00 means management runs the company with a permanent layer of debt, which is fine when operating profit is dependable. The pattern that deserves the most attention is steady decline. A ratio that steps down year after year means debt is growing faster than operating profit, or new loans carry higher rates than old ones.

A sharp drop in a single year is often an operating problem rather than a debt problem, because a fall in operating profit shrinks the denominator and pulls the ratio down. The drop still matters, because it shows how thin the cushion becomes in a weak year. The base effect to watch is a year with low operating profit. When the denominator is small, the ratio swings hard, so an interest burden far from its usual level deserves a check of the operating profit that produced it. A large non-operating gain can also fake an improving trend by lifting pre-tax profit for one year.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest coverage is operating income divided by interest expense, the number of times operating profit could pay the interest bill. The interest burden measures the same relationship from the other side, as the fraction of operating profit left after interest. The two are linked: a high interest coverage produces a high interest burden, and a low coverage produces a low burden. Reading both gives the same risk in two forms that suit different questions.

**What the combination tells you.** Interest coverage states debt safety as a multiple, which is easy to compare against a rule of thumb such as five times. The interest burden states the same safety as the slice of profit lost to interest, which plugs directly into return on equity. A coverage near one matches an interest burden near zero, and both warn that interest is about to swallow operating profit. Reading the pair lets an investor judge debt safety and see exactly how that safety feeds the bottom line.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net Debt divided by EBITDA measures the total size of the debt in years of earnings, where EBITDA is operating profit with depreciation and amortization added back. The interest burden measures the yearly cost of that debt rather than its size. Size and cost are different questions, and a full view of debt risk needs both. A large debt at cheap rates can show a modest interest burden, which the size measure alone would miss.

**What the combination tells you.** A high Net Debt to EBITDA paired with a healthy interest burden means a large debt carried at low rates, which is exposed to refinancing risk when rates rise. A low Net Debt to EBITDA paired with a low interest burden points to expensive debt or weak operating profit rather than a large load. Reading both separates the amount of the debt from the cost of the debt. The pairing shows whether a comfortable interest burden today rests on cheap rates that may not last.

### DuPont ROE Decomposition

**Why read the two together.** The DuPont ROE Decomposition splits return on equity into five levers, and the interest burden is the financing lever. Reading the interest burden inside the full decomposition shows its weight next to the operating and tax levers. The decomposition places it beside the efficiency lever and the equity multiplier as well. A single lever means little until it sits beside the others. The decomposition provides that context.

**What the combination tells you.** When the decomposition shows return on equity propped up by a high equity multiplier while the interest burden is falling, the company is borrowing more and paying more for it, a combination that can reverse sharply. When the interest burden is stable and the operating levers do the work, the return rests on the business. Reading the interest burden within the decomposition shows whether debt is helping or quietly draining the return. The full split keeps a financing effect from being mistaken for an operating one.

## Part 8: Limitations

The interest burden ratio uses accounting profit, not cash, so it can mislead when large non-operating items sit between operating income and pre-tax income. A one-off gain can lift the ratio above what interest alone justifies, and a one-off loss can sink it for reasons that have nothing to do with debt. The ratio also reflects the rates on existing loans rather than the rates the company will face at the next refinancing, so at the end of a cheap-money period the ratio flatters almost every borrower.

The ratio breaks down at the extremes. When operating income is near zero or negative, the ratio gives a huge or meaningless number exactly when debt risk matters most. The ratio says nothing about when the debt comes due, what conditions the loans carry, or whether the debt is in a foreign currency. A company can show a comfortable interest burden while a wall of repayments looms just outside the income statement. The interest burden is a useful lever inside the DuPont ROE Decomposition and a partial measure of debt risk on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current interest burden produced by low debt, by cheap old loans, or by both? What does the ratio become if rates reset to today's market?
2. How much of the debt has a floating rate or comes due within three years? What would the interest burden be after refinancing at current rates?
3. Did any large non-operating gain or loss move the ratio this year? What is the interest burden with that item removed?
4. In the company's worst operating year on the chart, how much of operating profit did interest consume?
5. Has recent borrowing funded acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends rather than investment in the business? Is the interest burden falling as a result?
6. How does the interest burden compare with direct competitors that carry similar debt? Is this company paying an unusually high cost for its borrowing?
7. If return on equity rose this year, did a higher equity multiplier come with a lower interest burden? Is the leverage helping or hurting after interest?
8. What loan conditions reference interest cover or debt levels? How large a fall in operating profit would breach them?
9. Is any interest being earned on cash and netted against interest paid? Does that net make the ratio look better than the gross debt cost?
10. Over the multi-year chart, is the interest burden stable, or does it drift down as debt grows faster than operating profit?
