# Goodwill / Assets

## Part 1: Definition

Goodwill / Assets measures the share of a company's total assets that is goodwill. Goodwill is the premium a buyer pays in an acquisition above the fair value of the acquired company's net assets. An acquisition is the purchase of one company by another. Net assets are the acquired company's assets minus its liabilities, and fair value is the price those assets would fetch on the open market. Total assets are everything the company owns, measured at balance-sheet value. The ratio answers one question: how much of the balance sheet rests on prices paid above book value in past acquisitions?

Book value is the worth of an asset as recorded in the company's accounts. A high ratio means acquisitions built much of the company. A low ratio means the company grew mostly by building its own operations. The figure is shown as a percent so companies of different sizes compare on the same scale.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The company grew by building its own operations and made few acquisitions. Almost no premium sits on the balance sheet. |
| 10 to 25 percent | Acquisitions play a part, but physical and other assets still dominate. |
| 25 to 50 percent | Goodwill is a major share of the balance sheet. The company has grown heavily by buying others. |
| Above 50 percent | Goodwill outweighs the company's physical assets. Much of the balance sheet is the premium paid in deals. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Goodwill / Assets = Goodwill ÷ Total Assets`

Goodwill is the top number. Goodwill is the accounting entry a buyer records when it pays more for a company than the fair value of that company's net assets. The premium covers things that do not appear on the seller's balance sheet. Examples include its brand, its customer relationships, and its trained workforce. Goodwill sits on the buyer's balance sheet as an asset after the deal closes.

Total assets is the bottom number. Total assets is the sum of everything the company owns. The total includes cash, equipment, and goodwill among other holdings. Goodwill is part of total assets, so the ratio shows goodwill as a fraction of the whole.

Under current accounting rules goodwill is not written down a little each year. Goodwill stays on the balance sheet at its recorded amount and is tested once a year. If the test finds the acquired business is worth less than the goodwill implies, the company records an impairment. An impairment is a writedown (a reduction in an asset's recorded value) that lowers both goodwill and reported profit.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The ratio flags how much of a company's value rests on past deals rather than on assets you can touch or count. A balance sheet loaded with goodwill is backed less by cash, inventory, and equipment. The quality of the acquisition decisions determines whether the goodwill is worth what it says.

A high ratio raises the risk of a future impairment. When an acquired business underperforms, its goodwill is written down, and the charge flows straight through to reported profit. The more goodwill on the books, the larger the potential writedown hanging over future earnings.

A large impairment is a delayed admission that an acquisition was overpriced. The premium looked justified on the day of the deal, and the writedown years later concedes it was not. Companies tend to delay these charges as long as the rules allow, so an impairment often confirms a problem the market already suspected.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Acquisition activity is the main driver. Each deal done above the fair value of the target's net assets adds goodwill to the balance sheet. A company that grows by buying others will see the ratio climb deal by deal.

Impairment charges pull the ratio down. A writedown removes goodwill from the balance sheet in one step, so the ratio falls the year a charge is taken. The size of the rest of the balance sheet matters too, because a company that also holds heavy equipment or inventory will show a lower goodwill share than a company that owns little equipment but did the same deals.

How the company grows sets the long-run level. A firm that builds its own factories, brands, and products keeps goodwill low. A firm that buys those things through acquisitions carries the premium as goodwill instead.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | Typical level | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Serial acquirers in software, healthcare, and media | High | Growth comes from buying companies, and much of each price is a premium booked as goodwill. |
| Capital-intensive producers and miners | Low | Value sits in physical plant and reserves, which are recorded as physical assets rather than goodwill. |
| Consumer brands built by acquisition | High | Large premiums are paid for established brand names, and the premium lands in goodwill. |
| Banks | Variable | Goodwill appears when a bank buys another bank, but a large asset base of loans keeps the share modest. |
| Young companies grown from within | Near zero | Little has been acquired, so almost no goodwill exists. |

A cross-sector comparison of the ratio is meaningless when one sector grows by acquisition and another grows by building physical assets, because the two record value in different places on the balance sheet.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in the ratio means the company is growing through acquisitions and adding goodwill with each deal. A reader should check whether the acquired businesses are earning enough to justify the premiums piling up. Growth by acquisition is not a problem on its own, but a rising goodwill share raises the stakes if the deals disappoint.

A sudden drop almost always marks an impairment. The company has conceded that an acquired business is worth less than it paid, and the writedown cut the goodwill in one year. A single large step up in the line usually marks one big acquisition that closed that year. A long flat line at a high level means the goodwill has neither grown through new deals nor been written down, which can mean the acquisitions are holding up or that a needed impairment is being postponed.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROA

**Why read the two together.** ROA is return on assets, the profit a company earns for each dollar of assets it holds. Goodwill sits inside total assets, which is the denominator of ROA. A large goodwill pile inflates that denominator and drags ROA down even when the operating business is sound. Reading the goodwill share next to ROA shows how much of a weak return is caused by carrying goodwill.

**What the combination tells you.** A low ROA paired with a high goodwill share often means the company paid up for acquisitions that have not yet earned their keep. A healthy ROA despite a high goodwill share means the acquired businesses are producing enough profit to justify the premiums. Watching both over time shows whether new deals are lifting returns or just enlarging the asset base. The pairing separates a genuine earnings problem from the drag of a goodwill-heavy balance sheet.

### Tangible Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Tangible Book Value Per Share is the company's equity per share after goodwill and other intangible assets (non-physical assets such as patents and brand value) are removed. Goodwill / Assets measures how much goodwill is on the books, and tangible book value strips that goodwill out entirely. Reading them together shows what the balance sheet is worth once the acquisition premiums are set aside. The two look at the same goodwill from opposite directions.

**What the combination tells you.** A high goodwill share paired with a thin tangible book value means most of the company's recorded equity is acquisition premium rather than hard assets. A large impairment could wipe out much of that equity in a single charge. A low goodwill share with a solid tangible book value means the balance sheet stands on assets that would survive a writedown. The pairing reveals how much real net worth sits beneath the goodwill.

### Net Income

**Why read the two together.** Net Income is the company's bottom-line profit after all costs, including any goodwill impairment. A goodwill writedown is charged against net income in the year it is taken. A company with a high goodwill share carries the risk of a large future charge that could turn a profit into a loss. Reading net income next to the goodwill share keeps that risk in view.

**What the combination tells you.** Steady net income alongside a high and stable goodwill share suggests the acquisitions are performing and no writedown is looming. A sudden loss driven by an impairment confirms that an acquired business fell short of its price. Tracking the two together shows whether the goodwill on the books is being supported by real earnings. The pairing links the balance-sheet premium to the profit it is supposed to produce.

## Part 8: Limitations

Goodwill is recorded at historical cost and is never marked up, so the ratio reflects old deal prices rather than current worth. A high ratio is not automatically bad, because a premium paid for a business that earns strong returns can be money well spent. The ratio says nothing about whether the acquired operations are actually performing, only about how much premium sits on the books.

Impairment timing is left largely to management judgment, so the ratio can stay high long after an acquisition has soured. The goodwill number also depends on accounting choices made at the time of each deal, such as how much of the price was assigned to goodwill instead of other intangible assets. Comparisons across companies are rough, because two firms can book the same deal differently. The ratio is a useful gauge of acquisition risk, not a verdict on any single deal.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What share of total assets is goodwill, and how much of that goodwill came from a single large acquisition?
2. Are the businesses behind the goodwill earning enough to justify the premiums that were paid?
3. Has the company taken any goodwill impairments in the past, and what did those writedowns reveal about its acquisition record?
4. Is the goodwill share rising because of new deals, or holding flat while a needed writedown is postponed?
5. How much would tangible book value fall if the goodwill were written off in full?
6. Does the company grow mainly by acquisition or by building its own operations?
7. How does the goodwill share compare with direct competitors that grew the same way?
8. What were the prices and the stated reasons for the largest deals that created the goodwill?
9. Would a large impairment turn the company's reported profit into a loss?
