# PEG Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The PEG ratio measures whether a stock's price is reasonable given how fast the company's profit is expected to grow. PEG stands for price/earnings to growth. The ratio takes the price-to-earnings ratio and divides it by the expected growth rate of earnings. The price-to-earnings ratio, often shortened to P/E, is the share price divided by the earnings per share. The ratio shows how many years of current profit a buyer pays for the shares. The PEG ratio answers one question: is the price of the growth fair?

A PEG of 1.0 means the price-to-earnings ratio equals the growth rate. A company trading at 30 times earnings while growing 30 percent a year scores a PEG of 1.0. The same company growing only 10 percent a year scores 3.0, which means the price is high for the growth on offer. A PEG below 1.0 means the growth is being sold cheaply. A PEG well above 1.0 means the growth is already paid for and then some. The idea was made popular by the investor Peter Lynch.

Common readings of the ratio:

| PEG level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | The price-to-earnings ratio is lower than the growth rate. The growth may be underpriced. |
| Around 1.0 | The price matches the growth. A common rule of thumb for fair value. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | The price runs ahead of the growth. Common for high-quality companies. |
| Above 2.0 | The growth is fully priced. A small miss on growth can cut the price sharply. |
| Negative or undefined | Earnings or the growth rate is negative or zero, so the ratio cannot be read. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`PEG = P/E ÷ Expected Earnings Growth Rate`

The price-to-earnings ratio is the top number. The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. Earnings per share is the company's net profit divided by the number of shares. The price-to-earnings ratio on its own says nothing about growth. A high price-to-earnings ratio can be fair for a fast grower and far too high for a slow one. The PEG ratio exists to settle that question.

The expected earnings growth rate is the bottom number. The growth rate is the percentage by which earnings per share are expected to rise each year, usually over the next several years. The growth rate is entered as a plain number, so 15 percent growth is entered as 15. Dividing the price-to-earnings ratio by this number tests whether the price paid per year of profit is in line with how fast that profit is climbing. The growth figure is a forecast, which is the weakest part of the ratio.

| Variant | What it uses | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Trailing PEG | Price-to-earnings ratio from the last reported year over past growth | Uses figures that already happened. Backward-looking, but it does not depend on a forecast. |
| Forward PEG | Price-to-earnings ratio over forecast growth for the next few years | The version most analysts use. The vault notes the forecast must be measured the same way for every company being compared. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The price-to-earnings ratio alone cannot tell a cheap stock from an expensive one. A company at 40 times earnings can be cheaper than a company at 12 times earnings if the first grows far faster. The PEG ratio brings growth into the comparison. The ratio lets an investor judge a fast-growing company without rejecting it for a high price-to-earnings ratio.

The ratio is most useful for growth companies, where the plain price-to-earnings ratio looks alarming but the growth may justify it. A standalone price-to-earnings ratio ignores the future. The PEG ratio puts the price and the growth side by side in a single number.

The ratio also gives a quick rule of thumb. A PEG near 1.0 suggests the price is fair. A PEG far above 1.0 suggests caution. The vault shows that average PEG ratios differ sharply across groups, so the rule of thumb works best within a single sector rather than across the whole market.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The share price moves the price-to-earnings ratio at the top. A rising price lifts the price-to-earnings ratio and pushes the PEG higher, even when the growth forecast has not changed. Reported earnings also move the top number. A weak quarter lowers earnings per share, lifts the price-to-earnings ratio, and raises the PEG.

The growth forecast moves the bottom number and is the most fragile input. Analysts set the forecast, and the forecast can be optimistic. A higher forecast lowers the PEG and makes the stock look cheaper. The time horizon of the forecast matters too, because a five-year growth estimate can differ from a one-year estimate. A small change in the forecast swings the ratio widely. A company growing 5 percent shows a very different PEG if the forecast moves to 7 percent than a company growing 30 percent shows when its forecast moves by the same two points.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

A fair PEG in one sector can be high in another. The vault reports that technology stocks tend to carry lower PEG ratios on average than non-technology stocks, so a flat rule of 1.0 misreads many sectors.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Fast-growing technology | Often below 1.5 | High growth shrinks the ratio. The vault finds tech PEG ratios run lower than the market average. |
| Mature consumer goods | Around 1.5 to 2.5 | Slow steady growth means a modest price-to-earnings ratio is divided by a small growth number. |
| Cyclical industrials | Hard to read | Forecast growth swings with the cycle, so the ratio jumps around year to year. |
| Banks and insurers | Less reliable | Earnings depend on interest rates and loan losses, which makes growth forecasts unstable. |
| Companies with no growth or shrinking profit | Not meaningful | A zero or negative growth rate makes the ratio undefined or negative. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two sectors carry different normal growth rates or different earnings stability. Compare a company against direct peers with similar growth profiles.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows the PEG over several years next to the live number. A PEG that drifts higher means the price is rising faster than the growth outlook, so the stock is getting more expensive for the growth it offers. A PEG that drifts lower means the price is falling or the growth forecast is rising, so the growth is getting cheaper. The reason behind the move matters more than the move itself.

A sudden jump in the PEG often comes from a cut to the growth forecast rather than a price change, because a lower forecast shrinks the bottom number and inflates the ratio. Check whether the price moved or the forecast moved. Watch for base effects after a weak year. A company recovering from a poor year can show very high forecast growth off a low base, which pushes the PEG down and makes the stock look cheap for a reason that will not last. Read the trend together with the forecasts that drove it.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### P/E

**Why read the two together.** The PEG ratio is built from the price-to-earnings ratio, so the two are linked by design. The price-to-earnings ratio shows the raw price per year of profit. The PEG ratio shows whether that price is fair given the growth. Reading the pair shows whether a high price-to-earnings ratio is a warning or is justified by fast growth.

**What the combination tells you.** A high price-to-earnings ratio with a low PEG means the high price is backed by strong growth, which can be reasonable. A high price-to-earnings ratio with a high PEG means the price is high and the growth does not support it. A low price-to-earnings ratio with a high PEG means the company is cheap on price but barely growing. The pair separates the price from the growth that is meant to justify it.

### EPS

**Why read the two together.** EPS, earnings per share, sits inside both the top and the bottom of the PEG ratio. EPS sets the price-to-earnings ratio, and the growth rate of EPS is the bottom number. The PEG ratio depends entirely on EPS being measured cleanly. A distorted EPS distorts the whole ratio.

**What the combination tells you.** A PEG built on EPS that is inflated by one-off gains is unreliable, because the growth rate is measured off a false base. Checking the quality of EPS shows whether the growth in the PEG is real. Steady, recurring EPS growth supports the PEG. Lumpy EPS driven by special items makes the ratio meaningless even when the number looks attractive.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC, return on invested capital, measures how much profit the company earns on the money it puts to work. The PEG ratio assumes the forecast growth is worth paying for. Growth only creates value when ROIC is above the company's cost of capital. ROIC tells whether the growth in the PEG is profitable growth.

**What the combination tells you.** A low PEG paired with high ROIC is the strongest combination, because cheap growth is also profitable growth. A low PEG paired with low ROIC is a warning, because the company is growing by spending money that earns poor returns. Reading the two together shows whether the growth that makes the PEG look cheap is building value or destroying it.

## Part 8: Limitations

The PEG ratio depends on a growth forecast, and forecasts are often wrong. A single optimistic forecast can make an expensive stock look cheap. The ratio also assumes a straight-line relationship between the price-to-earnings ratio and growth, which does not hold for very high or very low growth rates. A company growing 50 percent a year does not deserve a price-to-earnings ratio of exactly 50. The ratio ignores risk, so two companies with the same PEG can carry very different chances of missing the forecast.

The ratio cannot be computed when growth is zero or negative, which rules out shrinking and cyclical-trough companies. The ratio also ignores dividends, so a slow grower that pays a large dividend looks worse on PEG than its total return deserves. Different analysts use different growth horizons and different earnings definitions, so two PEG figures for the same company can disagree. The PEG ratio is a useful screen and a poor final verdict.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current PEG low because the price fell or because the growth forecast rose? What does the ratio become if the forecast returns to a normal level?
2. Where does the growth forecast come from, and how many years does it cover? Has the company hit its past forecasts or missed them?
3. Is the EPS used in the ratio clean, or is it inflated by one-time gains? What is the PEG using recurring earnings only?
4. Is the forecast growth profitable growth? Does ROIC sit above the cost of capital, or is the company growing by earning poor returns?
5. How does the PEG compare with direct peers that grow at a similar speed? Is the company cheap or dear within its own sector?
6. Is the company recovering from a weak year? Does a low base inflate the forecast growth and flatter the PEG?
7. Does the company pay a large dividend that the PEG ignores? Would the stock look better once the dividend is counted?
8. How sensitive is the PEG to small changes in the forecast? Would a two-point cut to growth change the verdict?
9. Is the growth rate so high that the straight-line PEG rule breaks down? Does the ratio still make sense at that growth level?
10. What specific risk could cause the company to miss the growth forecast that the PEG depends on?
