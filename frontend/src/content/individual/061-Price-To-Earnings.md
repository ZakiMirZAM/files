# Price To Earnings

## Part 1: Definition

The price to earnings ratio measures how much investors pay for each dollar of a company's yearly profit. The price is the current share price. The earnings are the company's profit per share over a year, also called earnings per share. The ratio answers one question: how many years of current profit does the market price represent?

A ratio of 15 means investors pay fifteen dollars for every dollar of yearly profit per share. A ratio of 40 means investors pay forty dollars for that same dollar of profit, usually because they expect the profit to grow much larger. A low ratio means the market pays little for current profit, which can reflect either a bargain or a business the market expects to shrink. The ratio is a price tag, not a measure of quality, so a high or low number means nothing until it is compared with the company's growth and risk.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 10 | The market pays little for current profit. This can signal a bargain, slow growth, or a business the market fears will decline. |
| 10 to 20 | A common range for established companies with steady, moderate growth. |
| 20 to 35 | The market expects above-average growth, or treats the earnings as unusually safe and predictable. |
| Above 35 | The market expects rapid growth. The price depends heavily on that growth arriving, so the risk of disappointment is high. |
| Negative or undefined | The company has no profit, so the ratio cannot be computed and carries no meaning. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Price To Earnings = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share`

The share price is the top number. The share price is what one share trades for in the market right now. The price reflects everything investors currently believe about the company's future, so it already contains their expectations for growth and risk. A higher price for the same profit produces a higher ratio.

Earnings per share is the bottom number. Earnings per share is the company's yearly net profit divided by the number of shares. Net profit is the money left after every cost, including interest and taxes. The earnings figure can be measured three ways: trailing earnings use the last reported year, current earnings use the year in progress, and forward earnings use the year ahead that analysts forecast. Trailing earnings rest on reported facts, while forward earnings rest on estimates that may prove wrong. The app uses the reported earnings, so the ratio shown is the trailing version unless stated otherwise.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The price to earnings ratio is the most widely used measure of relative value, because the ratio puts companies of every size on the same scale. Comparing two share prices alone tells nothing, since a fifty-dollar share can be cheaper than a ten-dollar share once profit is taken into account. Dividing price by profit per share removes size and leaves a clean figure that can be compared across companies.

The ratio captures what the market expects without forcing the investor to build a full valuation model. A higher ratio means the market expects faster growth, treats the earnings as safer, or both. Three forces drive a justified ratio: a higher growth rate raises it, a higher return on equity raises it, and higher risk lowers it. Return on equity is the profit the company earns on each dollar shareholders have invested. The ratio answers a question other metrics do not: relative to its current profit, is the market treating this company as cheap or expensive?

The ratio is also a starting point for spotting mispricing. A company trading far below its peers on this ratio, with similar growth and risk, may be undervalued. A company trading far above peers must justify the premium with faster growth or safer earnings, or the price may be too high.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The ratio moves with both the price and the earnings, and the two can pull in opposite directions. The price rises and falls with market sentiment, interest rates, and shifting expectations for growth. Lower interest rates tend to lift the ratio across the whole market, because safer alternatives pay less and investors accept a higher price for stocks. A change in the growth outlook moves the price quickly, often before reported earnings change at all.

The earnings figure carries its own distortions. A one-time gain, such as selling a building, inflates net profit and pushes the ratio down for a reason unrelated to the ongoing business. A one-time loss does the reverse and can make a healthy company look expensive. Accounting choices on depreciation, on cost writeoffs, and on acquisitions all shift reported earnings between companies. Such differences make two ratios harder to compare than they look. Heavy share buybacks reduce the share count and lift earnings per share, which lowers the ratio even when total profit is flat.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

A normal ratio depends on how fast a sector grows and how safe its earnings are. Faster growth and steadier earnings both support a higher ratio.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and consumer staples | Low, often 12 to 18 | Slow but steady growth and very predictable earnings. |
| Established industrials | Moderate, often 12 to 20 | Solid earnings that move with the economy. |
| High-growth technology | High, often 25 and up | The market prices in years of rapid future growth. |
| Cyclical companies, such as miners and automakers | Misleading at the peak | Peak earnings make the ratio look low just before profit falls, so judge across a full cycle. |
| Banks | Compare within banking only | Earnings depend on lending and reserves, which differ from industrial profit. |

Compare the ratio only against companies in the same sector with similar growth and risk. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless, because a low ratio in a fast-growing sector can be expensive while a higher ratio in a stable sector can be cheap once growth and risk are taken into account.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A rising ratio over several years means the market is paying more for each dollar of profit, usually because expectations for growth are improving or the earnings are seen as safer. A falling ratio means the market is paying less, which can reflect fading growth hopes or rising risk. The trend reflects changing expectations as much as changing fundamentals, so a moving ratio always raises the question of whether the price or the earnings drove the change.

A sudden spike often comes from a collapse in earnings rather than a jump in price. When profit nearly vanishes, the small earnings figure in the denominator makes the ratio explode, which says nothing good about the company. A cyclical company shows the reverse trap: the ratio looks lowest at the peak of the cycle, when earnings are highest, just before profit falls. Read the ratio against the earnings line. A ratio falling because earnings are rising faster than price is healthy, while a ratio falling because the price is dropping signals fading confidence.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### EPS

**Why read the two together.** Earnings per share is the bottom number of the price to earnings ratio, so the ratio cannot be judged without knowing whether the earnings figure is sound. A one-time gain or loss inside earnings per share distorts the ratio for a reason unrelated to the business. Checking the earnings trend reveals whether the ratio rests on stable profit or on a single unusual year. Reading both keeps the ratio honest.

**What the combination tells you.** A low ratio built on inflated one-time earnings is a trap, because the ratio will jump once the gain does not repeat. A high ratio built on temporarily depressed earnings may be cheaper than it looks, because the ratio will fall once profit recovers. Steady, growing earnings per share behind a stable ratio means the price tag reflects real, durable profit. The pair separates a genuine valuation signal from an accounting quirk.

### PEG

**Why read the two together.** PEG divides the price to earnings ratio by the company's expected earnings growth rate. The price to earnings ratio alone cannot tell whether a high number is justified, because growth is the main reason a high ratio can be fair. PEG folds growth into the picture and shows whether the price matches the growth the company is expected to deliver. Reading the two together turns a raw price tag into a growth-adjusted one.

**What the combination tells you.** A high price to earnings ratio with a low PEG means the price looks steep but is supported by fast expected growth, so it may be reasonable. A high ratio with a high PEG means the price outruns even the growth story and the stock may be overvalued. A low ratio with a low PEG can point to a genuine bargain. The pair answers whether the market is paying a fair price for the growth on offer.

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity measures the profit a company earns on each dollar shareholders invested. A higher return on equity justifies a higher price to earnings ratio, because the company turns retained profit into growth more efficiently. The price to earnings ratio alone does not show that quality, so pairing it with return on equity explains why a premium ratio can be deserved. Reading both links the price tag to the engine behind the profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A high ratio backed by a high and stable return on equity reflects a quality business that earns its premium. A high ratio with a weak return on equity is harder to justify, because the company is not converting profit into growth efficiently. A low ratio paired with a strong return on equity can signal an overlooked bargain. The pair tests whether the price the market pays matches the quality of the company's earnings.

### EV/EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** EV/EBITDA values the whole company against its operating cash earnings before financing and accounting deductions. The price to earnings ratio values only the equity against net profit. The price to earnings ratio is distorted by debt levels and tax rates, while EV/EBITDA strips both out. Reading the two together shows whether the equity price tag and the whole-company price tag tell the same story. A wide gap between them usually points to heavy debt or unusual taxes.

**What the combination tells you.** A high price to earnings ratio next to a normal EV/EBITDA often means debt or taxes are depressing net profit and inflating the equity ratio. A low price to earnings ratio next to a high EV/EBITDA can mean the equity looks cheap only because heavy debt magnifies thin profit. When both ratios agree, the valuation signal is more reliable. The pair separates a cheap business from a cheap-looking but heavily indebted one.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio depends entirely on the earnings figure, which is an accounting number open to distortion. One-time gains and losses shift reported profit. Depreciation policy and acquisition accounting shift it further, as does the choice to expense or capitalize costs. Two firms with identical businesses can show different ratios purely because of accounting choices. The ratio also breaks down when profit is negative, because a ratio against a loss has no meaning, and many young or troubled companies fall into that gap exactly when an investor most wants to value them.

The ratio is only as good as its comparison. A ratio means nothing in isolation. A fair number depends on growth, risk, and a return on equity that all differ from company to company. Comparing firms within the same sector helps, yet even peers differ in business mix and growth profile, so a low ratio is not proof of a bargain. The ratio also ignores debt entirely, so a heavily indebted company can show a low ratio that hides real risk. The price to earnings ratio is the best first question about value and a poor last one.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio built on normal earnings, or on a one-time gain or loss that will not repeat? What does the ratio become with unusual items removed?
2. Does the ratio use trailing reported earnings or a forward estimate? If forward, how reliable is the growth the estimate assumes?
3. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors that have similar growth and risk? Is the company cheaper or more expensive than its peers, and why?
4. Is the company cyclical? If so, is the low ratio a sign of value or a peak-earnings trap just before profit falls?
5. Does the growth rate the high ratio implies look achievable, given the company's history and market position?
6. Is return on equity high enough to justify the ratio, or is the market paying a premium the business does not earn?
7. How much debt does the company carry? Could a low ratio be hiding the risk that heavy borrowing creates?
8. Have buybacks lifted earnings per share and lowered the ratio, or is the lower ratio driven by real profit growth?
9. How has the ratio moved over several years, and was the change driven mostly by the price or by the earnings?
10. Does the whole-company view from EV/EBITDA agree with the equity price tag this ratio shows, or does a gap point to debt or tax effects?
