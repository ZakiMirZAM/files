# Acquisitions Net

## Part 1: Definition

Acquisitions net is the cash a company spent buying other businesses during the year, after subtracting any cash it received from selling businesses. An acquisition is the purchase of another company or a part of one. The word net means the sales of businesses have been deducted from the purchases to give one combined figure. The line answers one question: how much cash did the company put into buying other businesses this year?

Acquisitions net sits inside the investing section of the cash flow statement. The investing section records cash spent on or received from long-term assets. Buying a business is usually a large cash outflow, so the line is normally a negative number that subtracts from cash. The spending is separate from capital expenditure, which buys equipment, because acquisitions buy whole operations rather than individual assets.

Common readings of the line:

| Line reading | What that reading usually means |
|---|---|
| Large outflow | The company made one or more sizable acquisitions, spending heavily to buy growth. |
| Zero or near zero | The company grew on its own this year without buying businesses. |
| Positive number | The company received more from selling businesses than it spent buying, a net divestment. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The line is the cash spent on acquisitions minus the cash received from selling businesses:

`Acquisitions Net = Cash paid for businesses bought − Cash received from businesses sold`

The first input is the cash paid to buy other companies, measured net of any cash that came with the acquired business. When a company buys another, it also takes on that target's bank balance, so the reported figure is the purchase price reduced by the cash acquired. The figure counts only the cash portion of a deal. A purchase paid for with the buyer's own shares does not appear here, because no cash left the business.

The second input is the cash received from selling off businesses or divisions. Selling a business brings cash in, which offsets the spending on purchases. The line nets the two so a single number shows whether the company was, on balance, a buyer or a seller of businesses this year.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Acquisitions net shows how much a company relies on buying growth rather than building it. Organic growth comes from expanding the existing business, while acquired growth comes from purchasing other companies. The line reveals which path management is taking with real cash, which matters because the two paths carry very different risks and returns.

The line is a major use of shareholder cash. Acquisitions rank alongside capital expenditure, dividends, and buybacks as a claim on the company's resources. A company that spends heavily on acquisitions has less cash for those other uses. Reading the line shows where management is choosing to deploy the company's resources.

Acquisitions also carry a poor average record, because buyers often overpay and struggle to combine the bought business with their own. The price paid above the fair value of a target's net assets is recorded as goodwill on the balance sheet. Goodwill is an intangible item that stands for what was paid for reputation, customers, and other things not separately valued. A pattern of large acquisitions followed by later goodwill write-downs is a warning that the company is destroying value through dealmaking.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Strategy is the first driver. A company built on buying competitors, often called a serial acquirer because it makes deals year after year, will show a large and recurring acquisitions line. A company that grows on its own shows little or nothing here. The presence and size of the line reflect a deliberate choice about how to grow.

Opportunity and cycle move the line next. Acquisitions cluster when targets are cheap, when credit is easy, or when an industry is consolidating. The line can be near zero for years and then spike when a large deal closes, so it is naturally lumpy rather than smooth.

Deal structure shapes what the line shows. Only the cash portion of an acquisition appears here, so a company that pays with its own shares can buy a large business while the line stays small. The line therefore understates total acquisition activity for share-funded deals. A company that sells off divisions can also turn the line positive in a year of restructuring, even while it continues to make small purchases.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

How large and how frequent acquisitions are depends on whether the industry grows by building or by buying.

| Industry type | Typical behavior of the line | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Serial acquirers and roll-ups | Large and recurring | The whole strategy is to grow by buying many smaller companies. |
| Pharmaceuticals and technology | Lumpy, sometimes very large | Firms buy products, patents, and talent rather than developing everything in house. |
| Mature, consolidating industries | Periodic large deals | Slow organic growth pushes companies to buy scale and cut costs. |
| Capital-light organic growers | Near zero | The business expands on its own and rarely needs to buy others. |
| Banks and insurers | Deal-driven and regulated | Growth by acquisition is common but heavily shaped by regulators. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is meaningless because some industries grow almost entirely by acquisition and others almost never. Judge the line against the company's own strategy and history, and against direct competitors that grow the same way.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows this line as a multi-year series and as a percent of revenue. Reading acquisitions as a percent of revenue removes the effect of the company simply getting bigger, so the relative scale of dealmaking is comparable across years. Because acquisitions are lumpy, the trend is best read over many years rather than one, where a steady stream of spending reveals a true acquirer.

A pattern of large and repeated acquisitions, growing as a percent of revenue, means the company depends on buying to grow. Such a strategy must be judged by whether the deals earned a return, not just by their size. A history that is mostly empty with occasional spikes points to a company that grows on its own and buys only when a clear opportunity appears.

Watch for base effects and for the link to other lines. A year of heavy acquisitions often comes with a jump in debt or a fall in cash, so read the line next to the balance sheet. A common warning sign is a wave of acquisitions in one period followed by goodwill write-downs a few years later, which suggests the company overpaid. Acquisitions can also flatter later operating cash flow, so a company that grows mainly by buying deserves extra scrutiny of how much of its cash generation is genuinely organic.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free Cash Flow is the cash a company generates after capital expenditure. Free Cash Flow is available to fund dividends and buybacks, as well as debt repayment and acquisitions. Acquisitions net is one of the largest competing uses of that cash. Reading the two together shows whether the company funds its dealmaking from cash it generates or from outside money. The pairing reveals how acquisitions fit into the company's overall cash priorities.

**What the combination tells you.** Acquisitions funded comfortably out of Free Cash Flow point to a company buying within its means. Acquisitions far larger than Free Cash Flow mean the deals are funded by new debt or new shares, which raises risk. Watching both lines over time shows whether a buyer is disciplined about deal size or is stretching its finances to keep acquiring.

### Goodwill

**Why read the two together.** Goodwill is the premium paid for a business above the fair value of its net assets, recorded on the balance sheet after an acquisition. Acquisitions net is the cash that creates that goodwill. Reading the two together links the cash spent on deals to the intangible value sitting on the balance sheet as a result. The pairing shows how much of the company's assets are built on past acquisitions.

**What the combination tells you.** Heavy acquisition spending that piles up large goodwill makes the balance sheet dependent on those deals working out. A later write-down of that goodwill is an admission that a past acquisition was worth less than was paid. Tracking acquisitions against rising goodwill, and watching for write-downs, shows whether the company's dealmaking has created or destroyed value.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC, return on invested capital, measures the profit a company earns on all the money invested in the business. Acquisitions add a large amount of capital, including the goodwill paid, to that invested base. Reading acquisitions against ROIC shows whether the cash spent on deals is earning an adequate return. The pairing is the clearest test of whether dealmaking pays off.

**What the combination tells you.** A heavy acquirer whose ROIC stays high is buying businesses that earn more than they cost, which creates value. A heavy acquirer whose ROIC falls after deals is paying too much, so the new capital earns too little. Watching ROIC across years of acquisition spending shows whether management's deals are disciplined investments or value destruction.

## Part 8: Limitations

Acquisitions net captures only the cash portion of deals, so it understates acquisition activity whenever a company pays with its own shares. A large share-funded purchase can leave the line small while the company has in fact doubled in size and diluted its owners. The line must be read with changes in the share count and in debt to see the full cost of a deal.

The line also says nothing about whether an acquisition was a good buy. A large outflow could be a brilliant purchase or a ruinous overpayment, and the cash figure alone cannot tell them apart. The verdict comes only later, from whether the acquired business earns a return and whether goodwill is eventually written down. Acquisitions can also inflate reported operating cash flow, because the bought company's cash generation joins the buyer's total, which can make a serial acquirer look more cash-generative than its own organic business truly is.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How much of the company's growth in recent years came from acquisitions versus building the business on its own?
2. Were major acquisitions paid for in cash, in shares, or in new debt? What was the full cost including dilution?
3. Did the company fund its acquisitions out of Free Cash Flow, or did it borrow or issue shares to pay for them?
4. How much goodwill has built up from past deals, and has any of it been written down later?
5. Did ROIC rise, hold, or fall in the years after major acquisitions? What does that say about the prices paid?
6. Is the company a serial acquirer whose strategy depends on constant dealmaking, or an occasional buyer?
7. How much of reported operating cash flow comes from acquired businesses rather than organic growth?
8. Was a positive figure on this line a deliberate sale of a weak division, or the loss of a valuable business?
9. Did a wave of acquisitions coincide with a jump in debt or a fall in the cash balance?
10. What did management say each major acquisition would achieve, and did the bought business deliver on that promise?
