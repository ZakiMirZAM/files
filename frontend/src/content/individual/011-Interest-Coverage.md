# Interest Coverage

## Part 1: Definition

The interest coverage ratio measures how many times a company could pay one year of interest costs out of one year of operating income. Interest is the fee a company pays on borrowed money such as bank loans and bonds. Operating income is the profit the business earns from normal operations before interest and taxes. The ratio answers one question: does the business earn enough to carry the cost of the debt?

A ratio of 8 means the company earns eight dollars of operating income for every dollar of interest owed. A ratio of 1.5 means lenders already claim most of the operating income before shareholders see anything. The ratio is also called times interest earned because the ratio counts how many times earnings could cover the interest bill.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | Operating income cannot pay the year's interest. The company must spend savings, sell assets, or borrow more to pay lenders. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | The company can pay the interest but with little room for error. One weak year could create serious trouble. |
| 2.0 to 3.0 | Barely acceptable. Tolerable only when revenue is steady and predictable. |
| 3.0 to 5.0 | Adequate for most established companies. |
| 5.0 and above | Comfortable. This app's quality score awards a pass at 5.00 or higher. |
| 20 and above | Debt is so small that the ratio stops carrying information. The useful question becomes why the company borrows so little. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Interest Coverage = Operating Income ÷ Interest Expense`

Operating income is the top number. Analysts often call operating income EBIT, short for earnings before interest and taxes. The test asks whether the business itself earns enough to pay the interest, so the profit used must be measured before interest is taken out. A profit number that already subtracts interest would make the test circular.

Interest expense is the bottom number. Interest expense is the year's total cost of carrying all borrowed money, including bonds, bank loans, and the interest portion of leases. Principal is not included. Principal is the borrowed amount itself rather than the fee for borrowing. Principal is left out because an old loan can usually be replaced with a new loan when repayment comes due. Interest offers no such escape and must be paid from earnings every year.

Two stricter versions of the ratio exist:

| Version | Formula | What the version changes |
|---|---|---|
| EBITDA coverage | EBITDA ÷ Interest Expense | EBITDA is operating income with depreciation and amortization added back. Those are non-cash charges that spread the cost of assets over many years. This version produces the highest reading. |
| Capex-adjusted coverage | (EBITDA − Capex) ÷ Interest Expense | Capex means capital expenditures, the money spent on equipment and buildings. Subtracting capex first gives the strictest reading because the company is charged for the spending needed to keep the business running. |

This app's Fundamental Trends section displays the capex-adjusted version beside the headline ratio.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The amount of debt a company carries means little on its own. A debt that is safe for one company can sink another. The difference is whether earnings can carry the interest cost, and the interest coverage ratio states that difference as one number. A common rule of thumb among long-term investors is to look for a stable ratio of at least 5.

Lenders rely on the ratio to judge the risk that a company fails to pay. A higher ratio earns the company a better credit rating. A credit rating is a grade for how likely a borrower is to repay, and a better grade lowers the interest rate on the next loan. This app follows the same logic and uses the ratio when estimating the company's borrowing cost.

Shareholders care because interest is paid before any profit reaches shareholders. Dividends, buybacks, and reinvestment all come out of what remains after lenders are paid. The ratio shows how much of the operating result survives that first claim.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three forces move the ratio. The first is operating income, which rises and falls with the business cycle, with prices, and with costs. A company whose earnings swing widely will show a ratio that swings just as widely. The second is the amount of debt. Borrowing to fund acquisitions, share buybacks, or dividends raises the interest bill and lowers the ratio. The third is interest rates. Debt with a floating rate becomes more expensive as soon as market rates rise. Debt with a fixed rate becomes more expensive only at refinancing, which means replacing an old loan with a new loan at current rates. A ratio that looks stable may rest on old cheap loans that disappear at the next refinancing.

Accounting choices can distort single readings. One-time gains or losses inside operating income move the top number for reasons unrelated to the ongoing business. Capitalized interest hides part of the true cost, because capitalized interest is recorded as part of an asset's cost instead of as an expense. A very high ratio usually just means the company carries almost no debt, and at that point the ratio stops being informative.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The acceptable level depends on how steady the company's revenue is. Steadier revenue makes a thinner cushion safe.

| Industry type | Typical comfortable range | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | 2 to 4 | Revenue is near certain because regulators approve prices. A thin cushion is safe on purpose. |
| Established industrial companies | 5 to 10 | Earnings are solid but move with the economy, so a larger cushion is required. |
| Software and other low-debt companies | Very high or undefined | Many carry no meaningful debt, so the ratio says little about them. |
| Heavily indebted companies, such as those owned by buyout firms | 2 to 3 | Lenders accept the thin cushion in exchange for higher interest rates and strict loan conditions. |
| Cyclical companies such as miners and auto makers | Judge at the worst year | Earnings can fall by half or more in a downturn. The ratio in the best year proves nothing. |
| Banks | Not applicable | Interest is the core cost of the banking business rather than a financing cost. |

Always compare a company against others in the same sector, and weight the worst year on the chart more heavily than the latest year.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A flat high line means debt never constrains the business. A flat low line means management deliberately operates close to the limit lenders allow. The pattern that deserves the most attention is steady decay. A ratio that steps down year after year means debt is growing faster than earnings, or new loans carry higher rates than the old ones. A multi-year slide rarely reverses without a deliberate decision to pay down debt.

A collapse in a single year is usually an earnings problem rather than a debt problem, and earnings problems often recover with the business cycle. The collapse still matters because the collapse reveals the worst-case level that the company's debt must be sized against. Compare the headline line with the capex-adjusted version in the Fundamental Trends section. A headline ratio that holds steady while the capex-adjusted version sinks means equipment spending is quietly consuming the cushion.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Debt Service Coverage

**Why read the two together.** The interest coverage ratio tests only the interest fee. Debt service coverage adds the principal payments that come due in the same year. A company can look comfortable on interest alone while large repayments are scheduled in the near future.

**What the combination tells you.** A high interest coverage ratio combined with a low debt service coverage ratio means the repayment schedule is the real risk rather than the interest cost. Such a company depends on refinancing to survive, and refinancing can become expensive or unavailable in a credit crunch. When both ratios are high, the debt is safe on both the fee and the repayment side. When both are low, the company has a genuine solvency problem.

### (EBITDA − Capex) ÷ Interest

**Why read the two together.** The headline ratio credits the company with earnings before any equipment spending. Most businesses must keep spending on equipment and facilities just to maintain current earnings. Charging the company for that routine spending before measuring coverage gives a more honest cushion.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide gap between the headline ratio and the capex-adjusted ratio means the business consumes heavy spending just to stand still, so the true cushion is the smaller number. A narrow gap means the business is capital light and the headline ratio can be trusted. Watching both lines over time shows whether rising equipment costs are eroding a cushion that the headline ratio still reports as healthy.

### Net Debt ÷ EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** The interest coverage ratio measures whether the company can afford the yearly fee on the debt. Net Debt ÷ EBITDA measures the total size of the debt in years of earnings. Affordability and size are different questions, and a complete view of debt risk requires both answers.

**What the combination tells you.** Low total debt with high coverage means conservative finances. High total debt with high coverage means a large load carried at cheap rates, which is exposed to refinancing risk when rates rise. Low total debt with weak coverage points to an earnings problem rather than a debt problem. Reading the pair separates the cost of the debt from the amount of the debt.

### Operating Leverage

**Why read the two together.** Operating leverage measures how sharply operating income moves when revenue moves. Operating income is the top number of the interest coverage ratio. A company with high operating leverage will see the coverage ratio swing violently with the business cycle even when the debt never changes.

**What the combination tells you.** High operating leverage combined with a modest coverage ratio is dangerous because a recession can push coverage below 1 quickly. Low operating leverage with the same coverage level is far safer because earnings fall gently in a downturn. Together the two metrics show how much of today's cushion would survive a bad year.

## Part 8: Limitations

Operating income is an accounting figure rather than cash, so the ratio can overstate the real ability to pay when a company books profit faster than the company collects cash. The ratio ignores principal completely, which is why the debt service view is a required companion. The ratio also reflects the rates on existing loans rather than the rates the company will face at the next refinancing, so at the end of a cheap-money period the ratio flatters almost everyone.

Several reporting choices reduce comparability. Capitalized interest understates the true cost. Lease accounting differs between companies. Some companies subtract the interest earned on cash from the interest paid, which shrinks the bottom number. For a company with almost no debt the ratio is enormous and meaningless. For a company with negative operating income the ratio cannot be computed at all, and that failure happens exactly when the answer matters most. The ratio says nothing about loan conditions, foreign currency debts, or when the debts come due. The ratio is the best first question about debt and a poor last one.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio produced by unusually strong earnings, unusually cheap old loans, or both? What does the ratio become when either returns to normal?
2. What was the ratio in the company's worst earnings year on the chart? Could today's interest bill have been paid from that year's operating income?
3. How much of the debt has a floating rate or comes due within three years? What would the ratio be if all the debt repriced to current market rates?
4. Does the capex-adjusted version in the Fundamental Trends section tell the same story as the headline ratio? Or is equipment spending consuming the apparent cushion?
5. Is any interest being capitalized into assets instead of being expensed? What is the true ratio with the full interest cost included?
6. Does the company's borrowing cost look consistent with the coverage level, or are lenders charging far more or far less than the ratio would suggest?
7. If the ratio is extremely high, why does the company borrow so little? Is the caution a strategy, a reaction to past trouble, or a sign of shrinking investment opportunities?
8. What loan conditions reference coverage or debt levels? How large an earnings decline would break them?
9. How does the ratio compare across direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle? Which competitor would be forced to cut back first in a long downturn?
10. Have recent buybacks, dividends, or acquisitions been funded with new debt? Is management spending the cushion at the moment in the cycle when the cushion matters most?
