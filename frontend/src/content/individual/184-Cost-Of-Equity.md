# Cost of Equity

## Part 1: Definition

The cost of equity measures the annual return that shareholders require for owning a company's stock. It is often written as Ke. Shareholders take more risk than lenders, because they are paid last and their returns are not promised, so they demand a higher return in exchange. The cost of equity puts a number on that demand. It is the rate of return a shareholder could reasonably expect somewhere else for the same level of risk, which is why it is also called the required return on equity.

The cost of equity is not a cash payment the company writes out, the way it pays interest on debt. It is an expectation, the return shareholders need to see for holding the stock to be worthwhile. That makes it the equity hurdle. If the company earns more than its cost of equity on the shareholders' money, it is rewarding them for the risk. If it earns less, shareholders would have been better off elsewhere, even when the company reports a profit.

| Cost of equity level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Low (high single digits) | A stable, low-risk business with a beta near or below one, or a low interest-rate environment. |
| Moderate (around ten percent) | A typical company with average market sensitivity. |
| High (well into double digits) | A volatile, high-beta business, or a high interest-rate or high-risk-premium environment. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

This app uses the capital asset pricing model, known as CAPM. The formula is:

`Ke = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium`

The risk-free rate is the return on a safe government bond, the reward for simply waiting with no risk of default. Beta measures how much the stock moves relative to the overall market: a beta of one moves with the market, above one swings more, and below one swings less. The equity risk premium is the extra return investors demand for holding stocks rather than the safe bond.

The model starts from the risk-free rate and adds a risk reward sized by beta. A stock that swings twice as hard as the market (beta of two) earns twice the market's risk premium on top of the risk-free rate. A stock that barely moves (low beta) earns only a small premium. If the risk-free rate is 4 percent, beta is 1.2, and the equity risk premium is 5 percent, the cost of equity is 4 + 1.2 × 5, which is 10 percent. The model ties the required return directly to how risky the stock has been relative to the market.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The cost of equity is the benchmark for the shareholders' return. A company's return on equity tells you what it earned on the owners' money, but that number means little until you compare it with what the owners required. A return on equity of 9 percent looks fine until you learn the cost of equity is 11 percent, at which point the company is falling short of what shareholders need. The cost of equity is the line return on equity must beat.

It is also the largest piece of the weighted average cost of capital, and it is the right hurdle on its own for businesses where the blended cost of capital does not apply. Banks and insurers fund themselves with deposits and policies as part of their normal business, so debt is not a simple financing choice and WACC is ill-defined. For these companies, the cost of equity is the correct hurdle, and return on equity minus the cost of equity is the proper value-creation test.

The cost of equity also feeds valuation directly. It is the discount rate for cash flows that belong to shareholders, and it sits inside the justified price-to-book and justified price-to-earnings calculations. Wherever the question is what shareholders should require, the cost of equity supplies the answer.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three inputs drive the CAPM cost of equity. The risk-free rate sets the floor, so when government bond yields rise, every company's cost of equity rises with them. This is why costs of equity climb across the board when interest rates increase, regardless of any change in the businesses themselves.

Beta scales the risk reward. A higher beta, meaning a stock that swings harder than the market, raises the cost of equity, because shareholders demand more for the wider ride. A company in a volatile, cyclical industry will carry a higher beta and a higher cost of equity than a steady one. The equity risk premium is the third input, the general appetite for stock risk across the whole market. When investors grow nervous and demand more to hold stocks, the premium widens and lifts every cost of equity. Because all three inputs are market-driven, the cost of equity can move even when the company stands still.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The cost of equity varies by industry mainly through beta, because some industries swing far more with the economy than others. Stable, defensive businesses carry low betas and low costs of equity, while cyclical or growth-driven ones carry high betas and high costs of equity.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and consumer staples | Low cost of equity | Steady demand gives a low beta, so the risk reward added is small. |
| Healthcare and large pharma | Low to moderate | Defensive demand keeps beta and the required return contained. |
| Industrial and manufacturing | Moderate | Cyclical earnings raise beta and the required return. |
| Technology and growth | High | High betas and uncertain earnings push the required return up. |
| Banks and insurers | Used as the main hurdle | WACC is ill-defined for lenders, so the cost of equity is the benchmark of choice. |

Compare the cost of equity within an industry, and remember that most of the difference between two companies comes from their betas and from the interest-rate environment they share.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A rising cost of equity over time usually traces back to one of three things: higher interest rates lifting the risk-free rate, a rising beta as the stock becomes more volatile, or a widening equity risk premium as the market turns cautious. A falling cost of equity usually means the reverse. Because two of the three inputs are shared by every company, much of the movement in any one firm's cost of equity reflects the broader market rather than the firm alone.

The part worth isolating is beta, since it is the company-specific input. A cost of equity that climbed mainly because the firm's beta rose tells a different story from one that climbed because rates rose. Read the cost of equity next to return on equity over the same years. A cost of equity that stays below return on equity means shareholders are being rewarded. A cost of equity that rises past return on equity is a warning that the company has stopped clearing the equity hurdle. Watch for years where the change came almost entirely from the assumed risk-free rate or risk premium rather than from the business.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Beta

**Why read the two together.** Beta is the input that makes the cost of equity company-specific. The risk-free rate and the equity risk premium are shared across all firms, so beta is the part that reflects this stock's own risk. Reading beta explains why one company's cost of equity differs from another's.

**What the combination tells you.** A high cost of equity driven by a high beta says the market sees this stock as volatile and demands more for holding it. If the cost of equity rose mainly because beta rose, the change is about the company. If beta is steady but the cost of equity still climbed, the cause is the wider market through rates or the risk premium. Reading both separates company risk from market conditions.

### Excess Return (ROE − Ke)

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity is the hurdle, and excess return is return on equity minus that hurdle. The cost of equity gives the excess return its meaning, because without a hurdle there is nothing for return on equity to clear.

**What the combination tells you.** When return on equity sits above the cost of equity, the company creates value for shareholders, and the gap is the excess return. When return on equity falls below the cost of equity, the excess return turns negative and value is being destroyed. Reading the two together converts a raw return on equity into a clear verdict on whether shareholders are being properly rewarded.

### Justified P/B

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity is the discount rate inside the justified price-to-book formula. A higher cost of equity lowers the price-to-book the fundamentals can support, because future equity returns are worth less when shareholders demand more. The cost of equity is one of the levers that sets the fair multiple.

**What the combination tells you.** If the justified price-to-book is low, a high cost of equity may be the reason, telling you the market's risk demand is holding the fair multiple down. Comparing the cost of equity with return on equity inside the justified multiple shows whether the company deserves to trade above or below its book value. A company earning more than its cost of equity justifies a price-to-book above one.

## Part 8: Limitations

The cost of equity is an estimate built from a model, and each input is debatable. The equity risk premium is not observed directly and reasonable analysts choose different values, which alone can move the cost of equity by a point or more. Beta is usually measured from past price movements, which may not describe future risk, and a short or noisy measurement window can produce a misleading beta. The risk-free rate choice, short bond versus long bond, also changes the answer.

CAPM itself is a simplification. It assumes risk that matters is only the part that moves with the market, and it ignores company-specific factors that real investors may price. Because the cost of equity feeds valuation and the value-creation test, any error in it carries straight through. The figure can also shift from quarter to quarter as rates and the risk premium move, even when nothing in the business has changed. For all these reasons, the cost of equity is best read as a sensible range rather than a single precise number.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current cost of equity high because of the company's beta, or because of the shared inputs of the risk-free rate and the equity risk premium?
2. How does the cost of equity compare with the company's return on equity, and is the excess return positive or negative?
3. Has a recent rise in the cost of equity come from the business through a higher beta, or from the wider market through rates?
4. What beta is feeding the model, and does it reflect the company's true risk or just a noisy measurement window?
5. What equity risk premium is assumed, and how much would the cost of equity change if a different premium were used?
6. For a bank or insurer, is the cost of equity being used as the main hurdle in place of WACC?
7. Would a one or two point rise in the cost of equity push the company below its equity hurdle?
8. Is the cost of equity consistent across the years shown, or do changing assumptions make the trend hard to read?
9. Does the cost of equity support the company's actual price-to-book, or does the justified multiple sit far from the market's?
10. Is the company's return on equity comfortably above the cost of equity in bad years as well as good ones?
