# Price To Sales

## Part 1: Definition

The price to sales ratio measures how much the stock market pays for one dollar of the company's yearly revenue. Revenue is the total amount customers were billed for goods and services during the year, before any cost is subtracted. The ratio answers one question: how expensive is the company relative to the size of its business?

A ratio of 2 means investors pay two dollars of market value for every one dollar of annual revenue. The ratio is available even when the company loses money, because revenue is almost always positive. That availability is the main reason the ratio exists, since the price to earnings ratio cannot be computed for a company with negative earnings.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.5 | The market pays less than fifty cents per dollar of revenue. Thin profit margins or a shrinking business are usually the reason. |
| 0.5 to 1.5 | Typical for retailers, distributors, and other high volume businesses that earn a small profit on each sale. |
| 1.5 to 3.0 | Around the middle of the market. A company here usually converts a normal share of revenue into profit. |
| 3.0 to 8.0 | The market expects high profit margins, fast growth, or both. |
| Above 8.0 | The price rests almost entirely on future margins that the company does not yet earn. |

A low ratio is not automatically cheap. A company that keeps two cents of profit per dollar of revenue deserves a lower ratio than a company that keeps twenty cents.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Price To Sales = Market Capitalization ÷ Revenue`

Market capitalization is the top number. Market capitalization is the share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding, so the figure is the total price the market puts on the whole equity of the company. Equity means the owners' claim on the business after lenders are paid.

Revenue is the bottom number. Revenue is the year's total sales recognized under accounting rules. Revenue belongs in the bottom of the ratio because revenue is the least manipulated line on the income statement. Depreciation choices, inventory choices, and write-offs all change reported profit without changing revenue.

The same ratio can be written per share. Dividing both parts by the share count gives share price divided by revenue per share, which produces an identical number.

Two versions of the revenue multiple exist:

| Version | Formula | What the version changes |
|---|---|---|
| Price to sales | Market Capitalization ÷ Revenue | Compares an equity value against revenue that belongs to the whole firm. Debt is ignored, so a heavily indebted company shows a lower ratio without being cheaper. |
| Enterprise value to sales | Enterprise Value ÷ Revenue | Enterprise value adds debt and subtracts cash, so both sides of the ratio refer to the whole business. The internally consistent version. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Revenue survives when profit does not. A young company, a company in a bad year, and a company recovering from a loss all still report revenue. The price to sales ratio therefore keeps working across the full range of company conditions, which prevents a comparison group from quietly losing every unprofitable member.

Revenue is also the most stable line on the income statement. Profit falls faster than revenue in a downturn because fixed costs do not shrink with sales. The price to earnings ratio of a cyclical company swings far more than the price to sales ratio of the same company, so the revenue multiple gives a steadier reference point across a full business cycle.

The third reason is resistance to accounting choice. Accounting rules for recognizing revenue are broadly similar across companies and countries. Reported profit can be moved by depreciation schedules and by one-time charges. Revenue moves far less.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Profit margin is the strongest driver. A company that turns more of each revenue dollar into profit deserves and receives a higher ratio. Expected growth is the second driver, because faster future revenue raises the value placed on today's revenue base. Risk is the third driver, since a riskier stream of future profit is discounted more heavily and earns a lower ratio.

Leverage moves the ratio without changing the business. Leverage means the use of borrowed money. A company funded partly by debt has a smaller equity value for the same revenue, so the ratio falls. Two identical businesses with different debt levels will show different price to sales ratios, and the more indebted one will look cheaper.

Revenue recognition choices move the bottom number. Reporting the full value of a transaction rather than only the commission earned inflates revenue and depresses the ratio. Acquisitions add revenue immediately, so a company that buys growth can hold a low ratio while the underlying business stands still.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level of the ratio tracks the normal profit margin of the industry.

| Industry type | Typical range | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Grocers, distributors, and airlines | 0.1 to 0.6 | Net margins are often below three percent. Large revenue produces small profit. |
| General retail and manufacturing | 0.5 to 2.0 | Margins sit in the middle single digits to low teens. |
| Consumer brands and industrials with pricing power | 2.0 to 4.0 | Higher margins justify paying more per dollar of revenue. |
| Software and other capital light businesses | 4.0 to 15.0 | Each extra sale costs very little to deliver, so margins are high and scalable. |
| Pharmaceuticals and medical devices | 3.0 to 8.0 | Patent protection supports durable high margins. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Revenue for a lender mixes interest income and fees. The line is not comparable to product sales. |
| Real estate investment trusts | Not meaningful | Rental revenue is judged against funds from operations rather than against price. |

The rule for cross-sector comparison is simple. Comparing price to sales ratios across two industries is meaningless unless the profit margins are similar. Always read the ratio beside the margin, and prefer comparisons inside one industry.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A rising ratio over several years means the market is willing to pay more for the same revenue. The rise is justified when margins are also expanding on the chart, and the rise is a warning when margins are flat or falling. A falling ratio with stable margins usually signals a change in expected growth or a change in perceived risk rather than a change in the current business.

A step change in one year usually has a specific cause. A large acquisition adds revenue at once and drops the ratio without any improvement in value. A divestiture removes revenue and lifts the ratio. High volatility in the line normally comes from the share price rather than from revenue, because revenue moves slowly.

Two base effects can fake a trend. A recession year with depressed revenue leaves the ratio looking high even after the share price has fallen. A change in revenue recognition policy can shift the bottom number permanently, which creates a break in the line that has nothing to do with valuation.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Margin

**Why read the two together.** Net margin is net income divided by revenue, so net margin states how many cents of profit the company keeps from each dollar of sales. The price to sales ratio states what the market pays for that dollar of sales. Neither number can be judged without the other, because the fair multiple of revenue rises directly with the margin.

**What the combination tells you.** A low ratio combined with a healthy and stable margin is the setup that genuinely suggests an undervalued stock. A low ratio combined with a thin or falling margin usually means the market is correctly paying little for revenue that produces little profit. A high ratio combined with a high margin is a business the market already understands, so the question becomes whether the margin can last.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT means earnings before interest and taxes, so the EBIT margin measures profitability from operations before financing and tax effects. Net margin can be distorted by debt costs and by one-off tax items. Reading the price to sales ratio against the EBIT margin isolates the operating economics that support the multiple.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose EBIT margin is strong while its net margin is weak is carrying a heavy interest or tax burden, and that burden is the reason its revenue multiple is low. A company whose EBIT margin is rising while the price to sales ratio is flat is becoming cheaper on the measure that matters most. When both the margin and the ratio fall together, the market is repricing the operating business rather than the capital structure.

### P/E

**Why read the two together.** The price to earnings ratio prices the company against profit, and the price to sales ratio prices the same company against revenue. The gap between the two conclusions is entirely explained by the profit margin. Reading both shows whether a stock looks expensive because of its price or because of its current profitability.

**What the combination tells you.** A high price to earnings ratio beside a low price to sales ratio points to temporarily depressed margins, which is common at the bottom of a cycle. A low price to earnings ratio beside a high price to sales ratio points to unusually high margins that may not last. When the two ratios agree, the valuation signal is much more reliable.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity measures how much borrowed money the company uses against the owners' capital. The price to sales ratio uses only the equity value on top while using whole firm revenue on the bottom. Debt therefore pushes the ratio down mechanically, with no improvement in the underlying business.

**What the combination tells you.** A low price to sales ratio paired with high debt to equity is often a mirage, because the equity is small simply because lenders own more of the business. Comparing two companies in the same industry requires checking that the debt levels are similar before treating the lower ratio as cheaper. When debt to equity is near zero, the price to sales ratio can be read closer to face value.

## Part 8: Limitations

The deepest limitation is that revenue is not value. A company can grow revenue quickly while losing money on every sale, and the ratio will still produce a comfortable looking number. Value ultimately comes from profit and cash, so a revenue multiple without a margin check invites paying a high price for growth that never converts. The ratio also mismatches the two sides of the fraction, since equity value sits on top while revenue generated by the whole firm sits on the bottom.

Several practical problems reduce comparability. Revenue recognition policy differs between companies that report gross amounts and companies that report net commissions. Acquisitions inflate revenue without proving that the purchase created value. The ratio says nothing about risk, so a company with a high margin and a low ratio may simply be a fragile business. For banks and insurers the revenue line is not comparable to product sales at all. The ratio should not be used there.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is the company's net margin today, and is the current price to sales ratio consistent with that margin compared with direct competitors?
2. Is the margin that supports today's ratio at a cyclical high, a cyclical low, or a normal level for this business?
3. How much of the revenue growth over the years on the chart came from acquisitions rather than from selling more to existing and new customers?
4. Does the company report revenue on a gross basis or a net basis, and would the competitor comparison change under the other basis?
5. If the company is unprofitable, what operating margin must the company reach for today's ratio to be justified, and what must change in the cost base to get there?
6. How much debt does the company carry, and how would the ratio look if the debt were added to the market value on top?
7. Which revenue lines are recurring and contracted, and which depend on winning new orders every year?
8. Has the ratio moved because revenue changed or because the share price changed? Which of the two moved first?
9. What pricing power does the company have, and can prices be raised without losing volume if costs rise?
10. What would the ratio be if revenue fell to the level of the weakest year shown on the chart?
