# Investment Income Removed

## Part 1: Definition

Investment Income Removed is the part of a non-financial company's net income that comes from investments and interest rather than the core operating business. Net income is the final profit a company reports after all costs, interest, and taxes. Investment income is money the company earns from holding cash and securities, meaning financial investments such as bonds and shares of other companies. Investment income arrives as interest, dividends, and gains when securities are sold.

The core operating business is the set of products or services the company sells to customers. The number shown here is the after-tax investment income that is stripped out of reported profit. The figure left after the removal is called core net income. The metric answers one question: how much of the reported profit was produced by the operating business itself?

Common sizes of the removed amount:

| Size of the removed amount | What that size usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The operating business produced almost all of the reported profit. Investment income is not distorting the picture. |
| A small share of net income | A minor part of the profit came from investments. The operating result is still the main story. |
| A large share of net income | Much of the reported profit came from investments rather than operations. The operating business may be weaker than the headline profit suggests. |
| Larger than operating profit | The operating business is weak. Investment returns are carrying the reported result. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Core Net Income = Reported Net Income − After-Tax Investment Income`

Reported net income is the starting number. Reported net income is the bottom-line profit shown on the income statement, after every cost including interest and tax. The figure includes both operating profit and any income earned from investments.

After-tax investment income is the amount removed. After-tax investment income is the interest, dividends, and securities gains the company earned. The figure is measured after the tax charged on that income. The after-tax basis matches net income, which is also an after-tax figure. Removing a pre-tax figure from an after-tax figure would overstate the adjustment.

The number labeled Investment Income Removed is the after-tax investment income term in the formula. Subtracting the term leaves core net income, the profit from operations alone.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

An investor removes investment income to price the operating business on its own terms. The value of a company's operations depends on the profit those operations produce, not on returns from a pile of cash or securities. Mixing the two hides how the real business is performing.

Investment income can flatter a weak operating business. A company with a large cash balance can post rising profit while its products sell poorly, because interest and market gains lift the bottom line. Removing that income exposes the true operating result.

The securities that produce the income are not ignored in a valuation. The cash and marketable securities are valued separately at their market value and added back at the end of the valuation. Counting both the income stream and the securities themselves would value the same asset twice.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The size of the company's cash and securities holdings sets the base. A company sitting on a large cash balance and a big portfolio of bonds will earn more investment income than a company that holds little. The more the company holds, the larger the amount removed.

Interest rates and market returns move the income year to year. Higher interest rates raise the interest earned on cash and bonds. A strong year in the stock market raises the dividends and gains on holdings of shares.

One-off events distort a single year. Selling a block of securities at a profit creates a large gain that inflates investment income for that year alone. How the company reports stakes in other firms also matters. A large controlling stake is folded into the operating results. A small stake shows up as investment income.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Some industries carry far more investment income than others. The distortion is largest where a company hoards cash or holds shares of other firms.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cash-rich technology firms | Sizable removed amount | Large cash and securities balances earn meaningful interest and gains. |
| Holding companies | Sizable removed amount | The business is built around stakes in other firms, so investment income is a big part of reported profit. |
| Established industrials and retailers | Small removed amount | Most profit comes from selling goods, and cash balances are modest. |
| Capital-light service firms | Small or near zero | Little cash is tied up, so investment income stays minor. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | Investing is the core business, so interest and investment returns are the real operating income and must not be removed. |

A cross-sector comparison of the removed amount is meaningless once a financial company is in the mix, because the same income that is a sideline for an industrial company is the whole business for a bank.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A rising removed amount over several years means the company depends more and more on investment returns to produce its profit. Growth in the cash pile or the securities portfolio usually drives the rise. The trend is worth watching because operating profit and investment profit deserve different treatment in a valuation.

A single-year spike usually reflects a one-off securities sale rather than a lasting change. The spike fades the next year when the gain is not repeated. A line that jumps around from year to year points to trading gains and market swings, not to a steady income stream. A base effect can fake a trend, because a year of weak operating profit makes the investment share look large even when the investment income itself did not grow.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Income

**Why read the two together.** Operating income is the profit from the core business before interest and tax. Investment Income Removed strips the non-operating income out of the bottom line. Reading operating income next to the removed amount shows how much of the reported profit the operations actually earned.

**What the combination tells you.** A large removed amount sitting next to a small operating income means the reported profit leans heavily on investments rather than the business. A small removed amount next to a healthy operating income means the operations are carrying their own weight. Watching both over time reveals whether the company is drifting from an operating business toward an investment vehicle. The pairing is the clearest test of where the profit truly comes from.

### NOPAT

**Why read the two together.** NOPAT is net operating profit after tax, the after-tax profit the operating assets produce with interest and investment income already excluded. Investment Income Removed performs a similar removal starting from reported net income. Reading them together confirms that the non-operating income has been taken out consistently. Both figures aim at the same target from different starting points.

**What the combination tells you.** When NOPAT and core net income tell the same story, the operating profit picture is reliable. A gap between them points to items that one measure treats as operating and the other does not. The pairing helps a reader trust that the valuation is pricing operations rather than a cash pile. The comparison also flags companies where the operating and non-operating split is unusually blurry.

### Cash Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Cash Per Share is the company's cash and marketable securities divided by the number of shares. The cash and securities on the balance sheet are what produce the investment income. Reading the size of that holding next to the income it earns links the two halves of the same story.

**What the combination tells you.** A large cash balance paired with a large removed amount confirms that the investment income comes from a real and sizable holding. The holding is valued separately and added to the operating value at the end of a valuation. A large removed amount with only a small cash balance is a warning that the income may be volatile trading gains rather than steady interest. The pairing tells a reader whether the investment income rests on a durable asset base.

## Part 8: Limitations

The line between operating and investment income is a judgment, not a clean fact. Some income sits in a gray zone, such as the return on cash held for day-to-day operations. Different analysts draw the line in different places, so the removed amount is not perfectly comparable across companies. The tax rate applied to the investment income is usually an estimate rather than a figure the company discloses.

The adjustment can mislead when investing is a genuine secondary business. A company that runs a real financing arm alongside its main operations earns investment income that is part of how it makes money. Removing that income understates the company's true earning power. For banks and insurers the removal should not be applied at all, because investing is the whole business.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How large is the removed amount relative to reported net income? What does the operating result look like once the investment income is taken out?
2. What is driving the investment income: interest on cash, dividends from stakes in other firms, or gains on selling securities?
3. Did a one-time securities sale inflate the investment income this year? What would the figure be in a normal year?
4. How big is the company's cash and securities balance, and is the investment income consistent with the size of that balance?
5. Has the removed amount grown faster than operating income over the past several years? Is the company drifting toward relying on investment returns?
6. Are any stakes in other companies folded into operating income, and should the related income be reclassified as investment income?
7. Is the tax rate applied to the investment income reasonable, or does it distort the size of the removed amount?
8. Does the company run a financing or investment arm that is a real part of the business rather than a sideline?
9. After the removal, does the core operating profit still support the company's valuation on its own?
