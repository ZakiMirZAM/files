# Growth Viability

## What it is

Growth viability asks one question. Is the growth rate a valuation assumes a growth rate the business can actually deliver? A valuation rests on a forecast of future cash flow, and the growth rate is the input that drives the forecast. Aswath Damodaran treats growth as the most important input in a valuation, and the input most often accepted on faith. The section named "Is This Growth Real?" refuses to accept the growth rate on faith.

A growth rate can come from three places. The first is the company's own past record, meaning the rate at which revenue and earnings actually grew. The second is a forecast published by the analysts who follow the company. The third is the company's fundamentals, meaning how much profit the company reinvests in the business and what return the reinvestment earns. Only the third source has a mechanical link to the future. A past growth rate is a fact about years that are already over, and an analyst forecast is an opinion.

The section is a standing deck of 17 questions grouped into five tiers. Every question always appears, whatever the company. The company's own reported figures decide which questions carry a warning flag, so the flags point the reader at the checks that this company's numbers have tripped.

## The five questions this section asks

| Tier | What the tier tests | Why a growth rate fails the test |
|---|---|---|
| Has the past growth been sustainable | Whether earnings have outrun revenue, whether the record is a two-year streak or a ten-year record, and whether extending the growth rate would require an impossible share of the market within ten to fifteen years. The tier also checks whether revenue growth ever reached the operating income line | Earnings grew faster than sales for years, or the record is too short to be a record, or the rate compounds into a market share no company could hold |
| Will reinvestment and research and development sustain it | Whether the return on invested capital exceeds the cost of capital, and whether the company reinvests enough to fund the growth the model assumes. The tier also asks whether research spending is rising or being cut to flatter near-term earnings, and whether capital spending expands capacity or only maintains aging assets | Growth is assumed without the reinvestment that pays for it, or the reinvestment earns less than the cost of capital and adds no value |
| What could lower the future growth rate | How fast the return on invested capital is fading, and how much of the growth is a cyclical or commodity swing rather than a structural trend. The tier also tests whether the growth leans on a small number of products or customers, or on patents and licences that expire | Returns on capital are fading quickly, or the growth is a swing in the cycle, or the growth depends on something with an expiry date |
| Is the growth genuine, or a one-off | How much profit came from one-time or non-operating items, and whether charges called one-time recur every year under a new name. The tier also tests whether the growth is backed by cash collected rather than by rising receivables and accruals | Reported growth rests on gains the business cannot repeat, or on revenue booked before the cash ever arrives |
| Sentiment versus fundamentals, and extra scrutiny for small companies | The gap between the growth the current share price already assumes and the growth the fundamentals can fund. The tier applies extra scrutiny when the company is small or young or not yet profitable, and asks whether the growth story is merely possible or actually probable | The price assumes growth the business cannot fund, or the story is possible without ever being probable |

Several terms in the table need plain definitions. Reinvestment is the share of profit put back into the business as new plant, working capital, or acquisitions. Return on invested capital is the profit a company earns as a percent of the money invested in the business. Cost of capital is the return the company's lenders and shareholders require. Receivables are amounts owed by customers who have bought but not yet paid. Accruals are profit recorded in the accounts before the cash has been collected. A one-time item is a gain or a charge the company presents as unlikely to appear again.

## How this section works

The section does not hand you a verdict. You answer the questions in your own words first, before you read any explanation. Writing your own answer forces you to decide whether the growth has a source that lasts. The act of writing reveals what you truly understand and where you are only assuming.

Your answer saves automatically as you type. After you have written it, the "Explain using AI" button on each question acts as a tutor. The tutor helps only after you have tried. Reaching for the tutor first would remove the thinking that makes the lesson stick.

The questions are deliberately answerable rather than automatic pass or fail verdicts. A machine can spot that earnings outran revenue. Only a person can say whether the reason is durable.

## Why it matters for valuation

A discounted cash flow value is more sensitive to the growth rate than to almost any other input. A small change in assumed growth compounds over the forecast years and changes the answer substantially. Any growth rate accepted without testing therefore passes straight into the intrinsic value the model reports.

Damodaran ties growth to fundamentals rather than to history. Expected growth in operating income equals the reinvestment rate multiplied by the return on invested capital. Growth that is not funded by reinvestment does not exist in that equation. A model that assumes high growth with low reinvestment is assuming money the company never spent produced profit the company never earned.

Growth rates also fade. Penman shows that profitability and growth revert toward normal levels over time, because high returns attract competitors who compete the returns away. Pat Dorsey makes the same point through moats. A return on capital above the cost of capital decays back toward the cost of capital unless a durable competitive advantage protects it. A valuation that holds a high growth rate flat forever is quietly assuming a moat that nobody has named.

Benjamin Graham asked for a long record before trusting any growth figure, over roughly ten years rather than the last two. Schilit and Perler explain the other half of the problem. Reported growth can be manufactured by one-time gains, by charges called non-recurring that recur every year, and by revenue booked before the cash arrives. Growth that only exists in the accounts cannot fund anything.

## Common traps

The first trap is extrapolating a short streak. Two or three strong years is not a record, and the recent past is the weakest possible evidence for the distant future. A long record of steady growth is worth far more than a short record of spectacular growth.

The second trap is assuming growth without funding it. A model can raise the growth rate on the forecast tab without touching the reinvestment line. The resulting value is higher, and the higher value is fiction. Growth costs money, so the model must show where the money goes.

The third trap is missing that earnings have outrun revenue. Margin expansion is real but finite. Cost cuts end, and a company that has been growing earnings faster than sales for years is usually running out of costs to cut. Once margins stop rising, earnings growth falls back to revenue growth.

The fourth trap is accepting growth that shows up in receivables rather than cash. Sales recorded before customers pay produce reported growth with no cash behind it. Rising receivables and rising accruals alongside rising profit are the signal that the growth is an accounting event rather than a business event.

The fifth trap is cutting research and development to manufacture growth. Reducing research spending lifts reported profit immediately, and the lift looks like earnings growth. The cost arrives years later when the product pipeline is empty.

A final trap is ignoring the growth already priced into the stock. A company can be growing genuinely and still be a poor investment when the current price assumes even faster growth. The question is never only whether growth is real. The question is whether the real growth is larger than the growth the price already pays for.
