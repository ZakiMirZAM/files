# DuPont ROE Decomposition

## Part 1: Definition

The DuPont ROE decomposition takes return on equity and breaks it into the separate levers that produce it. Return on equity, written ROE, is net income divided by shareholder equity. Net income is the profit left after every cost, including interest and taxes. Shareholder equity is the owners' stake in the business, the assets left after subtracting what the company owes. ROE answers one question on its own: how much profit does the company earn for each dollar of owner money. The decomposition answers a second question that ROE hides: how does the company earn that return.

Two companies can post the same ROE for opposite reasons. One company earns it from fat profit margins and efficient use of assets. The other borrows heavily and amplifies a thin return. The headline number looks identical. The decomposition splits the single number into its parts so the source of the return becomes visible. The method was developed inside the DuPont corporation a century ago and remains the standard way to read ROE.

This app uses the five-factor version from the CFA curriculum. The five factors are Tax Burden and Interest Burden, then EBIT Margin and Asset Turnover, then Financial Leverage. Each factor is defined in Part 2b. The five multiply together to equal ROE.

| ROE driven mostly by | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| EBIT Margin | Pricing power or cost control. The return rests on operating quality and tends to be durable. |
| Asset Turnover | Efficient use of assets. Common in low-margin, high-volume businesses such as retail. |
| Financial Leverage | Borrowing. The return is amplified by debt and is more fragile in a downturn. |
| Tax Burden | A low tax rate. Often outside management control and prone to reversing. |
| Interest Burden | Low interest cost relative to operating profit. Falls when debt or rates rise. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The five factors multiply to ROE. The formula is:

`ROE = Tax Burden × Interest Burden × EBIT Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage`

The decomposition is built by inserting income statement and balance sheet lines into the ROE fraction so the fraction telescopes into five ratios. Written with the underlying terms:

`ROE = (NI ÷ EBT) × (EBT ÷ EBIT) × (EBIT ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Avg Assets) × (Avg Assets ÷ Avg Equity)`

NI is net income. EBT is earnings before tax. EBIT is earnings before interest and taxes, also called operating income. Avg Assets and Avg Equity are the average of the beginning and ending balance sheet values. Every numerator cancels against the next denominator. The product reduces to net income over average equity, which is ROE. The decomposition is an identity. The identity is true by arithmetic, not estimated.

Each factor isolates one lever of the business. The first three factors describe profitability. Tax Burden isolates the tax claim. Interest Burden isolates the financing cost. EBIT Margin isolates pure operating profitability before interest and tax. Asset Turnover isolates efficiency, the sales generated per dollar of assets. Financial Leverage isolates the balance sheet structure, the assets controlled per dollar of equity. Reading the five levers separately shows which one is doing the work.

The app computes each factor from the statements and uses EBIT in place of operating income. The balance sheet terms use averages, which smooths a mid-year buyback or acquisition. The section's ROE can differ slightly from the ratios panel's ROE because the panel uses period-end equity. A reconciliation line at the bottom of the section confirms the five factors multiply back to the average-equity ROE. The section also shows the net margin bridge. The first three factors multiplied together equal Net Margin, which collapses the five-factor form back into the classic three-factor form of margin times turnover times leverage.

## Part 2b: Every input, defined

The decomposition uses five levers, each a named metric defined in full elsewhere in the app. The summaries below say what each lever isolates and why the split matters.

Tax Burden is net income divided by pre-tax earnings. The lever measures the share of pre-tax profit that survives taxes. A value of 0.80 means the company keeps eighty cents of each pre-tax dollar. Tax Burden belongs in the chain because taxes are a real claim on the return. Isolating taxes stops a temporary tax break from being mistaken for an operating gain.

Interest Burden is pre-tax earnings divided by EBIT. The lever measures the share of operating profit that survives interest. A value of 0.95 means interest consumes five percent of operating profit. Interest Burden sits at or below 1.0 for any company that borrows. The lever is the counterweight to Financial Leverage at the end of the chain. Borrowing raises leverage and lowers Interest Burden at the same time. Whether debt helps ROE depends on which effect wins.

EBIT Margin is operating income divided by revenue. The lever is the operating heart of the chain, the share of each sales dollar kept after operating costs but before interest and tax. EBIT Margin is the lever most under management control. Pricing power and cost discipline move this lever. Its own definition in the ratios panel covers it in full.

Asset Turnover is revenue divided by average assets. The lever measures the sales generated per dollar of assets, the efficiency dimension of the business. Companies trade margin against turnover as a matter of business model. Its own definition in the ratios panel covers it in full.

Financial Leverage is average assets divided by average equity. The lever is also called the equity multiplier. A value of 2.0 means each dollar of equity controls two dollars of assets, so half the balance sheet is financed by someone other than shareholders. Financial Leverage is the amplifier at the end of the chain. The lever scales whatever the operating factors produce, in both directions. Net Margin, the product of the first three factors, is also a named metric in the app and equals net income divided by revenue.

The split matters because a high ROE earned through margin and turnover is not the same return as a high ROE borrowed through leverage. The first is operating quality and tends to repeat. The second is amplification and reverses when conditions turn. The single ROE number cannot tell the two apart. The five levers can.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

ROE on its own answers how much the company earns per dollar of equity while hiding how the company earns it. The how is what an investment decision needs. An ROE built from margin and turnover is operating excellence. The same ROE manufactured through borrowing is amplification that carries risk the single number never shows.

The decomposition turns the headline into a diagnosis. When ROE moves, the five-factor table shows which lever moved with it. A rise driven by EBIT Margin is a different fact than the same rise driven by Financial Leverage, and the two deserve different responses.

The decomposition also disciplines peer comparison. Two companies should be compared lever by lever, not on the product of different ingredients. The five tiles connect the app's profitability metrics and its leverage metrics into one multiplicative story. The chain links ROS and Asset Turnover to Interest Coverage and the debt ratios.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Each lever has its own driver, so the decomposition routes every ROE change to its true cause. Tax Burden moves with tax law and the mix of countries a company operates in. Credits and one-time settlements move it too. Most of that is outside management skill.

Interest Burden moves with debt levels and interest rates. The lever falls as borrowing rises or as old loans reprice to higher rates. EBIT Margin moves with pricing, input costs, and product mix. EBIT Margin is the genuine operating signal in the chain.

Asset Turnover moves with capital intensity and working capital discipline. Acquisitions and the investment cycle move it as well. Financial Leverage moves with debt policy and retained earnings growth. Writedowns and buybacks move it too. A buyback shrinks equity and raises the multiplier even when no new debt is added. The diagnostic rule is simple. When the ROE line moves, open the multi-year factor table and find which row moved with it.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Sectors sit at characteristic points in the five-lever space. Knowing the typical shape of each sector comes before judging the size of any one lever.

| Industry type | Typical lever shape | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and branded consumer | High margin, modest turnover, low leverage | The ROE is almost pure operating quality. |
| Grocery and discount retail | Thin margin, high turnover, moderate leverage | Volume and efficiency carry the return. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Decent margin, low turnover, heavy leverage | Regulated returns amplified by sanctioned debt. |
| Banks | Modest margin on assets, very high leverage | Interest is the core business and leverage is the raw material. Bank ROE uses its own framework. |
| Capital-heavy industrials | Moderate margin, low turnover, moderate leverage | Large asset bases hold turnover down. |

Comparing a retailer's leverage to a utility's, or a bank's multiplier to anyone's, is a category error. The honest comparison is lever by lever against direct peers. A company beating its industry on margin or turnover shows a real advantage. A company beating its industry only on leverage is simply more willing to borrow.

## Part 5b: When this model applies and when it fails

The decomposition applies to any company with positive equity and meaningful operating income. Most non-financial firms qualify. The model degrades in specific places that the reader must watch for.

Negative or near-zero equity makes Financial Leverage explode and the whole chain becomes meaningless. Years of buybacks can push a mature company into this state. Negative pre-tax earnings or negative EBIT make the burden ratios uninterpretable, which sidelines the tool in exactly the crisis years when the cause of trouble matters most.

For a financial company the chain computes but misleads. Interest is the bank's operating cost and leverage is its raw material, so the factors do not mean what they mean elsewhere. Bank analysis replaces the decomposition with margin-on-assets frameworks. Because the decomposition explains accounting ROE, it also inherits accounting's blind spots. The chain cannot see whether the equity base is distorted by history or whether net income is backed by cash. The earnings quality metrics beside it must supply that. Within its domain the decomposition is an identity and cannot be wrong. The judgment lies in knowing the domain.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows a multi-year table of every lever beside the ROE history. The table is the section's real instrument, because the table rewrites ROE's history as five separate stories. Read each row's path across the years rather than the single latest value.

A stable Tax Burden row is neutral. A rising one means more profit is kept, usually from a rate change worth checking for durability. A falling Interest Burden row means debt cost is eating a growing slice of operating profit, often before the leverage ratios raise an alarm. The EBIT Margin row is the operating trend and deserves the most weight. The Asset Turnover row tells the efficiency and investment cycle story. The Financial Leverage row drifting upward, especially alongside buybacks, means the equity base is thinning under the same operations.

Three patterns matter most. ROE flat while EBIT Margin falls and Financial Leverage rises is decline masked by amplification, the most important warning the table can give. ROE rising on EBIT Margin and Asset Turnover with leverage flat is the healthy configuration. All five rows steady across the full history is a business in equilibrium, where the question shifts to the price being paid for that stability. Watch for base effects, where a single weak prior year can make any row look like a trend.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** ROE is the headline the decomposition explains. The decomposition is the diagnosis and ROE is the symptom. Reading the single number first sets the question, and the five levers answer it. Without the headline the levers have no target, and without the levers the headline has no explanation.

**What the combination tells you.** A high ROE paired with a factor table dominated by EBIT Margin and Asset Turnover is an earned return that tends to repeat. The same high ROE paired with a factor table dominated by Financial Leverage is a borrowed return that reverses when conditions turn. Reading the pair tells the reader not just how large the return is but how safe it is.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC is return on invested capital, the return the business earns before the choice of financing. ROE includes the effect of leverage, while ROIC strips leverage out. Reading the two side by side separates operating return from the boost that borrowing adds.

**What the combination tells you.** ROIC close to ROE means the leverage levers are doing little and the return is mostly operating. A wide gap between a high ROE and a lower ROIC means borrowing is the source of the difference. The decomposition then shows whether that gap sits in the Financial Leverage row or the Interest Burden row.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest Coverage measures how many times operating income covers the year's interest bill. The Interest Burden lever measures the share of operating profit that survives interest. Both describe the cost of debt from the operating profit, so they move together. Reading them as a pair confirms what a change in the burden lever means.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling Interest Burden row alongside falling Interest Coverage means debt risk is genuinely rising. A falling Interest Burden row while Interest Coverage holds steady points to higher rates on a similar debt load rather than more debt. The combination separates a rising risk from a passing rate effect.

### Income Quality (CFO / NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income Quality compares cash from operations to net income. The decomposition explains net income, which is an accrual figure built from accounting estimates. Income Quality checks whether that net income is backed by real cash. The decomposition explains the number, and Income Quality tests whether the number deserved explaining.

**What the combination tells you.** A clean decomposition built on a low Income Quality reading is an elegant explanation of a figure that cash does not support. A high Income Quality reading means the explained ROE rests on cash earnings. Reading the pair stops the reader from trusting a well-decomposed return that the cash flow statement does not confirm.

## Part 8: Limitations

The decomposition explains accounting ROE and inherits every defect of accounting ROE. Net income can be inflated by accrual choices. Equity can be distorted by history or shrunk by buybacks. The single-period snapshot misses timing. All of those pass straight through the identity unchanged.

The five factors are not fully independent in reality. Borrowing moves both Financial Leverage and Interest Burden. Capital intensity links EBIT Margin and Asset Turnover. Reading the levers as separate dials overstates how freely management can move each one. The standard form also blends operating liabilities such as supplier financing with debt inside the leverage factor. The single multiplier then flatters neither analysis. The decomposition diagnoses but does not value. Nothing in the chain says what the ROE is worth paying for. Nothing in the chain speaks to cash, growth, or risk. The decomposition is the best explanation device in ratio analysis, and explanation is all it claims to provide.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which of the five factors contributed most to the current ROE level, and which contributed most to its change across the multi-year table?
2. Is the EBIT Margin row, the most durable lever, rising, flat, or falling? Does its direction agree with the gross margin and pricing evidence elsewhere on the page?
3. What does the Financial Leverage row show across the years? How much of its movement came from new borrowing versus buybacks shrinking the equity denominator?
4. Is the Interest Burden row stable, or is a growing slice of operating profit being consumed by lenders? Does the Interest Coverage ratio confirm the trend?
5. How much of the recent ROE change is the Tax Burden row? Is that movement tax law, country mix, or a one-time item, none of which deserves a higher valuation?
6. If Financial Leverage were held at its level from five years ago, what would today's ROE be? Is the market paying for operating improvement or for amplification?
7. How does each lever compare against the company's two closest peers? On which levers does this company actually win?
8. Does the reconciliation line hold cleanly? Where the section's average-equity ROE differs from the panel's period-end figure, which convention better represents this company's year?
9. Is the ROE being explained backed by cash, per the Income Quality metric, or is the chain explaining an accrual figure?
10. Given which levers produced the ROE, what specifically would have to break for the return to revert: a tax regime, a refinancing, a price war, or a capital program? Which of those risks is the current valuation ignoring?
