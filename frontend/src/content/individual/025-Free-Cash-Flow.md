# Free Cash Flow

## Part 1: Definition

Free cash flow is the cash a company has left after it pays its operating costs and the capital spending needed to keep and grow its assets. Operating cash flow is the cash the core business produces before any investing and financing activity. Capital expenditure (also called capex) is the cash spent on long-lived assets such as equipment, buildings, and technology. Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure.

Free cash flow answers one question. How much cash could the owners take out this year without starving the business? Free cash flow is often called owner earnings. Owner earnings is the cash that could be removed from the business each year without harming its operations. The remaining cash can fund dividends and buybacks. The same cash can repay debt or pay for acquisitions.

| Pattern | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| FCF strongly positive and near net income | Reported profit is turning into real cash. The business spends little extra capital to earn it. |
| FCF positive but far below profit | The company keeps cash, but heavy capex or working-capital needs absorb much of the profit. |
| FCF near zero | Operating cash is being fully consumed by capital spending. Little is left for owners this year. |
| FCF negative | Capex exceeds operating cash flow. The cause can be heavy investment or a weak operating business. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure`

Operating cash flow is the starting cash figure. Operating cash flow is the cash the core business generates before investing and financing activity. Operating cash flow already nets cash collections against operating payments and taxes.

Capital expenditure is the cash spent on long-lived assets. Capex appears in the investing section of the cash flow statement. Capex splits into two kinds. Maintenance capex is the spending needed just to keep current earnings going. Growth capex is the spending that expands the business. Companies rarely report the split, so the simple formula charges both kinds together.

A common variant separates the cash flow available before and after debt payments.

| Variant | What it measures | When it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) | Cash available to all investors before financing costs | Valuing the whole business, debt and equity together |
| Free cash flow to equity (FCFE) | Cash available to shareholders after debt payments | Valuing the equity directly |

This app uses operating cash flow minus capital expenditure.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Free cash flow answers a question that profit alone cannot. Profit can be high while the business consumes cash on capital spending to produce that profit. Free cash flow charges for that spending. Free cash flow shows the cash that is genuinely available after the business is kept running.

Free cash flow is the cash that actually funds owner returns. Dividends and buybacks both draw on this cash. Debt repayment and acquisitions draw on it too. A management plan is only as real as the free cash flow behind it.

Valuation models rely on free cash flow directly. Many models estimate a company's worth as the present value of its future free cash flow. Free cash flow connects the quality of the business to its value.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Everything that moves operating cash flow moves free cash flow. Earnings, working-capital swings, and the timing of cash collections all feed the operating cash flow base. A year of slow collections or rising inventory can pull free cash flow down even when profit holds steady.

The capex cycle is the distinctive driver. A year of heavy expansion spending can push free cash flow down or even negative in a healthy business. The end of such a spending wave can lift free cash flow sharply while the business merely coasts. The capex cycle is why free cash flow swings more than most metrics.

The choice to capitalize spending or expense it also matters. Capitalized spending appears in capex and lowers free cash flow over time. Expensed spending appears in operating cash flow and lowers it now. Cutting capex lifts free cash flow in the short term. The same cut can starve future earnings if the spending was needed.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | Typical FCF behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-light software | High and steady FCF | Capex is small, so most operating cash converts to free cash. |
| Utilities, telecom, industrials | Low or negative FCF during build-outs | Capex is heavy and arrives in waves, absorbing operating cash for years. |
| Early-stage growth firms | Negative FCF by choice | The company spends ahead of earnings to capture future growth. |
| Retail | FCF swings with the cycle | Store expansion and inventory needs move capex and working capital. |
| Banks | FCF is not a standard measure | Capex is not the main cash use, so the metric does not fit the model. |

A cross-sector comparison of raw free cash flow levels is meaningless. A large company will show more dollars than a small one for reasons of size alone. Scale free cash flow by revenue or by share count before comparing companies across sectors.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A multi-year free cash flow series tells more than any single year. A steady rise alongside revenue is the compounding pattern that valuation models reward. A steady decline raises the question of whether operating cash is weakening or capex is climbing. A step change usually marks a major expansion or the end of one.

High volatility is normal for capital-heavy businesses because capex arrives in waves. Base effects can fake a trend. A single light-capex year can lift free cash flow without any real improvement. A one-time working-capital release can do the same.

Separate a capex-driven dip from an operating decline. Free cash flow falling while operating cash flow holds means capex is rising. Free cash flow falling because operating cash flow is shrinking is a deeper problem. Decompose every large move into its operating and capex parts before drawing a conclusion.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure. The gap between the two figures is the capex burden the business carries. Reading both shows how much of the operating cash survives the capital spending.

**What the combination tells you.** A small gap means the business is capital-light and keeps most of its operating cash. A wide gap means heavy capex consumes the operating cash before owners see it. Watching the two lines over time shows whether the capex burden is rising or easing.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Depreciation measures how fast existing assets wear out. Capex measures what the company spends on new assets. Comparing the two shows whether the company is investing more or less than its assets are aging.

**What the combination tells you.** Capex above depreciation means the company is expanding its asset base, which often depresses current free cash flow. Capex below depreciation for several years means the company may be underinvesting and harvesting cash. This ratio tells you which phase of the spending cycle the current free cash flow reflects.

### FCF / Net Income

**Why read the two together.** Net income is the accounting profit. Free cash flow is the cash that profit becomes after capital spending. Dividing one by the other shows how much reported profit converts into free cash.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio near one means profit is turning into owner cash with little leakage. A ratio well below one means capital intensity or working-capital needs are absorbing the profit. A persistent gap suggests the earnings figure overstates the cash the business actually produces.

### P/FCF

**Why read the two together.** P/FCF is the price paid for each dollar of free cash flow. Free cash flow is the cash an owner can claim. The ratio links the company's price to the cash it generates for owners.

**What the combination tells you.** A low P/FCF can signal a cheap price or a free cash flow figure expected to fall. A high P/FCF means investors are paying up for future free cash growth. Reading the ratio against the free cash flow trend shows whether the price is supported by a rising or a fading cash stream.

## Part 8: Limitations

Free cash flow can be lifted in ways that do not reflect a stronger business. Cutting needed capex raises it now and weakens it later. Stretching payments to suppliers or selling receivables pulls cash forward without earning it. Total capex mixes maintenance and growth, so a company investing heavily for the future can look cash-poor while building real value.

Free cash flow is lumpy because capex arrives in waves. A single year tells little for a capital-heavy company, so a multi-year view is required. The operating cash flow input carries its own distortions, since stock compensation and timing effects can inflate it. Free cash flow also says nothing about how the cash is then spent. A company can generate strong free cash flow and waste it on poor acquisitions.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Decompose the current free cash flow level. How much of it comes from operating cash flow and how much from the level of capital expenditure?
2. Is the latest free cash flow figure shaped mainly by the operating business or by where the company sits in its capex cycle?
3. What share of current capex is maintenance and what share is growth? What would free cash flow look like if growth spending stopped?
4. Is Capex / Depreciation above or below 1.0, and for how many consecutive years? Does the current free cash flow reflect a building phase or a harvest phase?
5. How much of the free cash flow in the strongest years came from working-capital releases rather than normal operations?
6. How much of reported net income converts into free cash flow? Is the conversion rate steady, rising, or falling over the chart?
7. Does the free cash flow stream survive subtracting stock-based compensation from the operating cash flow base?
8. Is the free cash flow growth genuine expansion, or the result of a weak starting year and capex timing?
9. Where has the free cash actually gone over the chart's history? Track how much went to dividends and buybacks versus debt repayment and acquisitions.
10. If pricing power eroded, which part would weaken first, the operating cash margin or the capex requirement? Which line on this page would warn first?
