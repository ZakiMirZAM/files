# Retained Earnings

## Part 1: Definition

Retained earnings is the running total of all the profit a company has ever earned and chosen to keep inside the business instead of paying out. Net income is the profit left after all costs, interest, and taxes. Each year the company can hand some of that profit to shareholders as a dividend, which is a cash payment to owners. Whatever profit is not paid out stays in the company and adds to retained earnings. Retained earnings answers one question: how much of the company's lifetime profit has the company kept and put back to work?

Retained earnings sits in the equity section of the balance sheet. Equity is the owners' share of the company, the part of the assets that is funded by the owners rather than by lenders. Retained earnings is the part of equity the business built from its own profits. Retained earnings differs from paid-in capital, which is the money owners put in when they bought newly issued shares. In the app's vertical analysis the line is shown as a percent of total assets, so the reader can see how much of everything the company owns has been funded by kept-back profit.

| Retained earnings reading | What that reading usually means |
|---|---|
| Large and rising as a share of assets | The business has funded much of its own growth from profit it kept. |
| Small or flat | The company pays out most of its profit, or it is young and has not built up much profit yet. |
| Negative (called an accumulated deficit) | Lifetime losses, or payouts beyond lifetime profit, have wiped out the kept profit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Retained earnings is updated each year with a simple rollforward:

`Ending Retained Earnings = Beginning Retained Earnings + Net Income − Dividends Declared`

The starting number is last year's ending balance. Net income for the year is added because profit kept in the business raises the total. Dividends declared during the year are subtracted because a dividend pays profit out to owners and removes it from the kept total. A loss year subtracts instead of adds, because a loss reduces the profit the company has banked.

Two details change the line without the business itself changing. Share buybacks are sometimes charged against retained earnings, which can shrink the line at heavy repurchasers. A buyback is the use of cash to repurchase the company's own shares. Restatements, which are corrections to prior reported numbers, also adjust the opening balance. Certain gains and losses skip net income entirely and land in a neighboring equity line called accumulated other comprehensive income, so those items never flow through retained earnings.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Retained earnings shows how much a company has been able to fund itself. A business that keeps profit and reinvests it does not have to borrow or sell new shares to grow. A rising retained earnings line built from real profit is a sign the company has an internal engine for growth.

The level of retained earnings against paid-in capital tells a short history. A company whose retained earnings now dwarf the money owners first put in has repaid its founding many times over from profit. A company still showing a deficit is still consuming the capital owners supplied.

The kept profit only matters if the profit was put to good use. A common test from Warren Buffett is that every dollar a company keeps should create at least one dollar of market value over time. Retained earnings counts how much was kept. Return on equity grades what the kept money earns. The two questions belong together.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Net income drives retained earnings up. A strong profit year adds a large amount. A weak year adds little, and a loss year subtracts.

Dividends drive retained earnings down. A company that pays out most of its profit will show a flat line even in good years, because the cash leaves as fast as profit arrives.

Share buybacks can reduce the line at companies that charge repurchases against retained earnings. Heavy buybacks can even push the line negative at a profitable company, which is an accounting effect rather than a sign of trouble. Restatements and other corrections can rewrite the opening balance in either direction.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a healthy retained earnings level depends on where the company is in its life and how it returns cash to owners.

| Company type | Typical retained earnings behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Young growth companies | Often a deficit that deepens, then turns up | Early losses and heavy investment come before steady profit. |
| Mature, reinvesting companies | Large and steadily rising | Years of kept profit have funded the asset base. |
| High-payout mature companies | Flat at a high level | Profit is earned but paid straight out as dividends, so the line stops climbing. |
| Heavy buyback companies | Flat, shrinking, or negative | Repurchases charged against the line offset the profit added. |

A cross-company comparison of the raw retained earnings level is meaningless when the companies differ in age or payout policy. Compare the trajectory and the profit that built it, not just the height.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows retained earnings as its own multi-year series and as a percent of total assets. A steady climb means the company keeps profit year after year and funds itself from earnings. The slope of the climb is set by how much profit is kept and how well that kept profit earns. A line that rises while return on equity stays high is the strongest pattern, because kept profit is compounding.

A flat line usually means full payout, where dividends and buybacks equal the year's profit. A declining line means loss years, or payouts larger than profit. A single negative year inside a long rise is often a restatement or a buyback charge rather than a real loss, so check the rollforward before reading it as damage. A line that grows while return on equity fades is accumulation without compounding, which is profit kept but earning weak returns.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Income

**Why read the two together.** Net income is the yearly profit that feeds retained earnings. Retained earnings is the running stack of all the net income the company ever kept. Reading the flow next to the stack shows whether the kept total is growing because profit is strong or because the company simply stopped paying it out.

**What the combination tells you.** A retained earnings line rising in step with steady net income means the company earns profit and banks it. A retained earnings line that climbs while net income is flat usually means the company cut its dividend rather than grew its profit. Watching both over several years separates a genuine earnings engine from a company that is merely retaining more of a stagnant profit.

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity, or ROE, is net income divided by total equity. Retained earnings is a large part of that equity for most mature companies. ROE grades what the kept profit earns. Retained earnings counts how much profit was kept in the first place. Neither number means much alone.

**What the combination tells you.** A growing retained earnings line paired with a high ROE means kept profit is earning strong returns and compounding. A growing retained earnings line paired with a falling ROE means the company is piling up profit it cannot reinvest well. Reading the pair is the direct test of whether each kept dollar is building value.

### Dividends Paid

**Why read the two together.** Dividends paid is the cash sent out to owners, and that payout is exactly what is subtracted in the retained earnings rollforward. Retained earnings shows what was kept. Dividends paid shows what was returned. The two together show the full split of each year's profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A flat retained earnings line beside large dividends means the company chooses to return most of its profit rather than reinvest it. A rising retained earnings line beside small dividends means the company is keeping profit to fund itself. The balance between the two reveals management's choice between reinvesting and paying owners.

## Part 8: Limitations

Retained earnings is a historical accounting total, not a pile of cash. The kept profit has usually been spent on inventory and equipment and other assets. A company can therefore show large retained earnings and very little cash at the same time. The line says nothing about whether the kept profit earned a good return; that is the job of return on equity.

Several accounting choices distort the line. Buyback charges can shrink it or drive it negative at heavy repurchasers with no economic meaning. Restatements rewrite prior balances. The level alone is a record of the past, and the useful reading is the trajectory and the returns the kept profit earned.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is the multi-year path of retained earnings? Does the rollforward trace each move to profit and dividends, or to buybacks and restatements?
2. Is the line rising because net income is strong, or because the company stopped paying it out?
3. What does the kept profit earn, judged by return on equity over the same years?
4. Does each dollar kept appear to have built at least a dollar of market value over time?
5. How large is retained earnings against paid-in capital, and what does that ratio say about how far the company has repaid its founding?
6. If the line is negative, is the deficit from early-stage investment and real losses, or from buyback charges?
7. How does the company split each year's profit between dividends, buybacks, and retained earnings?
8. Have any restatements rewritten the opening balance, and what caused them?
9. How does the trajectory compare with peers at the same stage of life?
10. If retention continued at the current rate and current returns, what would the line compound to, and is that the company's stated plan?
