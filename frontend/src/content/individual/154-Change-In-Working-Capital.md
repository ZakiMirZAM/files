# Change In Working Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Change in working capital is the single line that captures how much cash the company's day-to-day operating accounts soaked up or released during the year. Working capital is the money tied up in running the business. The main pieces are money owed by customers, goods held in inventory, and money owed to suppliers. Money owed by customers is called accounts receivable. Goods held for sale are inventory. Money owed to suppliers is accounts payable. When these accounts grow, they tie up more cash. When they shrink, they free cash. The line nets all of those moves into one figure. The line answers one question: did the operating accounts consume cash or release it this year?

The operating section of the cash flow statement starts with net income and adjusts it toward the cash the business produced. Change in working capital is one of the largest adjustments. The line is negative when the accounts tied up more cash, which often happens as a company grows. The line is positive when the accounts released cash, which often happens in a slowdown.

| Direction of the line | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | Operating accounts consumed cash. Growth is absorbing cash into receivables and inventory. |
| Near zero | The accounts roughly held steady. Little cash moved through the operating loop. |
| Positive | Operating accounts released cash. The loop returned cash, often in a downturn. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The line is reported directly on the cash flow statement. The build is:

`Change In Working Capital = the sum of the changes in the operating accounts`

Each operating account contributes its change with a sign. A rise in money owed by customers subtracts cash, because the sale was booked but not yet collected. A rise in inventory subtracts cash, because cash went into goods not yet sold. A rise in money owed to suppliers adds cash, because the company is holding onto cash longer. Money collected in advance from customers also adds cash.

The app's table shows the component lines beneath this summary, so the breakdown is visible directly. The net sign and size against revenue measures how much cash the operating loop ties up. A business that collects fast and pays slowly can run this loop near zero or even release cash as it grows.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Change in working capital is the largest ordinary reason operating cash flow differs from profit. A company can report real profit while the cash sits parked in unpaid customer bills and unsold inventory. The line measures how much cash the operating loop is holding, which is why it sits at the center of judging cash quality.

The line also prices the cash cost of growth. Many businesses must fund more receivables and inventory as sales rise, and the line shows how much cash each year of growth absorbs. A business that consumes little working capital as it grows funds its own expansion. A business that consumes a lot must find the cash elsewhere.

The question the line answers is whether profit turned into cash or stayed locked in the operating accounts. A single year's swing can be timing, so the multi-year pattern carries the real signal about the business structure.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The operating accounts drive the line. Customer payment terms and collection speed move receivables. Demand and production decisions move inventory. Supplier terms move payables. Customers who pay in advance add cash. Each account can shift by management decision or by changing trade conditions.

Rhythm also moves the line. Seasonal businesses build inventory before peak selling and draw it down after, which swings the line within a year. Growth consumes working capital as the accounts expand, while a slowdown releases it as the accounts shrink. A downturn can produce a positive line that looks like strength but is really the operating loop unwinding as sales fall.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The structural behavior depends on how a sector's operating loop works. Comparing the line across very different loops can mislead.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription and prepaid services | Releases cash with growth | Customers pay before the service is delivered. |
| Retail and distribution | Consumes cash with growth | Inventory must be bought and held before it sells. |
| Heavy manufacturing | Large consumption with growth | Long production cycles tie up cash in goods and receivables. |
| Project-based businesses | Lurches between consumption and release | Work is billed in stages, so timing swings widely. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one business collects cash before it spends and another must fund inventory for months. Compare the line against companies with a similar operating loop.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the line as a percent of revenue across the years the app shows. Revenue is the common-size base for the cash flow statement. The multi-year net reveals the structure. Consistently negative lines mean the business spends cash to grow its operating accounts. Consistently positive lines mean the loop returns cash, a sign of customers funding the business. Oscillation around zero is the normal rhythm of timing.

The component lines beneath the summary let the trend be diagnosed. A consumption driven by receivables raises questions about collection. A consumption driven by inventory raises questions about demand. A release driven by stretching supplier terms is different from a release driven by falling sales. A positive line in a downturn is temporary comfort, since the loop re-absorbs cash once sales recover. Average across several years to see the structure, and bracket single-year swings as rhythm.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** Cash Conversion Cycle measures, in days, how long cash is tied up in the operating loop from paying suppliers to collecting from customers. Change in working capital measures the same loop in cash for the year. One states the loop in days, the other in dollars of cash moved.

**What the combination tells you.** A lengthening cash conversion cycle alongside a consuming working capital line confirms the loop is tying up more cash as it slows. A shortening cycle alongside a releasing line confirms the loop is freeing cash. The pair shows the same operating loop from the time side and the cash side.

### Income Quality (CFO / NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income Quality compares operating cash flow with net income to judge how cash-backed the profit is. Change in working capital is the largest ordinary driver of the gap between those two figures. A big working capital swing is often why operating cash flow sits above or below profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A weak Income Quality reading caused by a heavy consuming working capital line means profit is real but parked in receivables and inventory. A strong reading helped by a releasing line in a downturn can flatter cash quality temporarily. The pair shows how much of the cash-versus-profit gap is the operating loop.

### Accounts Receivables Change

**Why read the two together.** Accounts Receivables Change is the receivables component of this summary line, usually its largest single piece. Reading the component next to the total shows whether the overall working capital move is driven by collection from customers or by the other accounts.

**What the combination tells you.** A consuming working capital line driven mostly by rising receivables points to a collection or recognition issue. A consuming line driven mostly by inventory points to a demand or production issue. The pair lets the analyst see which leg of the loop is moving the total.

## Part 8: Limitations

The line nets several accounts into one figure, so the summary hides the components. A consumption from rising inventory looks the same as a consumption from slow collection, yet the two carry different meanings. The app's component table is needed to read the line properly.

Timing noise dominates single years. A large swing in one year can be a payment that landed just before or after the year-end, not a structural change. The multi-year average shows the structure, while one year shows mostly rhythm. The line should be read by component, averaged for structure, and bracketed for seasonal and cyclical swings.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Over several years, does the net line price the operating loop as a cash consumer or a cash releaser?
2. Which component drives the move among the receivables, inventory, and payables lines beneath the summary?
3. How much cash does the loop consume for each dollar of revenue growth?
4. Which years are ordinary rhythm and which reflect a real structural shift?
5. Is any year's consumption driven by receivables rising faster than sales, a possible collection warning?
6. How much of a positive line came from a downturn unwinding the loop rather than genuine improvement?
7. How does this line reconcile with the gap that Income Quality measures between cash and profit?
8. Could any extreme early year be a data-mapping difference, checked against the filings?
9. How does the operating loop's cash use compare against companies with a similar business model?
10. What would the line swing to if payment terms reverted to a normal level?
