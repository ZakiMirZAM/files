# FCF To Net Income

## Part 1: Definition

FCF to net income measures how much of each dollar of reported profit turns into spendable cash. FCF stands for free cash flow, the cash a company has left after running the business and paying for the equipment and buildings it needs to keep operating. Net income is the bottom-line profit shown on the income statement after all costs, interest, and tax. The ratio answers one question: is the profit the company reports backed by real cash, or does it exist mainly on paper?

Net income is built on accruals. An accrual is a profit or cost recorded when it is earned or incurred rather than when the cash actually moves. A company can book a sale as profit before the customer pays, so reported profit and cash collected can drift apart. The ratio measures that gap. A ratio near or above 1.0 means profit is converting fully into cash. A ratio well below 1.0 means a large part of the reported profit has not arrived as cash.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.5 | Less than half of reported profit becomes cash. Heavy reinvestment or weak earnings quality is likely. |
| 0.5 to 0.8 | A meaningful share of profit is consumed by the asset base. Common for growing or capital-heavy firms. |
| 0.8 to 1.2 | Profit converts well into cash. A healthy and typical range for a mature business. |
| Above 1.2 | Cash exceeds reported profit. Often a sign of large non-cash charges, or of a business shrinking its asset base. |
| Negative | Free cash flow is negative while profit is positive. The company reports earnings but burns cash. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`FCF To Net Income = Free Cash Flow ÷ Net Income`

The top number is free cash flow. Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Operating cash flow is the cash the business actually generated from operations. Capital expenditures, often shortened to capex, is the money spent on equipment and buildings and other long-lived assets. Subtracting capex matters because a company must keep buying assets just to stay in business, and that spending is not available to owners.

The bottom number is net income. Net income is the accounting profit after every expense, including non-cash charges such as depreciation. Depreciation is the yearly accounting cost that spreads the price of a long-lived asset over its useful life. Net income includes such non-cash entries, which is one reason it can differ from cash.

A simpler variant uses operating cash flow instead of free cash flow in the top number. The app's income-quality tile uses that operating-cash-flow version. The FCF version on this page is stricter because the version charges the business for the capex needed to keep running before measuring conversion.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Reported profit can be managed. Choices about when to book revenue and when to recognize costs can lift net income without any change in cash. Cash is far harder to fake. The ratio holds the reported profit up against the cash and shows how much of the profit is real.

A persistent gap between profit and cash is one of the earliest warning signs of trouble. Companies that collapse often report healthy profits for years while their cash conversion quietly deteriorates. Watching the ratio fall over time can flag aggressive accounting before the profit itself turns down.

The ratio also matters for valuation. Dividends, buybacks, and debt repayment all come from cash, not from accounting profit. A company that converts profit into cash poorly can fund less of these than its earnings suggest. The ratio shows how much of the headline profit the company can actually put to use.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The first driver is the gap between profit and cash, called the accrual gap. When a company books sales it has not yet collected, receivables rise and cash lags profit, which lowers the ratio. Receivables are amounts customers owe but have not yet paid. When a company runs down inventory or stretches its payments to suppliers, cash can run ahead of profit, which lifts the ratio.

The second driver is capital spending. Heavy capex pulls the top number down and lowers the ratio, even when the business is healthy and growing. A company in a major build-out phase will show a low ratio for years. A company that has finished investing will show a higher ratio as the capex falls away. The ratio reflects the investment cycle as much as the earnings quality.

Non-cash charges move the two numbers apart. Depreciation and other non-cash costs reduce net income but not cash, which tends to push the ratio above 1.0. One-off items distort single readings. A large asset sale can flood operating or reported figures for a year. A restructuring charge can crush net income while cash holds up, sending the ratio to an extreme that says little about the ongoing business.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a normal ratio depends on how much a sector must reinvest and how its non-cash charges compare with its real spending.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-light software and services | High, often above 1.0 | Little capex and large deferred revenue mean cash often runs ahead of reported profit. |
| Capital-heavy industrials and utilities | Lower, often well below 1.0 | Constant heavy capex consumes cash, so free cash flow stays small relative to profit. |
| Fast-growing companies | Low or negative | Growth absorbs cash into receivables, inventory, and new assets faster than profit converts. |
| Mature, stable companies | Near 1.0 | Capex roughly matches depreciation and working capital is steady, so profit and cash track closely. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Capex and the standard free cash flow definition do not fit financial business models. |

A cross-sector comparison is misleading when the sectors sit at different points in their investment cycles. Compare a company against its own history and against direct competitors at the same growth stage, not against a sector with a different capital profile.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The single most useful pattern is a steady decline in the ratio while reported profit keeps rising. A widening gap between rising profit and lagging cash points to aggressive revenue recognition, a build-up of receivables, or both. A multi-year slide deserves a close look at why cash is failing to follow profit.

A steady ratio near 1.0 across many years is the mark of clean, cash-backed earnings. A ratio that is volatile but averages near 1.0 usually reflects the lumpiness of capex and working capital rather than a quality problem, so read the average across the cycle. A ratio that jumps above 1.0 for a single year is often a one-off, such as an asset sale or a sharp cut in inventory, and tends to reverse. Always check whether a low ratio is caused by genuine reinvestment for growth or by profit that simply is not turning into cash.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Income Quality (CFO/NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income quality compares operating cash flow with net income, before capex is subtracted. FCF to net income goes one step further and charges the business for capex first. Reading both shows where any gap between profit and cash comes from. The first isolates the accrual gap, and the second adds the reinvestment burden on top.

**What the combination tells you.** A high income quality ratio with a low FCF ratio means the earnings are cash-backed but heavy capex is eating the cash, which is the normal profile of a capital-heavy or growing business. A low income quality ratio is more worrying because the problem sits in the accruals themselves, before any capex. Reading the pair tells the investor whether weak conversion is a spending choice or an earnings-quality concern.

### Sloan Accruals

**Why read the two together.** Sloan accruals measure the share of profit that comes from accounting entries rather than cash. A high accruals figure is the direct cause of a low FCF to net income ratio. The two metrics describe the same gap between profit and cash from opposite sides.

**What the combination tells you.** High accruals paired with a low conversion ratio is a strong warning that reported profit is running ahead of cash, and the earnings may not hold. Low accruals paired with a healthy conversion ratio confirms that the profit is genuine. When the two disagree, the difference usually traces to capex, which the conversion ratio charges for and the accruals measure does not.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the top number of the ratio in absolute dollars. The ratio shows conversion as a percentage, and the dollar figure shows the actual size of the cash. A high conversion ratio on tiny profit still produces little cash. Reading both keeps the percentage honest about scale.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising conversion ratio backed by a rising free cash flow figure is unambiguous improvement. A high ratio sitting on shrinking free cash flow means the business is converting well but generating less to convert. Watching the dollar series alongside the ratio separates a genuine cash engine from a flattering percentage.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** This ratio compares capital spending with the depreciation charge. A figure above 1.0 means the company is spending more on assets than it is writing off, which lowers free cash flow and the conversion ratio. The pairing explains how much of a low conversion ratio is simply heavy investment.

**What the combination tells you.** A low conversion ratio paired with capex well above depreciation points to a company investing for growth, which is a choice rather than a flaw. A low conversion ratio with capex near or below depreciation is harder to excuse, because the cash gap is not explained by investment. Reading the two together separates a growth-driven cash drain from an earnings-quality problem.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio is sensitive to the investment cycle and can punish good companies. A business spending heavily to build for the future will show a low ratio for years, even though the spending is the right decision. A business that has stopped investing will show a flattering ratio while its assets quietly age. The ratio cannot tell growth capex from maintenance capex, so it cannot say whether low conversion is healthy investment or a warning sign on its own.

Single-year readings are unreliable because both numbers swing with one-off items. Asset sales, restructuring charges, large tax payments, and swings in working capital can send the ratio far above or below its normal level for a year. A company with negative net income produces a ratio that cannot be read at all, because dividing by a negative profit flips the meaning. The ratio is a strong first screen for earnings quality and a poor final verdict, so it should always be read as a multi-year trend alongside the absolute cash figures.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio low because the company is investing heavily for growth, or because reported profit is not turning into cash? Which of the two is it?
2. Has the ratio been falling over several years while net income kept rising? If so, what is causing cash to lag profit?
3. How much of the gap between profit and cash comes from rising receivables or inventory rather than from capex?
4. Does the income-quality tile, which uses operating cash flow, tell the same story as this stricter free cash flow version? Where do the two diverge?
5. Was the latest reading distorted by a one-off item such as an asset sale, a restructuring charge, or a large tax payment? What is the ratio without it?
6. How does the company's capex compare with its depreciation? Is the low conversion explained by investment that exceeds the wearing-out of existing assets?
7. What was the ratio in the company's weakest year on the chart, and did cash still cover the profit then?
8. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors at the same growth stage? Does this company convert profit to cash better or worse than its peers?
9. If the ratio is above 1.0, is the excess cash a durable feature or a one-time effect of shrinking inventory or selling assets?
10. Can the company fund its dividends, buybacks, and debt repayments from the cash this ratio implies, or only from the accounting profit?
