# Net Debt

## Part 1: Definition

Net debt is total debt minus the cash the company holds. Total debt is all the borrowed money a company must repay, including short-term and long-term loans and bonds. Cash here means cash in the bank plus short-term investments that can be turned into cash quickly. Net debt answers one question: if the company used its ready cash to pay down borrowings today, how much debt would be left?

Net debt measures the real leverage left after the company's own reserves are counted. A company that owes one billion while holding one billion in cash carries no net debt, because the cash could clear the borrowing. When cash is larger than total debt, net debt is a negative number, called a net cash position. In the app's vertical analysis, net debt is shown as a percent of total assets, so a negative row marks a company with more cash than debt.

Common readings of net debt:

| Net debt level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative (net cash) | Cash exceeds borrowings. The company has financial firepower and low risk. |
| Low positive | A modest borrowing load that cash partly offsets. |
| High positive | Borrowings far exceed cash. Earnings and refinancing carry the weight. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The build subtracts cash from borrowings:

`Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash and Short-Term Investments`

Total debt is the gross amount of all borrowings, as defined in the Total Debt entry. Cash and short-term investments are the funds the company could draw on right away, including bank balances and liquid securities.

The subtraction rests on one idea: risk attaches to the borrowing the cash cannot cover. The catch is that not all cash is truly free. Cash held in a foreign subsidiary may be trapped by taxes, and cash needed to run daily operations is not really spare. Net debt reads best when only genuinely surplus cash is netted. Because net debt blends two moving parts, every change should be split into a debt move or a cash move before reading it.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Net debt shows the economic leverage that matters, not just the headline borrowing. A company can carry large gross debt and still be safe if it sits on an equal pile of cash. Net debt strips the offsetting cash away and reveals the position that actually exposes shareholders to risk.

Net debt feeds the standard leverage measures that lenders and buyers use. Net Debt / EBITDA states net borrowing in years of earnings. Enterprise value, the price to buy the whole business, adds net debt to the equity value because a buyer takes on the debt and gains the cash.

The trend in net debt also catches what gross debt can miss. Flat gross debt while cash builds is deleveraging in substance, and net debt falls even though the borrowed amount has not moved. Net debt that improves through cash piling up is a different fact from net debt that improves through actual repayment, so the source of the change is worth checking.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Net debt moves with both sides at once. Borrowing more or repaying loans moves the debt side. Generating cash or spending it on dividends, buybacks, and investment moves the cash side. Net debt is the race between the two, so a single year can see debt rise while cash rises faster, leaving net debt lower.

Buyback and acquisition years move both sides together, because cash leaves and debt is often raised to fund the deal, so net debt can jump sharply. Seasonal swings move the cash balance on the snapshot date, so a figure taken at a low-cash point of the year overstates net debt. Trapped or operational cash lowers net debt on paper while serving no real cushion, so the availability of the cash should be checked.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a safe net debt level depends on how steady the cash flows are and how much cash the business naturally holds.

| Industry type | Typical net debt behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Mature software and tech | Often net cash | Strong cash generation and few hard assets to borrow against. |
| Utilities and infrastructure | High positive | Steady regulated cash flows support large lasting net borrowing. |
| Retail | Swings with the season | Cash builds after peak selling and drains while restocking. |
| Cyclical producers | Judged at the trough | Cash and earnings both fall in a downturn, so net debt rises fast. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Cash and borrowing are the raw materials of the business. |

A cross-sector comparison of net debt is meaningless for banks and insurers, where holding cash and debt is the business itself. Compare a company only against peers with the same cash-flow pattern and the same seasonality.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows net debt as a percent of total assets over several years. Read the trend by splitting each move into its debt side and its cash side. Net debt building during a borrow-and-invest phase and thinning afterward is a normal investment cycle. Net debt falling because cash piles up is a different story from net debt falling because debt is repaid, and the discipline behind each differs.

The most telling moments are the crossings between net cash and net debt. A company moving from net cash into net debt has funded something large, and the source of that funding is the year's real story. A company moving from net debt back into net cash has either repaid borrowings or accumulated cash, and which one it was shapes how durable the improvement is. The worst year on the chart shows the stress position, the level net debt reaches when both earnings and cash come under pressure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Total Debt

**Why read the two together.** Net debt is total debt after cash, so the gross figure is the other half of the picture. Net debt can fall while total debt is flat or even rising, purely because cash grew. Reading total debt beside net debt shows whether the improvement came from less borrowing or from more cash. The two together separate the borrowing trajectory from the reserve trajectory.

**What the combination tells you.** Falling net debt with falling total debt is genuine deleveraging through repayment. Falling net debt with flat total debt is cash accumulation, which can reverse the moment the cash is spent. Reading the pair shows how solid the deleveraging really is and whether it would survive a large outflow.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net debt states the leverage in dollars. Net Debt / EBITDA states the same leverage in years of earnings. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Net debt alone does not say whether the load is large relative to what the business earns, while the ratio scales the figure to the earning power that must support it.

**What the combination tells you.** A modest net debt with a low Net Debt / EBITDA is comfortable on both size and earnings coverage. A modest net debt with a high ratio warns that earnings are thin relative to the borrowing. Watching both over time shows whether the leverage is being worked down by repayment or by rising earnings.

### Net Debt / OCF

**Why read the two together.** Net Debt / EBITDA uses an earnings figure that is not cash. Net Debt / OCF uses operating cash flow, the actual cash the business produces, which is a stricter test of repayment. Reading both alongside net debt shows whether the earnings used to size the debt are turning into real cash. A gap between the two ratios flags earnings that are not converting to cash.

**What the combination tells you.** When Net Debt / OCF is close to Net Debt / EBITDA, the earnings behind the leverage are cash-backed and trustworthy. When Net Debt / OCF is far higher, the company earns on paper but collects less cash, so the debt is harder to service than the earnings ratio suggests. The pair shows whether net debt is supported by cash or only by accounting profit.

## Part 8: Limitations

Net debt can flatter a company when its cash is not truly free. Cash trapped abroad or needed for daily operations still reduces the figure, even though it could not really be used to repay debt. The honest reading nets only surplus cash, which the headline figure does not do on its own.

Net debt blends two stories into one number, so a reader who does not split the change into debt and cash can misread accumulation as discipline. The figure is also a snapshot taken on one date, so seasonal cash swings can distort it. Net debt says nothing about when the debts come due or what they cost, and a negative net debt can sit beside borrowings that still carry strict loan conditions. Net debt is the best single read of economic leverage and a poor substitute for the full debt picture.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How does each year's change in net debt split between a debt move and a cash move?
2. How much of the netted cash is truly surplus, and how much is trapped abroad or needed to run the business?
3. What does net debt read in the company's worst year on the chart, when earnings and cash are both under pressure?
4. Has the company crossed between net cash and net debt, and what funded each crossing?
5. Is the improvement in net debt coming from actual repayment, or from cash piling up that could be spent at any time?
6. How does net debt as a share of assets compare with direct peers that share the same seasonality?
7. What ratio does net debt produce against earnings and against operating cash flow, and do the two agree?
8. How much does the snapshot date distort net debt because of seasonal swings in the cash balance?
9. What would net debt become if the company spent its cash on a planned acquisition or buyback?
10. Do strict loan conditions or near-term maturities sit behind the debt even when net debt looks low or negative?
