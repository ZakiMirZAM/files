# Net Debt To OCF

## Part 1: Definition

Net debt to OCF measures how many years of operating cash flow it would take to repay a company's debt, after subtracting the cash the company already holds. Net debt is all the company's borrowings minus the cash on hand, the borrowing that would remain if every spare dollar of cash were used to pay debt down. OCF stands for operating cash flow, the cash the business actually generated from its normal operations during the year. The ratio answers one question: how heavy is the debt compared with the cash the business produces to repay it?

The ratio frames debt in years. A ratio of 1.5 means a year and a half of operating cash flow, used entirely for repayment, would clear the net debt. A negative ratio means the company holds more cash than debt, so its net debt is below zero. The ratio uses cash flow rather than profit because debt is repaid with cash, and the metric tests repayment capacity directly.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | Cash exceeds debt. The company has no net borrowing to repay. |
| Below 2.0 | A light debt load. The business produces enough cash to clear its debt quickly. |
| 2.0 to 3.0 | A moderate load. Manageable for most stable companies. |
| 3.0 to 4.0 | A heavy load. Safe only when cash flow is steady and predictable. |
| Above 4.0 | A very heavy load. The company depends on conditions staying favorable to service the debt. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Net Debt To OCF = Net Debt ÷ Operating Cash Flow`

The top number is net debt. Net debt is total debt minus cash and short-term investments. Total debt is all interest-bearing borrowing, including bank loans, bonds, and the debt portion of leases. The cash is subtracted because cash on hand could be used to repay debt immediately, so net debt measures the borrowing that would truly remain.

The bottom number is operating cash flow. Operating cash flow is the cash the business produced from its core operations, taken from the cash flow statement. The figure already reflects the actual cash collected from customers and paid to suppliers and staff, after the swings in working capital. Working capital is the cash tied up in day-to-day items such as inventory and unpaid customer bills.

A close relative is net debt to EBITDA, which uses EBITDA in the bottom instead of operating cash flow. EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization, an estimate of cash earnings that ignores the swings in working capital. The app reports the operating-cash-flow version, which is stricter because the version uses the real cash the operations produced rather than an estimate of it.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Debt is repaid with cash, not with profit. A company can report healthy earnings while collecting little actual cash, and the earnings will not service the debt. Net debt to OCF tests the debt against the real cash the business generated, which is the cash available to repay it. The metric measures repayment capacity at its source.

The metric is stricter than the EBITDA version because operating cash flow already absorbs the working-capital swings that EBITDA ignores. A company that ties up a lot of cash in inventory and unpaid customer bills will look more indebted on this ratio than on the EBITDA version, which is the honest result. The metric catches a debt load that an earnings-based measure can flatter.

The metric also nets out the company's own cash. A business with large borrowings but an equally large cash pile is far safer than the gross debt suggests, because the cash could clear much of the debt at once. Using net debt rather than gross debt shows the true repayment burden after the company's existing resources are counted.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The first driver is the level of borrowing. New debt raised for acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends lifts net debt and raises the ratio. Repaying debt or building up cash lowers net debt and brings the ratio down. The ratio moves directly with how the company funds itself and with how much cash it chooses to hold.

The second driver is operating cash flow in the bottom number. A strong year of cash generation lowers the ratio, and a weak year raises it, even when the debt has not changed. Operating cash flow swings more than profit because the figure absorbs working capital. A build-up of inventory or unpaid customer bills drains cash and can push the ratio up sharply for a year that was otherwise normal.

One-off items distort single readings. A large tax payment, a legal settlement, or a temporary swing in supplier payments can move operating cash flow for a year without any change in the underlying business. An acquisition can add debt and cash flow on different timelines, so the ratio can spike before the acquired cash flow arrives. Because the bottom number is volatile, a single year's reading should be smoothed against several years.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a safe ratio depends on how steady the company's cash flow is and how the sector is structured.

| Industry type | Typical comfortable range | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | Higher, up to 4 or 5 | Regulated, predictable cash flow safely supports a heavier debt load. |
| Software and other capital-light firms | Often negative | Strong cash generation and little need for debt leave many holding more cash than debt. |
| Capital-heavy industrials | Lower, around 2 to 3 | Cyclical cash flow can fall sharply, so the debt load must leave room for a bad year. |
| Commodity producers | Judge at the worst year | Cash flow swings with the commodity price, so the ratio in a boom year understates the risk. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | Borrowing and cash are part of the business model itself, so the standard ratio does not apply. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the sectors have very different cash-flow stability. A utility can safely carry a ratio that would be alarming for a cyclical miner. Compare a company against direct competitors in the same sector, and weight its worst cash-flow year on the chart more heavily than its best.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in the ratio means debt is growing faster than the cash the business produces. A rise driven by new borrowing is a leverage decision to examine against the steadiness of the company's cash flow. A rise driven by falling operating cash flow is a different warning, because the business is generating less cash to service a debt load that has not changed. Identify which side is moving before drawing a conclusion.

A single bad year can spike the ratio when operating cash flow dips for a temporary reason such as a working-capital build or a large one-off payment. The spike matters less if cash flow recovers the next year. A steady decline in the ratio means the company is repaying debt or growing its cash generation, which strengthens the balance sheet. Watch the worst year on the chart most closely, because debt must be sized to survive a downturn, and the ratio in a strong year proves little.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net debt to EBITDA uses estimated cash earnings in the bottom, while this ratio uses the real operating cash flow. EBITDA ignores the swings in working capital, and operating cash flow includes them. Reading both shows how much working capital is affecting the company's true cash generation. The gap between the two ratios is the size of that effect.

**What the combination tells you.** A low net-debt-to-EBITDA ratio paired with a much higher net-debt-to-OCF ratio means working capital is draining cash that the earnings figure does not see, so the real debt load is heavier than EBITDA suggests. When the two ratios are close, the company converts earnings to cash cleanly and either measure can be trusted. Reading the pair exposes a debt load that an earnings-based measure would flatter.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Net debt to OCF measures the size of the debt in years of cash flow, while interest coverage measures whether profit can comfortably pay the yearly interest. Size and affordability are different questions. A company can carry a large debt it services easily at low rates, or a small debt it struggles to cover.

**What the combination tells you.** A high net-debt-to-OCF ratio with strong interest coverage means a large load carried cheaply, which is exposed to refinancing risk if rates rise. A high ratio with weak coverage is the dangerous case, where the debt is both large and expensive to service. Reading the two together separates how much is owed from how easily the current interest is paid.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Operating cash flow in this ratio comes before capital spending, while free cash flow is what remains after the company pays for the equipment and buildings it needs. Free cash flow is the cash truly left over to repay debt after the business has reinvested in itself. Reading both shows whether the operating cash in the ratio is actually free to service the debt.

**What the combination tells you.** A comfortable net-debt-to-OCF ratio can hide a problem if heavy capex leaves little free cash flow, because the operating cash is needed just to keep the business running. When free cash flow is strong relative to debt, the repayment capacity in the ratio is genuine. Reading the two together shows how much of the operating cash is really available to pay debt down.

### FCF / Total Debt

**Why read the two together.** FCF to total debt measures free cash flow against gross debt, the share of the whole debt the company could repay from one year of truly spare cash. Net debt to OCF measures net debt against operating cash flow before capex. The two come at repayment from different angles, one after capex and net of cash, the other before capex and gross of cash.

**What the combination tells you.** A healthy net-debt-to-OCF ratio backed by a strong FCF-to-total-debt ratio confirms the repayment capacity survives after capital spending. A healthy net-debt ratio with a weak FCF-to-debt ratio means capex is consuming the cash that would otherwise repay the debt. Reading the two together tests whether the repayment capacity holds up once the cost of staying in business is paid.

## Part 8: Limitations

Operating cash flow is volatile, which makes single-year readings unreliable. A working-capital build, a large tax payment, or a one-off settlement can move the bottom number for a year that was otherwise normal. A single distorted year can send the ratio to a level that misrepresents the business. The ratio should be read as a multi-year average and judged against the company's worst cash-flow year, not its best. A company with negative operating cash flow produces a ratio that cannot be read at all, and that happens exactly when the debt risk is highest.

The ratio measures the size of the debt but says nothing about when the debt falls due or what interest it carries. A large but long-dated and cheap debt is far safer than a smaller debt due next year at a high rate, yet the ratio can read them the same. Netting cash against debt assumes the cash is free to repay borrowings, but some cash is trapped in foreign subsidiaries or needed to run the business. The ratio is a strong first read on repayment capacity and a poor final word on debt safety.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio driven by the level of debt or by the level of operating cash flow? Which side of the ratio moved most recently?
2. What was the ratio in the company's worst cash-flow year on the chart? Could the debt have been serviced from that year's operating cash flow?
3. Was the latest operating cash flow distorted by a working-capital swing or a one-off payment? What is the ratio on a normalized cash flow?
4. How does this ratio compare with the net-debt-to-EBITDA version? How much is working capital draining the real cash the business produces?
5. After capital spending is paid, how much free cash flow is actually left to repay debt? Is the operating cash in the ratio truly available?
6. When does the debt fall due, and what interest does it carry? How much of the borrowing must be refinanced within the next few years?
7. How much of the netted cash is genuinely free to repay debt, and how much is trapped abroad or needed to run the business?
8. Can the company cover its interest comfortably, as shown by interest coverage, even though the total debt is large in years of cash flow?
9. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors in the same sector at the same point in the cycle? Is this company more or less indebted than its peers?
10. Has recent borrowing funded acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends? Is management adding debt at a point in the cycle when cash flow could soon weaken?
