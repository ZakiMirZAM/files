# Long Term Debt

## Part 1: Definition

Long-term debt is borrowed money the company must repay beyond one year. Debt is money the company has borrowed and must pay back, usually with interest. Interest is the fee a company pays on borrowed money. Long-term means the repayment date falls more than twelve months ahead.

The line gathers the company's longer-dated borrowings. Bonds and notes are debt the company sells to investors. The line also holds bank loans and drawn credit facilities that the company does not have to repay within the year. A credit facility is an agreed line of borrowing the company can draw on as needed. The portion of any long-term loan that comes due within the next twelve months is moved out of this line and into current liabilities, so long-term debt holds only the part due later. Long-term debt sits on the balance sheet inside long-term liabilities, below total current liabilities. The one question the line answers is how much borrowed money sits in the company's structure for the years ahead.

| Size of the line as a share of total assets | What that size usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The company funds itself with equity and cash rather than borrowing. The useful question is why it borrows so little. |
| Moderate | A common level. The company uses some borrowing alongside equity to fund the business. |
| Large | A debt-heavy structure. Read it next to earnings and interest cost to judge whether the company can carry it. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Long-term debt is not a calculated ratio. Long-term debt is a balance reported directly on the balance sheet. The line is the total of borrowings whose repayment dates fall beyond one year:

`Long-Term Debt = Bonds and notes + Long-term loans + Drawn credit facilities − Portion due within one year`

The first idea is the borrowings themselves. Bonds and notes are debt the company sells to investors, who lend the money and receive interest until the debt matures. Matures means the date the borrowed amount must be repaid. Bank loans and drawn credit facilities work the same way, with the company owing the principal plus interest. Principal is the borrowed amount itself, separate from the interest fee.

The second idea is the split by timing. Any part of a long-term loan due within the next twelve months is reclassified into the current portion of long-term debt inside current liabilities. Long-term debt holds only the remainder due later. A reader who wants the company's full borrowing must add the current portion back, because the two lines together make up the company's total debt.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Long-term debt is the heart of a company's financing risk. The line shows how much of the business is funded by lenders rather than owners. Lenders must be paid interest every year and repaid the principal at maturity, and those claims come before any return to shareholders. A large balance means a large fixed claim that the company must service in good years and bad.

The line is the main building block of leverage measures. Debt To Equity, Debt To Assets, and Net Debt to EBITDA all start from the company's borrowings. Long-term debt is usually the largest part of those borrowings. Interest Coverage tests whether operating income can carry the interest on this debt, and that test sizes the safety of the borrowing. The app passes a company on Interest Coverage at 5.00 and on Debt To Equity at or below 1.00.

The line also sets up the refinancing question. Debt rarely gets repaid from cash alone, because companies usually replace a maturing loan with a new one. Refinancing means replacing an old loan with a new loan. The size of long-term debt, and when it comes due, decides how exposed the company is to higher rates or tighter credit when the time to refinance arrives.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Borrowing and repayment drive the balance directly. Issuing a new bond or drawing a loan raises long-term debt. Repaying a maturity lowers it. Companies often borrow to fund acquisitions, large investments, or share buybacks. Each of these uses lifts the line. A company paying down debt steadily shrinks the line over time.

The passage of time also moves the balance without any new decision. As a loan approaches its final year, its amount shifts out of long-term debt into the current portion within current liabilities, which lowers the long-term line even though total debt has not changed. New borrowing at higher interest rates raises future interest cost without changing the principal on the line. For companies that borrow in foreign currencies, a change in exchange rates can raise or lower the reported balance with no new borrowing at all.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level of long-term debt depends on how steady the company's earnings are. Steadier earnings safely support more debt. Capital-heavy businesses borrow to fund long-lived assets, while capital-light businesses often carry almost none. Compare the line against peers in the same industry.

| Industry type | Typical behavior of the line | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | High and stable | Near-certain revenue safely supports large, long-dated borrowing. |
| Capital-heavy industrials and telecoms | Substantial | Long-lived assets are funded with long-term debt. |
| Capital-light software and services | Near zero | Few large assets to fund, so little need to borrow. |
| Companies owned by buyout firms | Very high | Debt is loaded on deliberately as part of the ownership model. |
| Banks | Not comparable | Borrowing is the raw material of the business, so the usual leverage view does not apply. |

A cross-sector comparison of the raw balance is meaningless. A utility's large debt backed by certain revenue is safer than a smaller debt at a company with swinging earnings. Always read the line within one company over time and against direct competitors, and weight the company's weakest earnings year when judging whether the debt is safe.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows long-term debt as a multi-year series and as a percent of total assets. Read the percent-of-assets line first, because it removes the effect of the company simply growing larger. A flat, low line means borrowing never constrains the business. A flat, high line means management deliberately runs the company with heavy debt. A steady rise as a share of total assets means the company is taking on more leverage, and the reason matters: funding growth is different from funding buybacks or covering shortfalls.

A steady fall usually reflects a deliberate decision to pay down debt, which lowers risk over time. A sharp one-year drop can be a real repayment, or it can be a maturity moving into the current portion of debt, so check current liabilities before reading a fall as deleveraging. A sharp rise often traces to a single acquisition or a large bond issue. Watch for base effects that can fake a trend, such as exchange-rate swings on foreign-currency debt. Always read this line together with earnings and Interest Coverage, because a rising debt balance is only a problem if earnings cannot carry the added interest.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Long-term debt creates the interest the company must pay each year, and Interest Coverage tests whether operating income can cover that interest. The balance shows the size of the borrowing, while the ratio shows whether the company can afford to carry it. A large debt balance is only safe if coverage is comfortable.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising long-term debt balance paired with falling Interest Coverage is a clear warning that the company is borrowing beyond what its earnings can carry. A large debt balance with high and steady coverage means the borrowing is affordable at current earnings, though refinancing risk still remains. Reading the pair separates the amount of the debt from the ability to service it, which are different questions.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt To Equity compares total borrowing with the owners' stake, and long-term debt is usually the largest part of that borrowing. The ratio shows how much of the company is funded by lenders versus owners. Reading the balance alongside the ratio shows whether a change in leverage comes from more debt or from a change in equity.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising long-term debt balance that lifts Debt To Equity above the app's pass level of 1.00 means lenders now fund as much as or more than the owners, which raises financial risk. A Debt To Equity that falls while debt holds steady means equity is growing through retained profit, which strengthens the structure. Reading the pair shows whether the company's financing is tilting toward lenders or toward owners.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net Debt to EBITDA measures total debt, net of cash, in years of earnings. Long-term debt is the main input to that total debt. The balance gives the raw size of the borrowing, while the ratio scales that size against the company's annual earnings power. The two together show both how much is owed and how heavy that load is relative to earnings.

**What the combination tells you.** A large long-term debt balance that still produces a low Net Debt to EBITDA means earnings are strong enough to make the debt manageable. The same balance producing a high ratio means the debt is heavy relative to earnings and exposed to refinancing risk when rates rise. Reading the pair turns the raw debt figure into a judgment about how many years of earnings the borrowing represents.

## Part 8: Limitations

Long-term debt as a single balance hides several things that decide how risky the debt really is. The line shows the amount but not the interest rate, the maturity dates, or the loan terms. A large debt at a low fixed rate with distant maturities is far safer than a smaller debt at a high floating rate due soon, yet both look the same on this one line. The line also excludes the current portion of debt, so a reader must add that back to see the company's full borrowing.

The line can also miss obligations that act like debt. Lease commitments, pension shortfalls, and guarantees can carry debt-like risk. Depending on the accounting, these do not always sit on this line. Foreign-currency debt can move with exchange rates rather than with any borrowing decision. Long-term debt is the principal subject of leverage analysis. The line must be read together with the maturity schedule, the interest cost, and the coverage ratios to judge the real risk.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is long-term debt as a percent of total assets this year, and how has that share moved over the last five years?
2. Once the current portion of debt is added back, what is the company's total borrowing, and how has that total changed?
3. What is the company's Interest Coverage, and could the interest on this debt have been paid from the worst earnings year on the chart?
4. How much of the debt carries a floating rate or comes due within the next few years, and what would interest cost become if it repriced to current rates?
5. Did a fall in long-term debt reflect a real repayment, or a maturity simply moving into the current portion of debt?
6. Was a recent rise in debt used to fund growth, an acquisition, buybacks, dividends, or a cash shortfall?
7. How does long-term debt compare with the owners' equity, and does Debt To Equity sit above or below the app's pass level of 1.00?
8. How heavy is the debt relative to earnings, measured by Net Debt to EBITDA?
9. Does the company carry debt-like obligations, such as leases or pension shortfalls, that sit outside this line?
10. How does the size and maturity profile of the debt compare with direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle?
