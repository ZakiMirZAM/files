# Deferred Income Tax

## Part 1: Definition

Deferred income tax is the part of the year's tax expense that was recorded on the income statement but not actually paid in cash that year. Tax expense is the income tax charge shown on the income statement. Companies often keep two sets of figures: one for the financial reports and one for the tax authorities. The two sets can recognize the same income or cost in different years. That timing gap creates a tax charge that is owed on the books but settled in cash later, or settled earlier. On the cash flow statement this non-cash portion is added to or subtracted from net income in the operating section. The line answers one question: how far did the cash actually paid in tax differ from the tax expense the income statement reported?

The operating section of the cash flow statement starts with net income and adjusts it toward the cash the business produced. Deferred income tax is one of the adjustments. The line is positive when the books charged more tax than the company paid in cash, because the extra charge used no cash. The line is negative when the company paid more cash tax than the books charged.

| Direction of the line | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Positive | Cash tax paid was below the reported tax expense. The gap is added back. |
| Near zero | Reported tax and cash tax moved closely together. |
| Negative | Cash tax paid exceeded the reported tax expense. The gap is subtracted. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The line is reported directly on the cash flow statement. The build is:

`Deferred Income Tax = the change in deferred tax balances for the year`

A deferred tax balance sits on the balance sheet and records tax owed in future years or tax prepaid. When that balance grows or shrinks during the year, the change appears here as a non-cash adjustment. A growing future tax liability adds to the line. A shrinking one subtracts from it.

The most common driver is depreciation. Depreciation is the spreading of an asset's cost over its useful life. Tax rules often allow faster depreciation than the financial books use. Faster tax depreciation lowers cash tax early and raises it later, which builds a deferred balance and produces a positive line in the early years. Tax losses carried forward from past years and changes in the tax rate also move the line.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Deferred income tax reveals whether the tax rate shown on the income statement matches the cash the company really hands over. The reported tax rate can look ordinary while cash tax runs well below it for years. The line measures that gap and turns the tax expense into a cash figure.

A run of positive lines means cash tax has stayed below the book charge. That gap acts like an interest-free delay on part of the tax bill, which leaves more cash in the business for now. A run of negative lines means the delay is reversing and the company is paying back the earlier saving.

The question the line answers matters most for valuation. A model that values a company on its cash flows needs the cash tax figure, not the book provision, when the two diverge for a long time. The line points to where that divergence is happening.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The capital spending cycle is the largest driver. A company investing heavily in equipment builds up faster tax depreciation, which pushes the line positive during the building years. As that spending slows, the early saving reverses and the line turns negative. The line therefore tends to swing with the investment cycle rather than with profit.

Tax losses also move the line. A company using up losses carried forward from past years lowers its cash tax, which feeds the line. A change in the statutory tax rate forces a one-time revaluation of the deferred balances, which can spike the line in a single year for a reason unrelated to operations. Judgments about whether future tax savings will be usable can also shift the balances and the line.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What is normal depends on how capital-intensive the industry is. Heavy investment tends to produce persistent positive lines.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-intensive builders | Persistent positive lines | Heavy equipment spending builds large faster-depreciation gaps. |
| Asset-light services | Small and oscillating | Few long-lived assets means little timing difference. |
| Companies recovering from losses | Swings as losses are used | Carried-forward tax losses lower cash tax until consumed. |
| Stable mature firms | Small oscillations near zero | Investment and depreciation roughly balance year to year. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one company is in a heavy building phase and the other owns few long-lived assets. Compare the line against companies at a similar point in their investment cycle.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the line as a percent of revenue across the years the app shows. Revenue is the common-size base for the cash flow statement. The line usually runs small and switches sign, so the direction of multi-year runs matters more than any single year. A stretch of positive lines means cash tax has stayed below the book charge, often during a heavy investment phase. A stretch of negative lines means that earlier gap is reversing.

A sign change from positive to negative often marks an investment cycle turning, as faster tax depreciation runs out and the saving is repaid. A sharp single-year spike usually signals a tax rate change or a one-time event rather than an operating shift. Bracket those spikes and read the underlying runs to judge whether the company enjoys a durable cash tax advantage.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Tax Burden

**Why read the two together.** Tax Burden measures how much of pre-tax profit the company keeps after the reported tax expense. The reported expense is the book charge. The deferred income tax line shows how much of that charge was non-cash. Reading the two together separates the tax rate on the books from the tax paid in cash.

**What the combination tells you.** A steady Tax Burden alongside persistent positive deferred lines means the company's cash tax rate is lower than its book rate suggests. A reversing deferred line warns that the cash advantage is fading even when the book rate looks unchanged. The pair shows whether reported tax and cash tax tell the same story.

### Income Quality (CFO / NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income Quality compares operating cash flow with net income to judge how cash-backed the profit is. The deferred income tax line is one bridge between those two figures. A large deferred adjustment is part of why operating cash flow can sit above or below net income.

**What the combination tells you.** A high Income Quality reading driven partly by a large positive deferred line means some of the apparent cash strength is a tax timing benefit that can reverse. A reversing deferred line drags Income Quality down as the earlier saving is repaid. The pair shows how much of the cash-versus-profit gap is tax timing.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Capex / Depreciation compares new capital spending with the depreciation being charged. That ratio tracks the investment cycle that drives the timing differences behind deferred tax. The deferred line tends to rise while a company invests faster than it depreciates.

**What the combination tells you.** A high Capex / Depreciation ratio with positive deferred lines confirms a building phase that is lowering cash tax for now. As the ratio falls toward one, the deferred line tends to turn negative and the cash tax saving reverses. The pair links the tax timing to the underlying spending cycle.

## Part 8: Limitations

Deferred income tax nets together several unlike drivers into one figure. A positive line from heavy investment looks the same as a positive line from using up tax losses, yet the two carry different meanings. The full breakdown lives in the tax footnotes of the company's filings, not in this single line.

The line can be distorted by one-time events. A change in the tax rate forces a revaluation that spikes the line without any change in the business. Reading the line in isolation can mislead, so it should be read with the deferred tax balances on the balance sheet and with the investment cycle.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Do the multi-year runs show the deferred balance building or reversing?
2. What does the cash tax rate work out to, taking the reported tax expense and removing this non-cash line?
3. Which drivers move the line, according to the deferred tax breakdown in the filings?
4. Where is the company in its investment cycle, and does that match the direction of the line?
5. Did any single year spike because of a tax rate change rather than operations?
6. How durable is any cash tax advantage, given what is building the deferred balances?
7. Does the valuation use book taxes or cash taxes, and does the gap between them matter over the long run?
8. How much future tax would reverse if the building phase ended and the timing gaps unwound?
9. How much of the line comes from using up tax losses carried forward from past years?
10. Does a sign change in the line line up with a turn in the company's investment cycle?
