# EV To EBITDA

## Part 1: Definition

The EV to EBITDA ratio measures how much it costs to buy a company's whole operating business compared with the cash earnings that business produces in a year. EV stands for enterprise value, which is the price to buy the entire company including its debt and after removing its cash. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest and taxes and before depreciation and amortization. EBITDA is a measure of operating profit before financing and accounting deductions. The ratio answers one question: how many years of operating cash earnings does the full purchase price represent?

A ratio of 8 means the buyer pays eight dollars of enterprise value for every dollar of yearly operating earnings. A ratio of 16 means the buyer pays twice as much, usually because the market expects the earnings to grow. A low ratio can mean a bargain or a business the market expects to weaken. The ratio measures the whole company rather than just the shares, so it includes the cost of taking on the company's debt.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 6 | The market pays little for operating earnings. This can signal a bargain, slow growth, or a business the market expects to decline. |
| 6 to 10 | A common range for established companies with steady growth. |
| 10 to 15 | The market expects above-average growth or treats the earnings as unusually safe. |
| Above 15 | The market expects rapid growth. The price depends heavily on that growth arriving. |
| Negative | EBITDA is negative, so the ratio cannot be computed and carries no meaning. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`EV To EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA`

Enterprise value is the top number. Enterprise value is the market value of the company's shares, plus its total debt, minus its cash. Debt is added because a buyer who takes over the company must repay or assume that debt, so the debt is part of the true purchase cost. Cash is subtracted because the buyer gets the company's cash and can use it to offset the price. The result is the cost of owning the operating business itself, free of how it happens to be financed.

EBITDA is the bottom number. EBITDA is operating profit with depreciation and amortization added back. Depreciation and amortization are non-cash charges that spread the cost of buildings, equipment, and intangible assets over many years. Adding them back, and measuring profit before interest and taxes, produces a figure close to the cash the operating business throws off. Cash is left out of enterprise value precisely because the interest that cash earns is not counted inside EBITDA, so keeping the cash in would overstate the ratio.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The price to earnings ratio values only the shares against net profit, which is measured after interest and taxes. EV to EBITDA values the whole company against profit measured before financing. The difference matters because it lets an investor compare companies that carry very different amounts of debt. The top number includes the debt and the bottom number sits above interest, so two companies with the same operations but different borrowing can still be compared fairly.

The ratio has three further strengths that draw analysts to it. Fewer companies report negative EBITDA than report negative net profit, so the ratio can be computed for more firms, including many that the price to earnings ratio cannot value. Differences in depreciation method between companies change reported profit but do not change EBITDA, so the comparison is cleaner. The ratio is especially useful for companies that spend heavily on long-lived assets, such as telecom networks and toll roads, where depreciation distorts net profit.

The ratio answers a question the equity-only ratios do not: relative to the cash the operations produce, what does it cost to own this entire business, debt and all? The whole-company view is the standard tool when one company is being valued for purchase against another.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move a justified ratio. A higher expected growth rate raises it, because buyers pay more for earnings that will grow. A lower cost of capital raises it, because future earnings are discounted less heavily. Cost of capital is the return investors require for the risk of the business. A lower tax rate raises it, because more of the operating profit survives to reach owners. Heavy reinvestment needs lower it, because earnings that must be plowed back into equipment are not free for owners.

The depreciation share of EBITDA also moves the ratio. A company whose EBITDA comes largely from adding back depreciation is less cash-rich than the headline suggests. Such a company should trade at a lower multiple. On the price side, market sentiment and interest rates shift enterprise value the same way they shift any price. A change in debt or cash also moves enterprise value directly, so a large debt repayment or a cash buildup changes the ratio even when the operating business is unchanged.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

A normal ratio depends on a sector's growth, its reinvestment needs, and how much its profit relies on depreciation add-backs.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-heavy infrastructure, such as telecom and toll roads | Lower headline, often 6 to 9 | Heavy depreciation and large reinvestment hold the multiple down despite stable earnings. |
| Established industrials | Moderate, often 7 to 11 | Solid earnings that move with the economy. |
| High-growth software | High, often 15 and up | The market prices in fast growth and the businesses need little physical reinvestment. |
| Cyclical producers, such as miners | Misleading at the peak | Peak EBITDA makes the ratio look low just before earnings fall, so judge across a full cycle. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | Interest and the structure of these firms make EBITDA meaningless for them. |

Compare the ratio only against companies in the same sector with similar growth and reinvestment needs. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless, because a capital-light software firm and a capital-heavy network operator face entirely different reinvestment burdens that the ratio cannot reconcile.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A rising ratio over several years means the market is paying more for each dollar of operating earnings, usually because growth expectations are improving or the business is seen as safer. A falling ratio means the market is paying less, which can reflect fading growth hopes or rising risk. The trend mixes changing expectations with changing fundamentals, so a moving ratio always raises the question of whether enterprise value or EBITDA drove the change.

A sudden spike often comes from a drop in EBITDA rather than a jump in price, because a shrinking denominator inflates the ratio. A cyclical company shows the reverse trap, where the ratio looks lowest at the peak of the cycle just before earnings fall. Read the ratio against the EBITDA line and against the company's debt. A ratio that falls because debt was repaid, which lowers enterprise value, is healthier than a ratio that falls because the share price collapsed. Watching enterprise value and EBITDA separately keeps the trend honest.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### P/E

**Why read the two together.** The price to earnings ratio values only the equity against net profit, while EV to EBITDA values the whole company against operating profit. The price to earnings ratio is shaped by debt and tax, which EV to EBITDA strips out. Reading the two together shows whether the equity price tag and the whole-company price tag agree. A wide gap between them usually points to heavy debt or an unusual tax position.

**What the combination tells you.** A high price to earnings ratio next to a normal EV to EBITDA often means debt and tax are depressing net profit and inflating the equity ratio. A low price to earnings ratio next to a high EV to EBITDA can mean the equity looks cheap only because heavy debt magnifies thin profit. When both ratios agree, the valuation signal is more reliable. The pair separates a genuinely cheap business from a cheap-looking but heavily indebted one.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** EV to EBITDA prices the whole company, debt included, against its operating earnings. Net Debt to EBITDA measures how large that debt is in years of the same earnings. The first metric tells the price of the business and the second tells how much of that price is debt the buyer must shoulder. Reading both shows how much of the enterprise value is equity and how much is borrowed money.

**What the combination tells you.** A moderate EV to EBITDA paired with a high Net Debt to EBITDA means much of the purchase cost is debt, which raises the risk for the equity owner. A similar EV to EBITDA paired with low debt means the value rests mostly on the shares, which is safer. When debt is high, a small fall in earnings can swing the equity value sharply even if enterprise value barely moves. The pair shows how leverage sits inside the whole-company price.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** EBITDA sits above the spending a business needs on equipment and the taxes it owes, so EBITDA overstates the cash that actually reaches owners. Free cash flow is the cash left after that spending and those taxes. Comparing the two shows how much of the headline EBITDA survives as real cash. A wide gap warns that the ratio flatters a capital-hungry business.

**What the combination tells you.** A low EV to EBITDA can still be expensive if free cash flow is far below EBITDA, because heavy equipment spending consumes most of the earnings. A higher EV to EBITDA can be fair if nearly all of the EBITDA converts to free cash flow. Watching the gap over time shows whether rising reinvestment is quietly draining the cash behind the headline. The pair corrects the main blind spot of measuring value against EBITDA.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** EBITDA adds back depreciation, which assumes the spending that depreciation represents does not need to be repeated. Capex to depreciation tests that assumption by comparing actual equipment spending with the depreciation charge. A ratio well above one means the company spends more than it depreciates, so the EBITDA add-back overstates true cash earnings. Reading both reveals how much the EV to EBITDA ratio flatters the business.

**What the combination tells you.** A low EV to EBITDA paired with capex far above depreciation means the cheap-looking multiple hides heavy reinvestment, so the true value is worse than the ratio suggests. Capex near or below depreciation means the add-back is fair and the multiple can be trusted. A rising capex-to-depreciation ratio over time warns that reinvestment is climbing faster than the depreciation charge admits. The pair shows whether the depreciation add-back inside EBITDA is honest.

## Part 8: Limitations

The biggest weakness is that EBITDA ignores the spending a business needs to survive. Adding back depreciation treats equipment as if it never wears out and never needs replacing, yet most companies must keep spending to maintain current earnings. EBITDA also sits above taxes and interest, which are real cash costs the owner cannot escape. A company can show a low, attractive ratio while almost none of its EBITDA reaches owners as cash, which is why the ratio flatters capital-heavy businesses most.

Several measurement problems also reduce comparability. Enterprise value is hard to pin down for companies that own stakes in other firms, because the share of earnings and the share of value do not always line up, which can make the ratio too high or too low. Companies define EBITDA differently, and some add back costs that are really recurring, which inflates the denominator and lowers the ratio. The ratio cannot be computed when EBITDA is negative. EBITDA is a measure of operating earnings, not of cash in hand, so the ratio is a useful whole-company price tag and a poor measure of what owners actually keep.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio built on normal EBITDA, or on a figure padded with add-backs of costs that actually recur each year?
2. How much of the company's EBITDA is just depreciation added back, and how much equipment spending does the company truly need to maintain its earnings?
3. How does free cash flow compare with EBITDA? How much of the headline earnings survives after equipment spending and taxes?
4. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors that have similar growth and reinvestment needs? Is the company cheaper or more expensive, and why?
5. How much of the enterprise value is debt rather than equity? Would a small drop in earnings swing the equity value sharply?
6. Is the company cyclical? If so, is the low ratio a sign of value or a peak-earnings trap just before earnings fall?
7. Does the company own stakes in other firms that could distort enterprise value relative to the EBITDA it reports?
8. Does the equity price tag from the price to earnings ratio agree with this whole-company view, or does a gap point to debt or tax effects?
9. How has the ratio moved over several years? Was the change driven by the share price, by debt changes, or by EBITDA?
10. Does the growth the high ratio implies look achievable? Judge it against the company's history, its reinvestment needs, and its market position.
