# Cash At Beginning And End Of Period

## Part 1: Definition

Cash at beginning and end of period reports the two cash balances that bracket the cash flow statement. The opening balance is the cash the company held on the first day of the year. The closing balance is the cash the company held on the last day of the year. The cash flow statement is the report that tracks every dollar of cash moving in and out during the year. These two balances are the start point and the end point of that report. The line answers one question: how much cash did the company begin the year with, and how much did it finish with?

The two balances connect the cash flow statement to the balance sheet. The balance sheet is the report that lists what a company owns and owes at one moment in time. The closing cash balance here must equal the Cash And Cash Equivalents line on the balance sheet for the same date. Cash And Cash Equivalents is the company's cash plus near-cash holdings that can be turned into cash quickly. The opening balance here equals last year's closing balance. The cash flow statement bridges the two.

Common readings of these balances:

| Reading | What that reading usually means |
|---|---|
| Closing higher than opening | The company added cash during the year. The net change in cash was positive. |
| Closing lower than opening | The company used cash during the year. The net change in cash was negative. |
| Both balances large relative to the company | The company keeps a thick cash buffer, which adds safety and reduces reliance on lenders. |
| Both balances thin relative to the company | The company runs lean on cash and may depend on steady cash inflows or available credit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

These balances are reported, not computed from a ratio. The relationship that links them is:

`Cash at End = Cash at Beginning + Net Change In Cash`

The opening balance is simply last year's closing balance carried forward. No calculation is needed. The company held a certain amount of cash on the first day of the year, and that figure becomes the start point of the statement.

The closing balance is the opening balance plus the net change in cash. Net change in cash is the total of the three cash flow sections plus the currency adjustment. The three sections are operating, investing, and financing activities. The currency adjustment is the effect of foreign exchange changes on cash. Adding that total to the opening balance gives the closing balance. The closing balance then must tie exactly to the Cash And Cash Equivalents line on the balance sheet, which is the check that the statement is complete and correct.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The two balances are the proof that the cash flow statement reconciles. The cash flow statement begins with the opening cash balance and ends with the closing balance. If the closing balance does not match the Cash And Cash Equivalents line on the balance sheet, the statement does not tie out. An investor uses the match as a basic check that the cash figures across the statements are consistent.

The size of the balances matters on its own. A company carrying a large cash balance has a buffer against bad years and does not need to borrow at the first sign of trouble. A company carrying a thin cash balance depends on steady cash coming in or on an open line of credit. The closing balance tells the reader how much room the company has if cash inflows pause.

The two balances also frame the whole year's cash story. The opening balance shows where the company started. The closing balance shows where it ended. Every cash flow in the three sections explains the move from one to the other. The reader can see the year's cash result at a glance before reading the detail.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The opening balance is fixed by history. The opening balance is whatever the company held at the end of the prior year, so nothing in the current year can change it. The closing balance moves with everything that happened in the year. Cash earned from operations lifts it. Cash spent on equipment and acquisitions lowers it. Cash from borrowing or issuing stock lifts it. Debt repayment, dividends, and buybacks lower it.

The forex effect also moves the closing balance. A company holding foreign cash sees its closing balance restated as exchange rates move, even though no cash entered or left. The closing balance therefore reflects both real cash flow and currency translation. A balance that grew may have grown partly because foreign currencies rose rather than because the business earned cash.

Management choices shape the level too. Some companies deliberately hold a large cash cushion as a policy. Others sweep cash out to pay down debt or return it to shareholders, keeping the balance thin on purpose. A high or low closing balance can reflect strategy as much as performance, so the level alone does not signal strength or weakness.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The right cash level depends on how steady and predictable the company's cash inflows are. A company with steady, predictable cash can run a thinner balance safely. A company with lumpy or uncertain cash needs a thicker cushion.

| Company type | Typical cash level | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Steady, predictable cash generators | Can run thinner | Reliable inflows reduce the need for a large buffer. |
| Cyclical companies | Hold larger buffers | Cash inflows can fall sharply in downturns, so a cushion is needed. |
| Fast-growing or acquisitive companies | Often large then drawn down | They raise cash, then spend it on growth and deals. |
| Capital-light software companies | Often very large | They generate cash fast and spend little, so balances build up. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Cash is part of the product, so the balance does not read like an industrial company's. |

A cross-company comparison of cash balances alone is meaningless. The right level depends on the business, the cycle, and management policy. Compare a company's cash balance against its own history and its own obligations, not against an unrelated company's balance.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows the closing cash balance as a multi-year series and as a percent of revenue in the vertical common-size table. Vertical common-size analysis restates each line as a percent of revenue so different years can be compared on the same scale. Cash as a percent of revenue shows how thick the company's buffer is relative to its size, which is more comparable across years than the raw dollar balance.

The shape of the series tells the story. A closing balance that rises steadily as a percent of revenue means the company is building a cushion, which adds safety but can also signal that management has not found good uses for the cash. A balance that falls steadily means the company is drawing the cushion down, which is fine if it funded investment or debt repayment and worrying if operations drained it. A balance that swings widely points to a cyclical business or to lumpy acquisitions and financing. The base effect to watch is revenue. A drop in revenue can make a flat cash balance look larger as a percent of revenue without any real change in the cushion. A single year inflated by a bond issue or drained by a large acquisition can break the trend, so a one-off year should not be read as a direction.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Change In Cash

**Why read the two together.** Net change in cash is exactly the gap between the opening and closing balances. The opening and closing balances show where the company started and ended, while net change in cash shows the size of the move between them. Reading them together connects the year's cash result to the balances that frame it.

**What the combination tells you.** A large closing balance reached through a positive net change shows the company added to an already healthy buffer. A falling closing balance driven by a negative net change shows the buffer is shrinking, and the three sections behind the net change reveal why. Watching both over the years shows whether the company is building or draining its cash position.

### Cash Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Cash per share is the company's cash balance divided by the number of shares. The closing balance is the numerator of that figure. Reading the two together translates the total cash balance into a per-share amount the shareholder can weigh against the share price.

**What the combination tells you.** A large and rising closing balance that lifts cash per share means each share is backed by more cash, which adds a floor under the share's value. A closing balance that grows only because the share count fell tells a different story than one that grows because the company earned cash. The pair shows whether the cash cushion is growing for the shareholder or just being divided among fewer shares.

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio compares a company's short-term assets to its short-term obligations. Cash is the strongest of those short-term assets, and the closing balance is the cash part of it. Reading the two together shows how much of the company's short-term safety rests on cash specifically.

**What the combination tells you.** A healthy current ratio backed by a large cash balance is safer than the same ratio backed mostly by inventory or receivables that may not convert to cash on time. A thin cash balance with an acceptable current ratio warns that the company's short-term safety depends on collecting or selling other assets. The pair shows the quality of the company's short-term cushion, not just its size.

## Part 8: Limitations

The cash balance is a snapshot at one instant and can be managed for the reporting date. A company can draw down debt or delay payments right before year end to show a larger closing balance, then reverse it days later. The single balance says nothing about how the cash moved during the year, which is why the balance must be read together with the cash flow sections that explain the change.

A large cash balance is not automatically good, and a small one is not automatically bad. A high balance can mean a strong, self-funding company, or it can mean management is sitting on cash it cannot deploy well. A low balance can mean a disciplined company that returns cash to owners, or one that is running dangerously lean. The balance also mixes real cash flow with currency translation when foreign cash is held. The level alone is a starting point, not a verdict.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Does the closing cash balance match the Cash And Cash Equivalents line on the balance sheet for the same date?
2. Is the closing balance higher or lower than the opening balance, and which section of the cash flow statement drove the move?
3. How does the cash balance look as a percent of revenue across the years on the chart?
4. Is a rising cash balance the result of earned cash, or of borrowing and issuing stock?
5. Does the company hold a buffer that fits the volatility of its cash inflows, or does it run thinner than its cycle warrants?
6. Was the closing balance inflated by a year-end draw on debt or by delayed payments?
7. How much of the closing balance reflects the forex effect on foreign cash rather than real cash flow?
8. Does a large cash balance reflect strong cash generation, or cash that management has been unable to deploy?
9. Did a fall in revenue make the cash balance look larger as a percent of revenue without a real change in the cushion?
10. How does the company's cash balance compare with its near-term debt maturities and other obligations?
