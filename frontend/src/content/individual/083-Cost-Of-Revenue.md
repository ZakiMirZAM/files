# Cost Of Revenue

## Part 1: Definition

Cost of revenue is the direct cost of making the goods sold or delivering the services sold during the period. Direct cost means cost that rises and falls with each unit produced, such as raw materials and the factory labor used to make the product. The line is also called cost of sales, cost of goods sold, or its short form COGS. Cost of revenue answers one question: how much did it cost the company to produce the exact goods and services it sold?

In the vertical analysis table this app shows, cost of revenue appears as a percentage of revenue. A reading of 60 percent means the direct cost of the product ate sixty cents of every revenue dollar, leaving forty cents of gross profit before any other expense. Cost of revenue is usually the single largest cost on the income statement, so its size and its trend set how much profit the company has to work with.

Common readings of the cost of revenue line:

| Cost of revenue as a share of revenue | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Low, well below half of revenue | The product carries a large markup over its direct cost, which is a sign of pricing power or a light-cost product. |
| Around half to two thirds of revenue | A normal range for many manufacturers and retailers. The remaining margin must still cover all other expenses. |
| High, close to revenue | The product barely sells for more than it costs to make. Little room is left for other expenses or profit. |
| Rising as a share of revenue over time | Input costs are climbing or prices are slipping. A negative trend that squeezes profit from the top down. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Cost of revenue is the sum of the direct costs tied to the products and services actually sold:

`Cost Of Revenue = Direct Materials + Direct Labor + Other Direct Production Cost`

Direct materials are the physical inputs that go into the product, such as the sugar in a drink or the steel in a machine. Direct labor is the pay for the workers who make the product on the line. Other direct production cost covers items such as the factory power and the depreciation of the machines used in production, where depreciation is the yearly charge that spreads an asset's cost over its useful life. The line includes only the cost of the units that were sold. The cost of unsold units sits in inventory on the balance sheet until those units sell.

The defining feature of this line is that the cost moves with each unit. When sales rise, cost of revenue rises with them, because more units need more materials and more labor. A drinks company with direct cost of fifty-five cents per can reports five dollars and fifty cents of cost of revenue when it sells ten cans. A company that simply buys finished goods and resells them reports its purchase cost here. The exact items inside the line are set by the company's accounting policy, which the company is required to disclose in its annual report.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Cost of revenue measures how efficiently the company turns inputs into the product it sells. The line sits directly under revenue, and the gap between the two is gross profit, the first measure of whether the core business makes money. A company that cannot keep its direct cost well below its selling price will struggle to ever earn a real profit, because every expense further down the statement still has to be paid.

The line also reveals what the company is exposed to. A drinks maker depends heavily on sugar, so a jump in the sugar price flows straight into cost of revenue and squeezes profit. Reading the line tells an investor which raw materials and which labor markets can make or break the company's margin. A business whose direct costs are stable and well controlled is far easier to forecast than one whose costs swing with commodity prices.

Watching the trend in cost of revenue against revenue is the heart of judging operating risk. If sales stay flat while cost of revenue keeps climbing, profit is quietly draining away. Spotting that drift early is the point of the analysis.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The main driver is the cost of inputs. Higher prices for raw materials, energy, or factory labor push the line up. A company that buys a commodity input has little control over that price, so its cost of revenue swings with the commodity market. Volume also moves the line, because more units sold means proportionally more direct cost, though a larger company can sometimes buy materials more cheaply in bulk and lower its cost per unit.

Several accounting choices move the line without the underlying cost changing. The method a company uses to value inventory affects which costs land in cost of revenue when prices are rising or falling. Companies also differ in what they classify as direct versus other operating cost, so two similar firms can split the same total cost differently. Where a company puts depreciation matters too, since some report production depreciation inside cost of revenue and others report it lower down. A company can also shift cost out of this line and into operating expenses, which flatters gross margin while leaving total profit unchanged.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a high or low cost of revenue depends entirely on the business model.

| Industry type | Typical cost of revenue behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and digital services | Very low share of revenue | Copying software costs almost nothing, so most of the revenue dollar survives as gross profit. |
| Manufacturers and consumer goods | Moderate to high share of revenue | Physical materials and factory labor consume a large part of each sales dollar. |
| Grocery and discount retail | Very high share of revenue | The business resells goods at a thin markup and makes money on volume, not margin. |
| Commodity producers | Driven by input and energy prices | Direct cost moves with the price of the raw material, which the company does not control. |
| Banks and insurers | Not a standard cost of revenue line | The main costs are interest and claims, not the production cost of a physical product. |

Comparing cost of revenue as a share of revenue across two different industries is misleading, because each model carries a different natural level. The comparison is only meaningful between direct competitors that make a similar product the same way.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows cost of revenue as a multi-year line and as a percent of revenue in the vertical table. Reading the percent-of-revenue line is the more useful view, because it strips out the effect of the business simply getting bigger. A flat percent-of-revenue line means the company is holding its direct costs in step with its prices, which is a sign of a stable, well-run operation. A line that drifts up as a share of revenue means input costs are rising faster than prices, or the company is discounting to keep volume. A multi-year rise in this share is a slow squeeze on profit that is hard to reverse.

A line that falls as a share of revenue means the company is gaining efficiency or pricing power, which is the pattern an investor wants. Watch for base effects that fake a trend. A spike in a commodity input can lift the line for one year and then fall back, so a single bad year is not the same as a lasting decline. Cross-check the raw cost line against revenue. If revenue is flat while the cost line keeps rising, the percent-of-revenue reading confirms a real problem rather than just growth.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Cost of revenue and gross margin are two sides of the same gap. Gross margin is the share of revenue left after cost of revenue, so when one rises as a share of revenue the other falls. Reading the two together turns the cost figure into the profit figure that survives the first round of expenses. The cost line shows the burden, and gross margin shows what remains.

**What the combination tells you.** A stable cost of revenue share and a stable gross margin together confirm a steady, predictable core business. A rising cost share with a falling gross margin shows a real squeeze, and the cost line tells whether the cause is materials, labor, or another input. A falling cost share with a rising gross margin signals improving efficiency or stronger pricing. Watching both lines over the years shows whether the company's core profitability is widening or narrowing.

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Cost of revenue should move with revenue, because more units sold need more direct cost. Reading the two lines together shows whether cost is growing only in proportion to sales or faster than sales. A direct cost that outruns revenue is the early sign of trouble in the core business.

**What the combination tells you.** Revenue and cost of revenue growing at the same rate means the business is scaling cleanly without losing efficiency. Cost of revenue growing faster than revenue means each new sale is becoming more expensive to fulfill, which thins the margin. Revenue flat while cost of revenue rises is the clearest warning of a margin squeeze. The pair shows whether growth is healthy or whether the company is running harder just to stand still.

### COGS / Inventory

**Why read the two together.** Cost of revenue is the cost of the goods that were sold, and inventory is the cost of the goods still waiting to sell. The ratio of the two measures how many times the company sold and replaced its stock during the year. Reading them together connects the income statement cost line to the balance sheet stock it draws from.

**What the combination tells you.** A high turnover of inventory against cost of revenue means goods move quickly and little cash is tied up in stock. A falling turnover means inventory is building faster than it sells, which can signal weak demand or goods at risk of becoming obsolete. Inventory growing much faster than cost of revenue is a warning that future write-downs may be coming. The pair links how cheaply the company produces with how efficiently it manages what it has produced.

## Part 8: Limitations

Cost of revenue depends on where the company draws the line between direct and other operating cost, and companies draw that line differently. A firm can move depreciation, freight, or part of its labor between cost of revenue and operating expenses, which changes gross margin while leaving total profit untouched. The inventory valuation method also shifts which costs land in the line when prices change, so two identical businesses can report different cost of revenue. These choices make the line treacherous to compare across companies without checking the accounting policy.

The line also hides its own composition. A single percentage does not say whether a rise came from materials, labor, energy, or a shift in product mix. The cause matters because some pressures are temporary and others are permanent. Cost of revenue says nothing about the expenses below it, so a company with a low cost of revenue can still lose money once marketing, research, and overhead are paid. The line is the best first measure of production efficiency and a poor measure of overall profitability.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is driving the current cost of revenue as a share of revenue: input prices, product mix, labor cost, or production efficiency? Which of those is likely to persist?
2. Which raw materials or commodities make up the largest part of the direct cost? How much would the margin move if that input price rose sharply?
3. Has the cost of revenue share trended up or down over the years on the chart? Does the trend track revenue or run independently of it?
4. Does the company include production depreciation and freight inside cost of revenue, or report them lower down? How does that choice compare with direct competitors?
5. What inventory valuation method does the company use, and how does that method affect the reported cost when input prices are rising?
6. Has any cost recently been reclassified between cost of revenue and operating expenses? Did total profit actually change, or only the gross margin?
7. How does the cost of revenue share compare with the closest direct competitor making a similar product? What explains any gap?
8. Is inventory growing faster than cost of revenue? Could that signal weak demand or a coming write-down?
9. How did cost of revenue behave in years when a key input price spiked? How quickly did the company pass the higher cost through to its own prices?
10. Does the company have long-term supply contracts or hedges that fix its input costs? When do those protections expire?
