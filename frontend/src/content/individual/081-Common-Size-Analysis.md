# Common Size Analysis

## Part 1: Definition

Common size analysis restates every line of a financial statement as a percentage of one base figure. The method is also called vertical analysis. A base figure is the single line that every other line is divided by. The income statement and cash flow statement use revenue as the base. The balance sheet uses total assets as the base. The method answers one question: what share of the whole does each line take?

Restating the statements as percentages removes the effect of size. A large company and a small company can then be compared directly, because both are measured on the same scale of one hundred percent. The percentages show the structure of the business. They show what share of each sales dollar becomes profit, and what fraction of the asset base sits in each form.

Common readings of a common-size line:

| What you see | What that pattern usually means |
|---|---|
| A cost line holds a steady share of revenue for years | The cost moves in step with sales. The business model is stable on that line. |
| A cost line rises as a share of revenue | Costs are growing faster than sales. Margin is being squeezed on that line. |
| A cost line falls as a share of revenue | The company is gaining cost discipline or scale on that line. |
| An asset line grows as a share of total assets | The business is shifting more of its resources into that asset type. |
| A single line spikes in one year only | A one-time event, not a change in structure. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The method is one division repeated across every line. The formula for any line is:

`Common Size Percent = Line Item ÷ Base Figure`

Building the common-size income statement is the first step. Each line from cost of revenue down to net income is divided by the period's revenue. A cost of revenue of 31 percent means thirty-one cents of each sales dollar pays for the product. The gross profit line below it then reads 69 percent. The same division continues through the operating expenses, the operating income, and the net income at the bottom. Each line shows one stage on the path from a sales dollar to a profit dollar.

Building the common-size cash flow statement is the second step. This app uses the same revenue base for the cash flow statement. Each source and use of cash is divided by revenue. A capex line of 8 percent means eight cents of each revenue dollar is reinvested in equipment and buildings. Capex is short for capital expenditures, the money spent on long-lived assets.

Building the common-size balance sheet is the third step. The base changes to total assets. Each asset line is divided by total assets and shows its share of everything the company owns. Each liability and equity line is also divided by total assets and shows how the assets are funded. The asset side sums to one hundred percent. The funding side sums to one hundred percent as well.

The tables can be read two ways. Reading down one column shows a single year's full structure. Reading across one row shows one line's history over the years the app displays.

## Part 2b: Every input, defined

The income statement base is revenue. Revenue is the total value of goods and services sold in the period. Revenue is chosen because it is the activity measure for the income statement. Every line on the income statement is a result of making sales. Dividing each line by revenue answers the operating question: what does each dollar of sales cost, and what does each dollar of sales yield? The choice makes margin analysis automatic. The gross profit line is the gross margin. The operating income line is the operating margin. The net income line is the net margin.

The cash flow statement base is also revenue in this app. Keeping the cash flow statement in the same units as the income statement creates a useful alignment. The net income line appears in both statements at the same percentage. The gap between any income line and its cash counterpart can then be read directly as a difference in percentage points of revenue.

The balance sheet base is total assets. Total assets is the sum of everything the company owns. Total assets is chosen because the balance sheet describes a stock of resources held at a point in time, not a flow of activity over a period. The question for the balance sheet is composition. Total assets as the base makes the funding structure easy to read. The total debt line becomes Debt To Assets. The equity line is its complement.

The time span is the final input. The app shows the common-size tables across many years. A long span is needed because it contains full business cycles. A long span also separates a lasting structural change from a single unusual year that a one-period view would mistake for structure.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Common size analysis answers the question that every ratio eventually raises: which line moved? The margins, the quality checks, and the leverage ratios each summarize a relationship between two lines. When one of those ratios changes, the next step is finding the cause in the underlying line. The common-size tables show exactly that.

The method enables two comparisons that raw dollar figures block. The first is a comparison across companies of very different size. A giant and a young rival in the same industry have directly comparable structures once both are stated in percentages. The second is a comparison across time within one company. Growth no longer hides a slow drift. A cost line creeping from 12 percent of revenue to 15 percent is visible no matter how fast revenue itself grew.

The method is also the foundation for spotting accounting tricks. Reclassifying a cost from one statement section to another shows up as one row falling while another rises. Parking expenses or stretching out a charge shows up as a row bending without a business reason. The common-size view makes those shifts plain.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Every change in the business moves some line, which is the point of the method. The work is sorting which mover matters. A shift in product mix moves whole blocks of lines at once. A company tilting toward services will show its cost structure, its margins, and its working capital lines all bending together.

Business cycles move the operating lines in a rhythm. Margins widen in strong demand and narrow in weak demand. One-time events spike a single line in a single year. A writedown, a legal charge, or the sale of a division each appears as an isolated jump that the surrounding years frame.

Accounting choices move lines without any change in economics. Shifting a cost between cost of revenue and operating expenses is one example. Recording a cost as an asset instead of an expense is another. Acquisitions reshape entire columns at once, because the acquired company's lines are added to the buyer's from the deal date forward.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The percentage structure is a picture of the business model. Each sector has a typical profile that the tables let a reader recognize.

| Industry type | Typical structural profile | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software | Thin cost of revenue, heavy research and sales shares, deferred revenue large on the funding side | The product costs little to copy and sell, while building and marketing it costs a great deal |
| Retail | Fat cost of revenue, thin margins, inventory and payables large | The business buys goods and resells them at a small markup |
| Manufacturing | Plant and equipment heavy on the asset side, moderate margins | The business converts materials using costly long-lived assets |
| Utilities | Very large plant share funded by a very large debt share | Regulated networks require huge fixed assets financed mostly with borrowing |
| Banks and insurers | Standard form does not apply | The balance sheet is the business itself, so it needs its own layout |

The cross-company use is a comparison of models. Two competitors' structures placed side by side show where one spends differently from the other. The within-sector skill is knowing the normal profile well enough that any deviation stands out at once.

## Part 5b: When this method applies and when it fails

Common size analysis applies to every company that has financial statements. The method is strongest in two situations. The first is comparing companies of different sizes in the same industry, because the percentages put both on the same scale. The second is spotting margin and cost-structure shifts within one company over time, because a drifting line is visible even while the dollars grow.

The method has real weaknesses. The first weakness appears when the base itself swings. A sharp drop in revenue makes every income statement cost line look bloated, even when the dollar cost did not change. The crisis-year percentages must be read with care. The second weakness is comparability across companies that present revenue differently. A company that reports revenue gross and a company that reports it net will show different cost shares for the same underlying business.

The largest weakness is that percentages hide absolute size. A company can hold a perfect structure while the business shrinks. Stable shares on a falling revenue base are structurally identical and economically far worse. The dollar charts shown beside the tables are the necessary companion. The percentages show the shape of the business. The dollar figures show the size.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The multi-year tables read as the structural biography of the company, and a careful reading follows a sequence. The first pass is a stability scan. Note which lines hold steady and which lines drift. The steady lines are the settled model. The drifting lines are the story.

The second pass looks for coordinated drift. A block of lines moving together marks a strategy change, a mix shift, or a model change. A shift toward services leaves a recognizable pattern across all three statements. The third pass isolates the anomaly years. A single column where several lines spike usually marks a one-time event that belongs outside any trend reading.

The fourth pass is the cross-statement check. An improvement on the income statement should echo in the cash flow lines. A change in balance sheet structure should reconcile with the financing lines. The three tables are one document. Their internal agreement is the deepest check the method offers, and a break in that agreement is a warning sign.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross Margin is the gross profit line of the common-size income statement read on its own. The common-size table places that single line in the full context of the lines above and below it. The cost of revenue line directly above gross profit is the exact complement of the margin. Reading the table shows what is driving the margin, not just the margin's level.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling gross margin in the table points straight to a rising cost of revenue share, because the two always sum to one hundred percent of revenue. The combination shows whether a margin change comes from pricing or from cost. A margin that holds while lower lines drift tells the analyst the pressure is below the gross line, in operating costs rather than in the core product economics. The table turns the single Gross Margin number into a full diagnosis.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT Margin is the operating income line of the common-size income statement. EBIT means earnings before interest and taxes, the profit from normal operations. The expense lines stacked above the operating income line in the table are the complete breakdown of what stands between gross profit and operating profit. The table shows every component that the single margin summarizes.

**What the combination tells you.** A shrinking EBIT Margin can be traced in the table to the exact expense share that grew. Rising sales costs, rising research costs, and rising administrative costs each leave a different signature in the rows. The combination separates an operating problem from a gross-margin problem, because the table shows whether the gross line held while the expense lines climbed. Reading the two together turns a margin decline into a named cause.

### Net Margin

**Why read the two together.** Net Margin is the net income line of the common-size income statement, the share of each revenue dollar that survives to the bottom. The table shows every layer that the dollar passes through on the way down. Taxes, interest, and one-time items all sit as their own lines between operating income and net income. The full path from sales dollar to profit dollar is visible at once.

**What the combination tells you.** A gap between a steady EBIT Margin and a falling Net Margin points to the lines below operating income, usually interest or tax. The combination shows whether weak profit comes from the operations or from the financing and tax structure beneath them. A net margin that moves more than the operating margin signals that the difference lives in those lower lines. The table tells the analyst which layer drained the profit.

### Operating Expenses

**Why read the two together.** Operating Expenses are the costs of running the business that sit below cost of revenue, such as sales, research, and administration. The common-size table states each operating expense as its own share of revenue. Reading the expense block alongside the revenue base shows how much of each sales dollar is spent on running the business rather than on making the product. The block sits directly between the gross profit line and the operating income line.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising operating expense share, against a steady revenue base, shows that overhead is growing faster than sales. The combination reveals whether a company gains operating leverage as it grows, meaning its expense shares fall while revenue rises. Falling expense shares over time are a sign of scale benefits reaching the bottom line. Rising expense shares are an early warning that growth is not yet paying for the cost of pursuing it.

## Part 8: Limitations

Percentages conceal absolute size, which is the defining trade of the method. A structure can look ideal while the business itself is shrinking, so the dollar trends shown beside the tables are mandatory companions. A swing in the base also corrupts the reading. A revenue collapse inflates every income statement cost share, and a difference in how two companies present revenue makes their cost shares hard to compare.

The method describes structure without explaining the cause. Every drift in a line still demands the underlying investigation that the line's own metric prescribes. A single unusual year can mislead, so the anomaly columns need to be set aside before reading any trend. The tables are also dense, and they reward systematic reading while punishing a quick glance. The method shows what the company is and has been. The forecasting belongs to the valuation models elsewhere on the page.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which lines have held steady across the full history shown, and which have drifted? Does the company's own narrative account for every material drift?
2. What does one year's column say the business model is? Does the structure match the sector's typical profile, or does it deviate in a way that needs explaining?
3. Which blocks of lines have moved together? What strategy, mix shift, or model change do the coordinated drifts record?
4. Which single-year columns hold the anomalies? What events explain them, and have they been set aside before any trend reading?
5. Do the three statements tell one consistent story? Are the income statement improvements echoed in the cash flow lines, and do the balance sheet changes reconcile with the financing lines?
6. Which expense shares are rising faster than the business explains? Is the cause a classification shift, a capitalization choice, or a real change in spending?
7. How does the structure compare against the closest competitor's? Where exactly in the structure does each company's advantage or burden sit?
8. What does the funding side show over time? Are the equity, debt, and operating liability shares strengthening the balance sheet or tilting it toward obligations?
9. Are the working capital lines thickening or thinning over the years shown? Look at the receivables share, the inventory share, and the payables share together.
10. If the structure of the best year on the table returned, and separately the worst, what would each imply for profit at today's revenue? Which structure does the current price seem to assume is permanent?
