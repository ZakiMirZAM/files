# Capital Charge

## Part 1: Definition

The capital charge is the dollar cost of using the invested capital for one year. Invested capital is the total money that lenders and shareholders have put into the operating business. Every dollar of that funding has a cost, because lenders charge interest and shareholders expect a return for the risk they take. The capital charge states that cost as a single dollar amount for the year. The charge answers one question: how much does the business have to earn just to pay for the money it uses?

The capital charge is what turns accounting profit into economic profit. A company subtracts the capital charge from its operating profit after tax, and only the remainder counts as value created. Economic profit measured this way is called EVA, short for economic value added. A business that earns exactly its capital charge has created nothing for its owners, because the profit was fully consumed by the cost of the funding.

Common readings of the charge against profit:

| Reading | What that reading usually means |
|---|---|
| Operating profit far above the charge | The business earns well beyond the cost of its funding and is creating value. |
| Operating profit roughly equal to the charge | The business is covering its funding cost and no more, so economic profit is near zero. |
| Operating profit below the charge | The business does not earn enough to pay for its capital and is destroying value. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Capital Charge = WACC × Invested Capital`

WACC is the first input. WACC stands for weighted average cost of capital, the blended yearly return that lenders and shareholders require for funding the business. The rate blends the cost of debt and the cost of equity in proportion to how much of each the company uses. WACC is expressed as a percentage, so it sets the price of each dollar of capital for the year.

Invested capital is the second input. Invested capital is all the money tied up in operations, debt plus equity, after removing cash the business does not need to run. Multiplying the rate by the base gives the total dollar cost of the funding for the year. A higher rate or a larger base produces a larger charge.

The capital charge does not appear on any financial statement. The charge is subtracted from NOPAT. NOPAT is net operating profit after tax, the after-tax profit from normal operations before any interest is taken out. NOPAT minus the capital charge equals EVA. A positive EVA means NOPAT cleared the charge, and a negative EVA means the charge was larger than the profit.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Standard accounting profit subtracts the cost of debt but never the cost of equity. Interest paid to lenders appears on the income statement, yet the return owed to shareholders does not. Profit can therefore look positive while the business is still failing to pay for all of its funding. The capital charge fixes that gap by pricing every dollar of capital, equity included.

Equity is not free, even though no bill for it ever arrives. Shareholders could have put their money into another investment of similar risk, so they require a return for choosing this company. The capital charge captures that required return as a real cost. A company creates value only when its operating profit clears the full charge, not merely the interest on its debt.

The capital charge is the input that separates the EVA valuation from ordinary profit-based views. By charging the business for its capital every year, the model rewards only the profit earned above that cost. The charge is why a company can report rising earnings and still show no economic profit, when the earnings were bought with ever more capital.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Two inputs move the capital charge, and both can shift for reasons unrelated to daily operations. The first is WACC. WACC rises when interest rates climb, when the company takes on riskier debt, or when its shares become more volatile. Each of those changes raises the charge. WACC falls when rates drop or the company becomes safer, which lowers the charge even if the business is unchanged.

The second input is invested capital. Building assets, buying another company, or funding more inventory raises the base and lifts the charge. Selling assets or running leaner lowers the base and the charge. Because the charge is the product of the two inputs, a small rise in the rate applied to a large base can add a great deal to the charge in a single year.

Accounting choices feed through the base. Counting leases or research spending as capital rather than as expense enlarges invested capital, which raises the charge. The choice does not change the underlying business, yet it changes the hurdle the profit must clear. A reader should know which treatment produced the base before judging whether the charge was met.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The size of the capital charge follows the size of the invested capital base, so capital-heavy sectors carry large charges and capital-light sectors carry small ones. What matters is not the raw charge but whether the business reliably clears it.

| Industry type | Behavior of the charge | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | Large and stable charge | A big asset base meets a low, steady cost of capital set against reliable revenue. |
| Capital-heavy industrials | Large charge that moves with rates | A big base makes the charge sensitive to changes in the cost of capital. |
| Capital-light software and brands | Small charge | Little capital is on the books, so the charge is easy to clear and tests the business only weakly. |
| Deep cyclicals | Steady charge against swinging profit | The charge holds fairly firm while profit rises and falls, so economic profit swings hard. |
| Banks and insurers | Not a clean measure | Capital and its cost are set by regulation, so the charge is not a plain operating hurdle. |

Comparing the raw capital charge across sectors is meaningless, because a large charge simply reflects a capital-heavy business rather than a demanding one. The useful comparison is whether each company clears its own charge, which the ROIC minus WACC spread states directly.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The capital charge shown year by year traces how the cost of the company's funding has moved. A steady rise usually means the invested capital base is growing, the cost of capital is climbing, or both. Reading the charge next to NOPAT is the point of the exercise. A charge that rises faster than NOPAT is squeezing economic profit, even when reported earnings still look healthy.

A step change in the charge usually points to a large acquisition that enlarged the base or a sharp move in interest rates that lifted the rate. A charge that stays flat while NOPAT climbs is the pattern of genuine value creation, because the profit is pulling clear of its funding cost. High volatility in the charge often comes from a shifting cost of capital rather than from the asset base, which changes more slowly.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** WACC is the weighted average cost of capital, the rate that sets the price of each dollar of funding. WACC is one of the two inputs that build the capital charge, so the charge cannot be read without it. A change in the charge is often driven entirely by a change in WACC rather than by the business.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising WACC lifts the charge and raises the bar the profit must clear, which can turn a value-creating company into a value-destroying one with no change in operations. A low WACC keeps the charge easy to clear and can flatter a weak business. Checking WACC alongside the charge shows whether a missed charge is an operating problem or simply a higher cost of capital.

### Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Invested capital is the base the charge is applied to, the total funding tied up in operations. The capital charge is that base multiplied by the cost of capital, so the base sets the size of the charge. A charge can grow purely because the company has committed more capital, with no change in rates.

**What the combination tells you.** A charge that grows in line with the base means the company is simply funding a larger business at the same cost. A charge that grows faster than the base points to a rising cost of capital instead. Reading the two together separates a bigger business from a more expensive one.

### NOPAT

**Why read the two together.** NOPAT is net operating profit after tax, the profit the capital charge is subtracted from. The charge and NOPAT are the two halves of the economic profit calculation, since EVA is NOPAT minus the charge. Neither number means much for value creation without the other beside it.

**What the combination tells you.** NOPAT above the charge means the business earned more than the cost of its funding and created value. NOPAT below the charge means the funding cost more than the business earned. Watching the gap between the two over time shows whether value creation is widening or narrowing.

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** EVA is economic value added, the profit that remains after the capital charge is subtracted from NOPAT. The capital charge is the exact amount removed to get from accounting profit to economic profit. Reading the charge shows how high the hurdle was that produced the EVA figure.

**What the combination tells you.** A small charge and a large EVA means the business creates value easily with little capital at stake. A large charge and a thin EVA means the business barely clears a heavy funding cost. A negative EVA shows the charge exceeded NOPAT, which flags value destruction that reported profit can hide.

## Part 8: Limitations

The capital charge is only as sound as the two inputs behind it. WACC is an estimate built from market data and assumptions, and reasonable analysts can arrive at different rates for the same company. Invested capital rests on book values and on choices about goodwill, leases, and research spending. A small change in either input moves the charge, and the charge in turn decides whether the company shows economic profit or loss.

The charge can also mislead when the base is measured poorly. A company whose real assets are expensed research or brands shows a tiny base, so its charge is small and almost any profit clears it. An old capital-heavy company can show the reverse, carrying a heavy charge on assets worth far less than their book value suggests. The capital charge sharpens the profit question, yet it inherits every weakness in the cost of capital and the capital base that feed it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current capital charge driven mainly by the size of the invested capital base or by the level of the cost of capital?
2. Does NOPAT clear the capital charge in the latest year? By how wide a margin?
3. In the company's worst year on the chart, did operating profit still cover the charge? What happened to economic profit that year?
4. Has the charge risen faster than NOPAT over the period shown? What has that done to economic profit even as reported earnings grew?
5. What cost of capital produced the charge, and how would the charge change if that rate were one point higher?
6. How much of the base that the charge is applied to is goodwill from past acquisitions? Does the business still clear the charge once goodwill is included?
7. Would counting leases or research spending as capital enlarge the base enough to change whether the charge is met?
8. Does a rising reported profit hide a charge that is rising just as fast, so no economic profit is actually being added?
9. How does the company's ability to clear its charge compare with direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle?
10. Is management adding capital that fails to earn back its charge, or is each new dollar of funding clearing the cost of capital?
