# Intangible Assets

## Part 1: Definition

Intangible assets are the non-physical assets a company carries on its balance sheet. An intangible asset has value but no physical form, so the asset cannot be touched. The line holds patents, trademarks, customer relationships, licenses, developed technology, and content rights. The line answers one question: how much of the company's value sits in nameable assets that exist on paper rather than in physical things?

Intangible assets reach the balance sheet in two ways. Most arrive through acquisitions. When a company buys another business, the buyer must identify and value the intangibles it acquired, such as the target's patents and brands. A smaller share arrives through internal capitalizing, where certain costs such as some software development are recorded as an asset rather than charged as an expense. The defining feature of the line is what it leaves out. The intangibles a company builds on its own, such as a brand grown through years of marketing, are usually not recorded here. Those costs are expensed as they are spent, so the most valuable intangibles often never appear on the balance sheet at all.

Common readings of the line as a share of total assets:

| Level versus total assets | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 10 percent | Few recognized intangibles. The company's value sits elsewhere or in unrecorded intangibles. |
| 10 to 30 percent | A meaningful base of acquired patents, brands, and customer relationships. |
| Above 30 percent | Intangible-heavy, usually from large acquisitions of technology or brand-led businesses. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Intangible assets are reported on the balance sheet at book value after amortization. The net figure is built as:

`Intangible Assets, Net = Gross Intangibles at Cost − Accumulated Amortization`

Gross intangibles is the value first assigned to the assets, usually set during an acquisition when the buyer measures what each patent, brand, or customer relationship is worth. The gross figure also includes internally capitalized costs that the rules allow to be recorded as an asset.

Accumulated amortization is the running total of amortization charged against those intangibles. Amortization is the spreading of an intangible's cost over the years the asset is expected to be useful, much as depreciation spreads the cost of physical assets. An intangible with a limited life, such as a patent, is amortized down toward zero. An intangible judged to last forever, such as some brands, is not amortized but is tested for write-down instead. As a share of total assets the common-size figure is `Intangibles % = Intangible Assets, Net ÷ Total Assets`. The vertical analysis table in this app shows that percent beside the raw dollar figure.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Intangible assets show how much of a company's recorded value rests on rights and relationships rather than on physical plant. Patents, brands, and licenses can be the true source of a company's competitive strength. The line is a partial measure of that strength, partial because the line captures only the intangibles that were acquired or capitalized, not the ones built from within.

The line also flags how much of the asset base is soft. Like goodwill, intangibles cannot easily be sold on their own and may lose value if the acquired business stumbles. A balance sheet heavy with intangibles holds fewer hard, sellable assets than the total suggests. The line is a measure of how much of the company's value depends on rights performing as expected.

The line answers a question with a built-in warning. The line shows the recognized intangibles, yet the most valuable intangibles of many great companies are missing entirely. A company that built a dominant brand on its own may show almost nothing here, while a company that bought a weaker brand may show a large balance. Reading the line means reading both what is there and what the accounting rules left out.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Acquisitions raise the line the most. When a company buys another business, the buyer assigns value to the target's patents, brands, and customer relationships, and those values land here. A large, intangible-rich acquisition can lift the line sharply in a single year. The buyer's judgment about what each intangible is worth sets how much is recorded.

Amortization lowers the line. Each year, amortization charges chip away at the intangibles with limited lives, shrinking the net figure even when the company buys nothing new. A write-down, also called an impairment, also lowers the line. A write-down cuts the recorded value when an intangible is judged to be worth less than its book value, such as a patent made obsolete or a brand that has faded. The write-down records a loss on the income statement.

Accounting choices shape the line heavily. The useful life a company assigns to an intangible sets how fast amortization runs, and the choice of which costs to capitalize versus expense decides what reaches the line at all. Two companies with similar businesses can report very different intangible balances based on these choices and on whether they grew by buying or by building.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Intangible balances depend on how a sector creates value and how that value enters the accounts.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Pharma and biotech | High | Acquired drug patents and pipelines carry large recorded values. |
| Media and entertainment | High | Content libraries, licenses, and acquired catalogs sit on the balance sheet. |
| Consumer brands | Moderate to high | Acquired brands appear, but brands built in-house do not. |
| Industrial and energy | Low | Value sits in physical plant, not in recorded intangibles. |
| Software with in-house products | Often low | The most valuable technology was built internally and expensed, so it is not recorded. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is rarely meaningful. A high balance reflects a sector's habit of acquiring intangibles, not its underlying quality. The accounting gap matters most here. A company can have enormous real intangible value and almost no recorded intangible assets. Compare a company only against peers that create and account for intangibles the same way.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows a multi-year chart of intangibles in dollars and as a percent of total assets. A line that steps up marks acquisitions that brought in patents, brands, or technology. The size of each step shows how intangible-rich each deal was. After an acquisition, the line usually drifts down as amortization wears the acquired intangibles away.

A line that declines steadily without new deals is the normal pattern of amortization grinding down past purchases. The decline is not a warning by itself. A sudden drop larger than ordinary amortization signals a write-down, the company admitting that a patent, brand, or technology is worth less than its book value. Repeated write-downs suggest the company keeps overvaluing what it buys. A line that climbs through serial acquisitions deserves a check against profit. When recorded intangibles grow but earnings do not, the acquired rights may not be delivering. Watch for a base effect: when total assets fall, a steady intangible balance rises as a percent of total assets even though no new intangible was added. Remember throughout that the chart shows only recorded intangibles, so a flat low line does not mean the company lacks valuable intangibles.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Tangible Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Tangible book value per share is the company's equity per share after goodwill and intangible assets are stripped out. Intangible assets are one of the two items the tangible measure removes. Reading the two together shows how much of the company's net worth would vanish if the recorded intangibles proved worthless. The pair separates the hard, sellable equity from the part built on rights and relationships.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with heavy intangibles and a thin tangible book value per share has little hard asset backing behind its shares. If its patents expire or its acquired brands fade, the recorded value can be written off and the shareholders' real cushion shrinks. A company with light intangibles and a solid tangible book value per share rests on harder assets. Reading both tells the investor how much of the stated net worth is physical and how much depends on intangible rights holding their value.

### P/B

**Why read the two together.** The price-to-book ratio compares the share price to the book value of equity, and intangible assets are part of that book value. Acquired intangibles inflate book value, which makes the price-to-book ratio look lower and the stock look cheaper. The pair tests whether the book value the ratio relies on is hard or soft. Recalculating against tangible book value strips the intangibles out.

**What the combination tells you.** A low price-to-book ratio resting on a book value full of intangibles is not the bargain it appears, because the intangibles could be written off. A company whose price-to-book ratio looks reasonable even after intangibles are removed has a firmer footing. Reading both keeps a reader from being misled by a ratio that depends on soft recorded intangibles. The gap between the two versions of the ratio measures how much the cheapness leans on intangibles.

### ROA

**Why read the two together.** Return on assets measures the profit a company earns for each dollar of assets. Intangible assets sit in that asset base and enlarge it, especially after a big acquisition. A heavy intangible balance raises the asset base and pulls the return down unless the acquired rights earn their keep. The pair links the size of the recorded intangibles to the return they help produce.

**What the combination tells you.** A large intangible base paired with a low return on assets means the company carries expensive recorded rights that are not yet earning enough. A company that earns a strong return despite heavy intangibles is putting those acquired rights to good use. Reading both judges whether the prices assigned to acquired intangibles were justified. A rising intangible base with a falling return is a warning that the acquisitions are not paying off.

## Part 8: Limitations

The line is incomplete by design. The most valuable intangibles of many companies, the brands and technologies they built themselves, never reach the balance sheet because the costs were expensed as spent. A company can hold enormous real intangible value and report almost nothing on this line. The line therefore understates intangible strength at companies that grew on their own.

The line is also shaped by judgment and resists comparison. The values assigned to acquired intangibles, the useful lives chosen, and the timing of write-downs all depend on management's estimates. A company that grew by acquisition shows large intangibles, while an identical company that built the same capabilities internally shows almost none. The book value reflects past purchase decisions, not what the rights are worth today. The line tells a reader that intangibles were acquired, but the line alone cannot say whether they remain valuable or whether the company's real intangible strength is even recorded.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What share of total assets is recorded intangibles, and how much of that came from acquisitions rather than internal capitalizing?
2. Which intangibles make up the line: patents, brands, customer relationships, or technology? Which is largest?
3. Has the company recorded any write-downs of intangibles? Were they one-off events or a repeated pattern?
4. Are recorded intangibles growing through acquisitions while profit stays flat? Are the acquired rights delivering?
5. How much would tangible book value per share fall if the intangibles were written off?
6. Is a low price-to-book ratio leaning on a book value padded with acquired intangibles?
7. How much of the company's real intangible strength is unrecorded because it was built in-house and expensed?
8. What useful lives and amortization choices does the company use? Are they aggressive compared with peers?
9. Did total assets fall in a year when intangibles rose as a percent, making a base effect rather than a new purchase the cause?
10. How does the intangible base compare with direct competitors that create and account for intangibles the same way?
