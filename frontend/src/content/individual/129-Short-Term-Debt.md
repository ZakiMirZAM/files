# Short Term Debt

## Part 1: Definition

Short term debt is one balance sheet line. The line totals the company's borrowings that come due within one year. The borrowings include commercial paper, revolver draws, notes payable, and the current portion of long-term debt. Commercial paper is a short loan a company sells to investors for a few months. A revolver draw is money taken from a pre-arranged credit line, much like using a credit card limit. The current portion of long-term debt is the slice of a longer loan whose repayment date now falls inside the next twelve months. Short term debt is interest-bearing, meaning the company pays a fee for the borrowing.

Short term debt sits on the liability side of the balance sheet, inside current liabilities. Current liabilities are debts due within a year. The line answers one question: how much borrowed money must the company repay or refinance in the coming year? Refinance means replacing an old loan with a new one. In the common-size table the line shows as a percent of total assets, which measures the weight of near-term financing pressure.

The line splits into two kinds with different readings. Working-capital borrowings, such as commercial paper and revolver draws, fund daily operations and roll over routinely while credit markets work. Current portions of long-term debt are the real maturity walls, the repayments the year must execute. A maturity wall is a large amount of debt all coming due at once.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Short term debt is not a formula. The line gathers every interest-bearing borrowing due within twelve months:

`Short Term Debt = Short-term borrowings + Current portion of long-term debt`

Short-term borrowings are programs like commercial paper and revolver draws shown at their current balances. The current portion of long-term debt is reclassified each period as a longer loan's maturity date approaches the one-year mark. Reclassification means moving the amount from the long-term row to the short-term row on the calendar alone, with no change to the actual debt. These reclassifications can swing the line up or down for purely mechanical reasons.

The footnotes to the financial statements hold the full maturity schedule. A maturity schedule lists how much debt comes due in each future year. The short term debt line is just one year's slice of that ladder, so the footnote schedule is needed to see the whole picture.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Short term debt measures rollover exposure. Rollover exposure is the risk that the company cannot replace maturing debt when it comes due. The line shows how much financing the year must replace, set against the cash and the market access the company has to replace it. Rollover exposure is the most date-sensitive part of any test of whether a company can survive.

The composition decides the level of worry. Routine commercial paper at a financially strong company is ordinary plumbing that rolls over without drama. Large maturity walls at a strained company are real event risk, because a failed refinancing can force a crisis. The split between the two kinds matters more than the headline total.

Short term debt also moves the liquidity ratios mechanically. When long-term debt reclassifies to short-term, the Current Ratio and Quick Ratio fall even though nothing about the business changed. A careful reader adjusts for these mechanical swings rather than reacting to them.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The maturity calendar writes the line. As each long-term loan approaches its due date, its balance migrates into the short-term row, delivering that year's wall. Working-capital programs swing with seasons and with management policy, rising when the company needs more short funding and falling when it needs less.

Market access prices the rollability of the debt. Rollability is how easily maturing debt can be replaced. When credit markets are calm, refinancing is easy. When markets seize up, refinancing becomes costly or impossible. Stress can also migrate debt perversely. A covenant breach can make long-term debt due immediately, swelling the short-term line exactly when refinancing is hardest. A covenant is a condition in a loan agreement that the borrower must meet.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level follows the company's funding model.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial paper users | Steady standing balance | They fund operations continuously with short paper. |
| Seasonal businesses | Large swings through the year | They borrow short to fund peak inventory, then repay. |
| Term-funded companies | Episodic walls | Their short-term line spikes only when a long loan matures. |
| Banks and insurers | Read differently | Short-term funding is the core of the business, not a financing choice. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the funding models differ. Read the maturity schedule's shape everywhere. A smooth ladder of small maturities is far safer than a tower of debt all due in one year, whatever the sector.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read short term debt as its own multi-year series and as a share of total assets. The own series shows the raw balance over time. The share of total assets shows whether near-term repayment pressure is growing relative to everything the company owns.

Read the swings by composition. The breathing of routine programs up and down is normal noise. The arrival of a maturity wall is the calendar at work, and each refinancing is a test the company either passes or fails. The pattern to catch is the dangerous one: the short-term line swelling while cash and liquidity shrink. A growing front edge of debt set against thinning means to repay it is the classic path into a liquidity crisis. Watch the share rise while the cash line falls. Remember that part of any swing is just mechanical reclassification from the long-term row, which the footnote schedule lets you separate from real new borrowing.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Total Debt

**Why read the two together.** Total Debt is the sum of all the company's borrowings, short-term and long-term together. Short term debt is the front slice of that whole stack. The short-term line alone cannot show whether a big maturity sits behind it. Reading the two together places this year's wall inside the full ladder of debt.

**What the combination tells you.** A small short-term line inside a large Total Debt means most of the debt matures later, so the near-term pressure is light for now. A large short-term line relative to Total Debt means a heavy slice is due soon and refinancing is the immediate task. A smooth gap between the two over time means the ladder is well spread. The pair shows whether the maturity profile is calm or front-loaded.

### Quick Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The Quick Ratio divides the most liquid assets by current liabilities to test whether the company can pay near-term bills without selling inventory. Short term debt is a major current liability inside that test. The most pressing question is whether liquid assets can cover the maturing debt. Reading the two together answers it directly.

**What the combination tells you.** A high Quick Ratio against a large short-term line means the company holds enough liquid assets to cover the maturities if markets close. A low Quick Ratio against a large short-term line means the company depends on refinancing to survive, which is dangerous in a credit crunch. A high ratio with a thin short-term line is the safest setup. The pair shows whether the rollover is covered in hand or hangs on market access.

### Debt Service Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Debt Service Coverage divides operating earnings by the year's total debt payments, both interest and principal. Short term debt is the principal portion of that bill coming due this year. Interest cover alone misses the repayment burden. Reading the two together captures the full claim the year places on earnings.

**What the combination tells you.** A high short-term line with weak Debt Service Coverage means the year's repayments exceed what earnings can cover, so the company must refinance or sell assets. Strong coverage against a modest short-term line means the maturities are comfortably affordable. Watching both over time shows whether rising maturities are outrunning earnings. The pair separates the size of the year's bill from the company's ability to pay it.

## Part 8: Limitations

Reclassification mechanics move the line without any real event. A long loan crossing the one-year mark swells the short-term figure even though the company's debt is unchanged. The reader must strip out these mechanical swings before drawing conclusions.

The two kinds of short-term debt differ sharply in risk, yet the headline figure blends them. Routine commercial paper and a dangerous maturity wall look the same at the top line. The real maturity picture lives in the footnotes, not on the face of the balance sheet. The line is only one year's slice of the full debt ladder. Short term debt is the due-now borrowing. The line must be read by kind. The line must be set against the means to repay it and placed on the company's maturity calendar.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How does the line split between routine working-capital programs and term-debt maturity walls?
2. What cash and committed credit facilities stand against the maturing debt?
3. What does the coming year's refinancing require, and at what interest rate compared with the existing loans?
4. How did the company handle past maturity walls, judging its refinancing record?
5. What long-term debt could accelerate into the short-term line on a covenant breach?
6. How much of any recent swing is mechanical reclassification rather than real new borrowing?
7. What does the commercial paper program depend on, such as the company's credit standing and market conditions?
8. How does the size of the front edge line up with the position in the business cycle?
9. What would a closed credit market force the company to do, such as asset sales or harsh new terms?
10. Is the maturity ladder behind this year's slice being rebuilt smoothly, or is another wall forming?
