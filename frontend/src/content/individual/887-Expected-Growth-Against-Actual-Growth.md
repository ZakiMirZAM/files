# Expected Growth Against Actual Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Expected growth is the growth rate a company's own reinvestment and returns imply. It is not a forecast and it is not guidance. It is arithmetic: a business grows by putting money back into itself and earning a return on that money.

Actual growth is what revenue and profit did. Setting the two beside each other tests whether the growth a company reported can be explained by the money it put to work.

| Reading | What the reading suggests |
|---|---|
| The two agree | The growth is explained by reinvestment. It can repeat |
| Actual far above expected | The growth came from somewhere reinvestment cannot explain |
| Actual far below expected | Money was invested and the growth did not arrive |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

On the operating side the formula is:

`Expected growth = Reinvestment rate × Return on invested capital`

On the equity side the same logic uses the equity figures:

`Expected growth = Retention ratio × Return on equity`

The retention ratio is the share of profit kept rather than paid out, which is one minus the payout ratio.

This app computes the expression twice. Once on the current year, and once on a five year average reinvestment rate multiplied by a five year average return. The two are shown together. Neither replaces the other, because a single year of either input can be unusual and the comparison is what tells you so.

The relationship can also be read backwards, which is often the more useful direction:

`Reinvestment required = Growth rate ÷ Return on invested capital`

That form takes a growth rate as given and reports the reinvestment it demands. A company projecting growth it is not funding will fail this test immediately.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used

A growth rate taken from history assumes the past repeats. A growth rate taken from guidance assumes management is right. Expected growth assumes neither. It asks what the company's own spending and returns can support.

The test is most valuable when it fails. A company growing far faster than its reinvestment explains is growing on something that will not repeat, and naming that something is the analysis.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Both inputs can be unusual in any single year. The reinvestment rate swings on the timing of one large project or one move in working capital. The return on capital swings on a one time item or a tax settlement. The product of two unusual numbers is an expected growth rate that describes nothing.

A negative reinvestment rate is the case that most needs care. It means capital spending fell below depreciation, so the company is consuming its asset base. Multiplied by a positive return, it produces a negative expected growth rate, and that negative figure is information rather than an error. A company can grow while reinvesting nothing, but not forever.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the divergence across the window rather than in one year. A company whose actual growth has run above its expected growth for a single year had a good year. A company where the two have diverged for five years is being measured wrongly, or is growing on something the formula cannot see.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Reinvestment Rate

**Why read the two together.** The reinvestment rate is one of the two inputs, so a surprising expected growth rate is usually a surprising reinvestment rate wearing a disguise. Reading the input directly tells you whether the output is worth anything.

**What the combination tells you.** An expected growth rate near zero can mean the company earns nothing on its capital, or it can mean the company is not reinvesting at all. Those are opposite situations and only the input separates them.

### Return on Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** The return is the other input, and it sets the ceiling on what reinvestment can achieve. The same money put to work at 50 percent and at 5 percent produces very different growth.

**What the combination tells you.** High reinvestment at a low return is growth that destroys value, and the growth rate alone will not show that. The pair does.

## Part 8: Limitations

The formula assumes a stable return on capital. A company whose return is moving will not grow at the rate the current return implies, and the efficiency term that corrects for this is itself an estimate.

The formula also breaks for young companies and for companies building out infrastructure, because their spending arrives years before any profit does. Their current earnings are not evidence about their long term potential, and multiplying a reinvestment rate by a return computed on almost no profit produces a number with no meaning.
