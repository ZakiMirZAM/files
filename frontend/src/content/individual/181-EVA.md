# EVA

## Part 1: Definition

EVA is short for economic value added. It measures the profit a company earns in actual dollars after it has paid every operating cost, the tax bill, and one more charge the income statement never shows: a rent on the money tied up in the business. The income statement stops at accounting profit. EVA keeps going and subtracts the cost of the capital the company uses, then states what is left as a dollar figure.

Capital here means the money supplied by lenders and shareholders to run the company. That money is not free. Lenders want interest and shareholders want a return, so the company owes a yearly charge on the capital it holds. EVA reports the dollar profit that survives after that charge. It is a close cousin of the EVA margin, which expresses the same surplus per dollar of sales. EVA itself is the raw dollar amount, so a large company and a small company are not on the same scale until the figure is sized against something else.

| EVA level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | The company earns an accounting profit but does not cover the cost of its capital. The business is destroying value for its owners. |
| About zero | The company earns exactly the return its capital costs. Owners are no better off than they would be in a similar investment elsewhere. |
| Positive and growing | A real dollar surplus above the capital charge, and the surplus is widening each year. |
| Large and positive | A wide dollar surplus, usually a sign of strong pricing power or very efficient use of capital. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Invested Capital)`

NOPAT is short for net operating profit after tax, which is the profit from normal operations after tax but before any interest on debt. It is the starting point. WACC is short for weighted average cost of capital, the blended rate that lenders and shareholders require on the money they put in. Invested capital is the total amount of that money, the debt and equity actually working in the business.

The term in parentheses is the capital charge, the dollar cost of holding all that capital for a year. Subtracting the capital charge from NOPAT leaves the true surplus, the profit earned above what the capital itself demanded. If NOPAT is 120 and the capital charge is 100, EVA is 20. The business cleared its hurdle by 20 dollars. If the charge were 130, EVA would be negative 10, and the business would be falling short even though it reported an operating profit.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A normal profit figure can look healthy while the business is still failing its owners. A company can report positive net income and yet earn less than the return its capital costs. EVA catches that case directly. It charges the business for the capital it uses, so only profit above that hurdle counts as real value created.

EVA is also a clean way to judge whether growth is worth having. Growth that earns less than the cost of capital lowers EVA even as revenue rises. A bigger company is not automatically a better one. EVA tells you whether the extra size came with extra value or just extra capital. A rising EVA means the company is widening the gap in dollars between what it earns and what its capital costs.

Because EVA is stated in dollars, it shows the size of value creation, not just the rate. A small percentage spread on a very large capital base can produce a large EVA, and a wide spread on a tiny base can produce a small one. The dollar figure captures the scale that a percentage hides.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three forces move EVA. The first is operating profit, which rises and falls with prices, volumes, and costs. Stronger pricing power lifts NOPAT and lifts EVA. The second is the amount of invested capital. A company that ties up more money in equipment, inventory, or acquisitions carries a larger capital charge, which lowers EVA even when profit holds steady. The third is the cost of capital itself. A higher WACC raises the charge and shrinks EVA.

Accounting choices can distort a single reading. Goodwill from past acquisitions inflates the capital base and enlarges the charge, so a serial acquirer often shows a thinner EVA. WACC is an estimate, and a low estimate understates the charge and flatters the figure. Surplus cash held outside the operations can be removed from capital, which changes the charge. One-time gains or losses inside operating profit move NOPAT for reasons unrelated to the ongoing business.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The size of EVA depends on how much capital an industry needs and how much pricing power it holds. Capital-heavy industries carry large charges and have to earn a great deal of operating profit just to break even on value.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and asset-light services | Often strongly positive | Little capital is tied up, so the capital charge is small and most operating profit survives as surplus. |
| Consumer brands with pricing power | Moderate to strong | High margins clear the capital charge with room to spare. |
| Industrial and manufacturing | Thin and cyclical | Heavy equipment means a large capital charge, and profit swings with the economy. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Often near zero | Enormous capital bases produce huge charges, and regulated prices cap the surplus. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Capital and financing are the raw material of the business, so the standard capital charge does not apply. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when two companies need very different amounts of capital to operate. Compare a company against others in the same industry.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in EVA means the company is earning a wider dollar surplus over its capital cost each year. The rise can come from better operating profit or from using less capital to produce the same sales. A steady decline is a warning. Falling EVA means the capital charge is claiming a larger share of operating profit, often because the company is investing heavily without the profit to match.

A step change usually marks an event rather than a trend. A large acquisition can drop EVA in one year by loading the capital base with goodwill. A change in the assumed cost of capital can move the whole line without anything happening inside the business. High volatility points to a cyclical company whose operating profit swings with the economy. Read EVA next to revenue and invested capital, because a figure that climbs only because the company is shrinking its asset base is a different story from one that climbs on real operating gains.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### NOPAT

**Why read the two together.** NOPAT is the operating profit after tax that forms the top of the EVA formula. Reading NOPAT on its own shows the raw profit before any capital charge is applied. EVA then subtracts that charge to reveal the surplus.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising NOPAT with a falling EVA means the capital charge is growing faster than profit, so the extra profit is not creating value. A rising NOPAT alongside a rising EVA means growth is genuinely profitable after the cost of capital. Watching both shows whether reported profit growth is the kind that rewards owners.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** WACC sets the rate behind the capital charge that EVA subtracts. A small change in WACC moves the charge and can flip EVA from positive to negative. Reading WACC tells you how high the hurdle was that the company had to clear.

**What the combination tells you.** A positive EVA earned against a high WACC is more convincing than the same EVA against a low one, because the company cleared a taller hurdle. If EVA turned negative in a year when WACC rose, the cause may be the rate rather than the business. Reading both separates a real operating decline from a rising cost of money.

### EVA Margin

**Why read the two together.** EVA is the surplus in dollars. The EVA margin is that same surplus stated as a percentage of revenue. One shows the size of value creation, the other shows its quality per sale.

**What the combination tells you.** A large EVA paired with a thin EVA margin points to a low-margin business that creates value through sheer scale. A modest EVA paired with a wide margin points to a smaller, highly profitable operation. Reading both separates how much value is created from how efficiently each sales dollar creates it.

## Part 8: Limitations

EVA rests on an estimated cost of capital. That cost is not observed directly, so a small change in the estimate moves the capital charge and the EVA figure. A low WACC estimate flatters EVA, and a high one punishes it, with no change in the business itself. The capital base is also an accounting figure. Goodwill from acquisitions can swell the base and depress EVA, so a single low reading may reflect an old deal rather than a weak operation.

EVA also reflects the choices made when computing NOPAT and capital, and different analysts make different adjustments for leases, research spending, and one-time charges. Two analysts can produce two different EVA figures for the same company. EVA is a backward-looking measure of value created on existing assets, and it says nothing on its own about future investment opportunities. A negative EVA in one year does not prove the business is failing if the company is early in a heavy investment phase that has not yet produced its returns. Because it is a raw dollar number, EVA also needs context from size before it can be compared across companies.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current EVA driven by strong operating profit, a small capital base, or a low assumed cost of capital? What does EVA become if WACC is set higher?
2. How much of the invested capital is goodwill from past acquisitions? What is EVA on the operating assets alone?
3. Has EVA been positive across the worst year on the chart, or only in good years?
4. Does the EVA margin agree with the trend in this dollar figure, or do the two diverge?
5. Is operating profit being lifted by one-time gains that will not repeat? What is EVA without them?
6. Is the company growing revenue and invested capital while EVA falls? Is that growth earning less than the cost of capital?
7. How does EVA compare with direct competitors of a similar size that need a similar amount of capital?
8. Is surplus cash being counted inside the capital base, and would removing it change the charge and the EVA?
9. If EVA is negative, is the business early in an investment phase, or is it a mature operation that simply does not cover its capital cost?
10. How large is EVA relative to the company's invested capital, so the dollar surplus can be judged against the size of the bet behind it?
