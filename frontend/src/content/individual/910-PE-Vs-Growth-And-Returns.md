# P/E vs Growth and Returns

## Part 1: Definition

The price-to-earnings ratio, written P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share. It says how many dollars a buyer pays for one dollar of yearly profit. A high P/E is only worth paying when the company can grow its earnings. Growth is only worth paying for when the company earns more on new investment than its cost of equity. The cost of equity is the yearly return shareholders demand for owning the stock.

This panel puts those three things side by side: the price, the growth, and the returns. It shows figures only. The reader decides whether today's P/E is justified.

The central formula is the fair P/E:

`Fair P/E = (1 − g ÷ ROE) ÷ (Ke − g)`

ROE is return on equity, the yearly profit on each dollar of shareholder money. Ke is the cost of equity. The letter g is the growth rate the company can keep forever. The part `1 − g ÷ ROE` is the share of earnings the company can pay out. The rest must be reinvested to produce the growth.

Growth must stay below both Ke and ROE. Growth at or above ROE would need a payout below zero, meaning new shares sold every year, and the formula then stops describing a value. The panel shows n/a in that case.

When ROE equals Ke the formula collapses to `1 ÷ Ke`. Growth then adds nothing, because every dollar reinvested earns only what shareholders could earn elsewhere. This is the panel's key lesson.

| ROE compared with Ke | What growth does to the fair P/E |
|---|---|
| ROE above Ke | Growth raises the fair P/E. Each reinvested dollar creates value. |
| ROE equal to Ke | Growth changes nothing. The fair P/E is 1 ÷ Ke. |
| ROE below Ke | Growth lowers the fair P/E. Each reinvested dollar destroys value. |

## Part 2: How each figure is calculated

**Price and yield.** The P/E uses forward earnings: the analyst consensus for next year's earnings per share, from Yahoo Finance. The fair P/E formula is written in next year's earnings, so the two compare like for like. The trailing P/E divides today's price by the last reported yearly earnings per share. The earnings yield is earnings divided by price, which is 1 ÷ forward P/E. The 10-year Treasury yield is the risk-free rate inside the cost of equity.

**Growth.** Four growth figures appear, each measured a standard way.

| Figure | How it is measured |
|---|---|
| 5-year earnings growth | Compound yearly growth of earnings per share over the last five fiscal years: (last ÷ first) to the power 1/5, minus 1. |
| Historical 5-year growth (trend) | The slope of a regression of the logarithm of earnings per share on time, over the same six years, turned into a yearly rate. It uses every year, not only the two ends. |
| Consensus growth (next year) | The analysts' earnings per share for next year divided by their figure for this year, minus 1. It is shown for comparison. For some foreign listings the two estimates are published in different currencies, so the figure is n/a. |
| Sustainable growth | ROE × retention. Retention is the share of earnings not paid as dividends. |

Neither historical measure exists when earnings were negative in the window. A percentage change from a loss has no meaning, so the panel shows n/a.

The PEG ratio is the trailing P/E divided by the 5-year earnings growth in whole percent. It uses the trailing P/E because the growth is measured from trailing earnings. A forward P/E would count the first year of growth twice. The PEG does not remove the effect of growth on the P/E, so it is a quick glance only.

The reinvestment rate and incremental ROIC come from the company's metric history. The reinvestment rate is the share of after-tax operating profit put back into the business. Incremental ROIC is the extra operating profit earned on the extra capital invested.

**Returns.** ROE, the cost of equity and stable growth are the same figures the P/B vs ROE panel above uses. ROE is the company's median return on equity over the years on file. ROIC is return on invested capital. WACC is the weighted cost of all the company's capital. The two spreads, ROE − Ke and ROIC − WACC, are shown for every year on file.

**Fair P/E.** The growth inside the formula is the stable rate: ROE × retention, never below zero and never above the 10-year Treasury yield. No business can outgrow the whole economy forever, and the risk-free rate is the ceiling the valuation engine uses everywhere.

| Figure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Actual P/E ÷ fair P/E | Forward P/E divided by fair P/E |
| P/E with no value-adding growth | 1 ÷ Ke |
| Growth premium share of price | 1 − (earnings yield ÷ Ke) |

The growth premium share is the part of the price that pays for growth. Earnings that never grow are worth earnings ÷ Ke, so the rest of the price is paying for growth in advance.

## Part 3: What today's P/E implies

**Growth the P/E implies.** The same formula solved for g. It is the growth rate the company must keep forever, at the ROE used (the median over the years on file), to justify today's forward P/E.

**Two-stage version.** The company grows at one rate for five years. After that, new investment earns only the cost of equity, so later growth adds no value. The panel solves for the five-year growth rate that justifies today's P/E. Growth faster than ROE needs a negative payout, meaning new shares must be sold. That caps what five years can add. When no five-year rate reaches today's P/E, the panel says so instead of showing a number.

**Implied years of high growth.** The same two-stage model, solved for the number of years. The growth rate is the 5-year earnings growth. The answer is the number of years the business must keep growing at that rate, with the ROE used, before returns fall to the cost of equity. It sits beside the reader's own answer on the durability of the company's competitive advantage.

**Implied return at consensus growth.** The formula solved for the return instead. A buyer at today's forward P/E earns `g + (1 − g ÷ ROE) ÷ P/E` a year if earnings grow at the consensus rate forever. The panel shows it beside the cost of equity.

**Implied growth vs deliverable growth.** The growth the P/E implies, minus sustainable growth and minus consensus growth. A positive figure means the price asks for more growth than the company has shown it can deliver.

## Part 4: Sensitivity, scenarios and peers

**Sensitivity grid.** The fair P/E recalculated with the cost of equity two points either side of today's figure, and growth two points either side. A cell where growth reaches the cost of equity or ROE has no fair P/E, so it shows n/a. Darker cells hold higher fair P/E values.

**Rate shock.** The 10-year Treasury yield moved 100 basis points (1 percentage point) up and down. The cost of equity moves by the same amount. The ceiling on stable growth moves with it.

**Bear, base and bull.** The panel's three growth figures are 5-year earnings growth, sustainable growth and consensus growth. Ranked from lowest to highest they become bear, base and bull. Each is held for five years in the two-stage model.

**Peers.** Every profitable company in the industry that reports in US dollars, on one method: P/E is the fiscal-year-end market value divided by net income, and the fair P/E uses each company's own ROE, cost of equity and payout on the same trailing basis. These peer figures use the latest year's ROE, so the company's own row can differ from the panel above, which uses the price today and the ROE used. The table shows actual minus fair P/E. The panel then fits a straight line of P/E against the spread ROE − Ke across all of them. The company's residual is its P/E minus the line's P/E at its own spread. A line through a handful of firms describes nothing, so no line is drawn on fewer than five peers.

Companies outside the plausibility bounds the scorecards use are left out: a P/E above 100, an ROE beyond plus or minus 100 percent, or a cost of equity outside 2 to 25 percent. Figures that far out usually come from tiny earnings or a tiny book value, and one of them would move the line more than the rest of the industry together.
