# Debt Service Coverage

## Part 1: Definition

The debt service coverage ratio measures how many times a company could pay one year of total debt payments out of one year of operating income. Debt service means the full bill owed to lenders for the year. Debt service combines two parts. The first is interest, which is the fee a company pays on borrowed money. The second is the principal portion due this year, which is the slice of the borrowed amount itself that the loan schedule requires the company to repay. Principal is the original sum borrowed, separate from the fee for borrowing. The ratio answers one question: does the business generate enough to cover both the interest and the principal coming due this year?

A ratio of 2.0 means the company earns two dollars of operating income for every dollar of debt payment owed that year. A ratio below 1.0 means operating income alone cannot cover the year's debt bill. The shortfall must then come from cash savings, asset sales, or new borrowing. The metric is stricter than interest coverage, which tests only the interest fee, because debt service coverage adds the principal repayments due in the same year.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | Operating income cannot cover the year's interest and principal. The company must use savings, sell assets, or borrow more to pay lenders. |
| 1.0 to 1.25 | The company can cover the payments but with little room for error. One weak year could create serious trouble. |
| 1.25 to 1.5 | Acceptable for most established companies with steady revenue. |
| 1.5 and above | Comfortable. This app's quality score awards a pass at 1.50 or higher, meaning earnings exceed the full debt bill by half again. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Debt Service Coverage = Operating Income ÷ Total Debt Service`

Operating income is the top number. Operating income is the profit the business earns from normal operations before interest and taxes. Operating income belongs on top because the test asks whether the business itself produces enough cash to satisfy lenders, so the profit used must be measured before financing costs are taken out. A profit number that already subtracts interest would make the test circular.

Total debt service is the bottom number. Total debt service is interest expense plus the principal repayments scheduled for the year. Interest expense is the year's full cost of carrying all borrowed money such as bonds, bank loans, and leases. Principal repayment is the slice of the original borrowed amount that comes due this year. Principal is included here, while interest coverage leaves it out, because the full contractual claim is interest plus the maturing principal. A company can cover its interest comfortably while being crushed by the principal coming due.

Variants exist, and lenders define the exact terms inside each loan agreement.

| Version | Bottom or top number | What the version changes |
|---|---|---|
| Operating-income based | Operating income ÷ (interest + principal due) | The version this app uses. Operating income is profit before interest and taxes. |
| EBITDA based | EBITDA ÷ (interest + principal due) | EBITDA is operating income with depreciation and amortization added back. Those are non-cash charges that spread the cost of assets over many years. This version reads higher. |
| Cash-flow based | (EBITDA − capex) ÷ (interest + principal due) | Capex means capital expenditures, the money spent on equipment and buildings. Subtracting capex first gives the strictest reading. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The size of a company's debt means little on its own. The useful question is whether the company can actually make the payments, because companies default on payments rather than on ratios. Debt service coverage turns an abstract debt load into a concrete yearly affordability test, and it does so on the full bill rather than the interest alone.

Lenders rely on the ratio more than any other. Banks write debt service coverage into loan covenants. A covenant is a condition inside a loan agreement that the borrower must keep, such as holding coverage above an agreed level. Project financiers size whole deals to a minimum coverage level. The app follows the same logic and awards a quality-score pass at 1.50 or higher.

Shareholders care because every dollar of debt service is paid before any profit reaches shareholders. Dividends, buybacks, and reinvestment all come out of what remains after lenders are paid. Thin coverage means the equity survives on a small residual, while high coverage means the debt payments barely dent the earnings stream.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The top number moves with everything that drives operating earnings: revenue, margins, and the business cycle. A company whose earnings swing widely will show a ratio that swings just as widely, and the ratio is often strongest exactly when the business is least at risk. The bottom number moves with three distinct forces. The debt balance itself rises with acquisitions and buybacks funded by borrowing. Interest rates reprice the floating-rate portion immediately, and reprice fixed-rate debt at each refinancing, which means replacing an old loan with a new loan at current rates. A low-rate stretch can flatter coverage until the rollover arrives.

The repayment schedule controls the principal part of the bill. Two companies with the same total debt can show very different coverage depending on whether repayments are spread evenly or bunched into a single year. A single large maturity year can push the ratio down sharply even when the business has not changed. Lease obligations, where they are treated as debt, add their own payments to the bottom number. When the ratio moves, the first question is whether the cause was earnings or one of the debt inputs. The debt inputs are the interest rate, the debt balance, and the repayment schedule.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The level a sector can safely run reflects how steady its revenue is. Steadier revenue makes a thinner cushion safe.

| Industry type | Typical range or behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | Low minimums accepted | Revenue is near certain because regulators approve prices, so lenders accept a thin cushion on purpose. |
| Real estate and project finance | Lender demands a set minimum, often 1.2 to 1.5 | Lenders size the loan to the contracted rents or cash flows and refuse to lend below the minimum coverage. |
| Cyclical producers, such as miners and airlines | Judge at the worst year | Earnings can fall by half in a downturn, so coverage of 3.0 at the peak can be below 1.0 in the trough. |
| Capital-light low-debt companies | Very high or undefined | Many carry little debt, so the ratio says little about them. |
| Banks | Not applicable | Debt is the operating raw material of a bank rather than a financing cost. |

Always compare a company against peers with similar repayment schedules, and re-run the ratio mentally at recession-level earnings before trusting it.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A high and steady line is the picture of a balance sheet that never forces management's hand. A rising trend usually traces to debt being paid down or earnings growing, and a rising trend is straightforwardly good. The pattern that deserves the most attention is the slow decline: earnings flat while debt creeps up, or debt flat while each refinancing reprices at a higher rate. Either way the cushion thins a little each year until an ordinary recession becomes a serious problem.

A sharp drop in a single year is the key base effect to watch. Lumpy principal maturities can make the line jump in one year without the business changing at all, because a large repayment bunched into that year inflates the bottom number once and then disappears. The follow-up question is whether that maturity was refinanced smoothly or paid down. Read the line against the interest coverage chart. Debt service coverage falling while interest coverage holds steady means the problem is the principal schedule rather than earnings power, which is a refinancing-calendar problem with a refinancing-calendar solution.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest coverage tests only the interest fee on the debt. Debt service coverage adds the principal repayments that come due in the same year. Reading both separates the cost of carrying the debt from the burden of repaying it, which are two different risks that can move apart.

**What the combination tells you.** A high interest coverage ratio combined with a low debt service coverage ratio means the repayment schedule is the real risk rather than the interest cost. Such a company depends on refinancing to survive, and refinancing can become expensive or unavailable in a credit crunch. When both ratios are high, the debt is safe on both the fee and the repayment. When both are low, the company has a genuine solvency problem.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Debt service coverage measures whether this year's payments are affordable. Net Debt / EBITDA measures the total size of the debt in years of earnings. Affordability and total size are different questions, and a complete view of debt risk needs both answers.

**What the combination tells you.** Low total debt with high coverage means conservative finances. High total debt with high coverage means a large load carried at cheap rates, which is exposed to refinancing risk when rates rise or the maturity arrives. Low total debt with weak coverage points to an earnings problem rather than a debt problem. The pair separates the amount of the debt from the affordability of this year's payments.

### FCF / Total Debt

**Why read the two together.** The coverage ratio uses operating income, which is an accounting figure rather than cash. FCF / Total Debt uses free cash flow, the cash actually left after the company spends on equipment to keep running. Free cash flow is the resource that truly makes the debt payments, so the pair checks whether the earnings behind the coverage arrive as spendable cash.

**What the combination tells you.** Strong coverage paired with weak FCF / Total Debt means the accounting profit is not converting into cash, often because equipment spending or working capital is absorbing it. Strong coverage paired with healthy free cash flow confirms the company can both afford this year's bill and chip away at the whole debt balance over time. Reading the pair shows whether the comfortable coverage is real or only on paper.

### Operating Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Operating income is the top number of the coverage ratio, and operating income can include profit that has not yet been collected as cash. Operating cash flow shows how much cash the business actually produced from operations. Comparing the two confirms whether the earnings that cover the debt are arriving as cash or sitting in unpaid invoices.

**What the combination tells you.** Operating income well above operating cash flow means part of the apparent coverage is made of accruals that may never turn into cash, which weakens the affordability the ratio reports. Operating cash flow that tracks operating income confirms the coverage is built on real cash. Watching both over several years reveals whether a steady-looking coverage line rests on collected cash or on profit the company is still waiting to receive.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio's definitions vary widely. Practitioners put operating income, EBITDA, or EBITDA minus capex on top. Practitioners also differ on which principal payments to include on the bottom. Figures from different sources are rarely comparable without checking the exact recipe. Operating income can also overstate the cash truly available for debt service, because a business can show passing coverage while having no free cash left after mandatory equipment spending and working capital needs.

The ratio is a single-year snapshot, usually covering twelve months. The snapshot is blind to a large maturity sitting just outside the measurement window, and it ignores undrawn credit lines and cash reserves that provide real capacity to pay. The ratio also says nothing about loan covenants, which can put a company in default at coverage levels well above 1.0. Treat the ratio as the lender's headline question, then read the actual debt footnote for the detail behind the answer.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What exactly is in this year's debt service: how much is interest, how much is scheduled principal, and how lumpy is the maturity calendar over the next five years?
2. Which moved the coverage trend on the chart, the earnings on top or the debt service on the bottom? If the debt service moved, was the cause a growing debt balance or a bunched maturity? Was it instead a higher rate at the latest refinancing?
3. What would coverage be in the company's worst earnings year of the past decade, with today's debt service held constant? Does it stay above 1.0?
4. How much of the debt has a floating rate or matures within two years? What does coverage look like if refinancing happens at current market rates rather than the older embedded rates?
5. Does operating cash flow confirm the operating income used on top, or is part of the apparent coverage made of accruals that never arrive as cash?
6. After mandatory equipment spending and working capital needs, how much cash is genuinely free for debt service? Would coverage built on that stricter number still pass?
7. What covenants attach to the major loans, at what coverage and leverage levels do they trip, and how much headroom exists right now?
8. Why does the company carry this debt at all? Is the asset or acquisition it funded actually producing the earnings that now service it?
9. How does this company's coverage compare with its closest competitors at the same point in the business cycle? Who in the peer group would be forced to sell assets first in a two-year downturn?
10. Is management's stated leverage policy consistent with the chart, and do its buyback and dividend decisions respect the coverage cushion or spend it?
