# Return on Capital Against Its Own History

## Part 1: Definition

The five year average return on invested capital is the mean of a company's return on invested capital across the last five reported financial years. Set beside the current year, the pair asks whether this year's return is the level the business normally earns.

The attribution goes one step further. It decomposes the distance between the current return and the average into the three things that can move a return on capital: the operating margin, the capital turnover, and the tax rate. It answers why the gap exists rather than only reporting that it does.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The return on invested capital breaks into three parts that multiply together:

`ROIC = Operating margin × (1 − Tax rate) × Capital turnover`

Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue. Capital turnover is revenue divided by invested capital. The tax term converts operating profit to an after tax figure.

Each effect is then measured by moving one component to its current value while holding the other two at their five year averages:

`Margin effect = (Margin_now − Margin_avg) × (1 − Tax_avg) × Turnover_avg`

The tax effect and the turnover effect are computed the same way, each moving its own component and holding the rest. The three effects sum to the total gap.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used

A return on capital that rose from 44 percent to 52 percent tells you the direction and nothing else. The three effects tell you the cause, and the cause decides whether the improvement should be carried into a forecast.

A gap driven by a wider operating margin is a different business result from a gap driven by a lower tax rate. The first can persist. The second reverses the moment the tax settlement does.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The margin effect moves with pricing, with cost control, and with any one time item sitting inside operating profit. The tax effect moves with the effective rate, which can swing on a single settlement or a change in where profit is booked.

The turnover effect deserves the most care, because it can rise for opposite reasons. Turnover rises when revenue grows against a steady asset base, which is genuine productivity. Turnover also rises when the asset base shrinks through write downs, disposals, or simple underinvestment, which is not productivity at all. The number is identical in both cases and the meaning is opposite.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Returns above the cost of capital invite competition, so a high return normally fades unless something blocks entry. The speed of the fade across the window is evidence about whether an advantage is durable.

Watch which of the three effects carries the trend. A return held up year after year by turnover while the margin erodes is a business being worked harder on a thinner base, and that has a floor.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Sales / Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Capital turnover is one of the three components, so reading it separately shows whether the return is being driven by the asset base or by profitability. It is the component most often mistaken for good news.

**What the combination tells you.** Turnover rising alongside a steady or growing capital base is genuine productivity. Turnover rising while invested capital falls is the asset base shrinking, and the return will look better while the business gets smaller.

### Operating Margin

**Why read the two together.** The margin is the component that reflects pricing power most directly. A return on capital held up by margin is resting on the company's ability to charge more than its costs, which is the part of a business a competitor finds hardest to attack.

**What the combination tells you.** A widening margin alongside a rising return on capital is the strongest version of the result. A flat margin alongside a rising return means the gain came from somewhere else, and that somewhere else needs naming.

## Part 8: Limitations

The attribution reconciles only when the return and its three components are measured on one base. Where the headline return comes from a different source than the components, the effects will not sum to the displayed gap, and the decomposition describes an arithmetic that the page does not show.

The three effects also interact. Moving one component while holding the others fixed assigns the small overlap between them to whichever effect is measured first. The effects are a guide to which driver dominates, not an exact accounting to the last decimal.
