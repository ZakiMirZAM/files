# Net Interest Margin

## Part 1: Definition

Net interest margin measures the lending spread a bank earns. A bank makes money by borrowing at one rate and lending at a higher one. It takes in deposits and other funding, pays a low rate of interest on them, then lends that money out as loans or invests it in bonds at a higher rate. The difference between what it earns on its lending and what it pays on its funding is its core profit. Net interest margin expresses that difference as a percentage of the assets the bank holds.

The metric answers a focused question: for every dollar the bank has working in loans and securities, how much net interest does it keep after paying for its funding? It is the most important profitability measure for a lender, because lending spread is where a traditional bank earns most of its income. Net interest margin is meaningful only for spread-based lenders. For a software company, a retailer, or a manufacturer, the figure is near zero or simply not applicable, because they do not earn their living by lending money.

| Net interest margin level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 2 percent | A thin spread, common at large banks competing on price or holding low-yield assets. |
| 2 to 4 percent | A typical, healthy spread for a commercial bank. |
| Above 4 percent | A wide spread, often a consumer lender or a bank in a higher-rate market. |
| Near zero or not applicable | The company is not a spread-based lender, so the metric does not describe its business. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Net Interest Margin = (Interest Income − Interest Expense) ÷ Total Assets`

Interest income is everything the bank earns from lending and from interest-bearing investments. Interest expense is everything it pays out to depositors and other funding sources. Subtracting expense from income gives net interest income, the dollar profit from the spread alone. Dividing that by total assets scales it, so a large bank and a small one can be compared on the same basis.

Some analysts divide by average earning assets rather than total assets, since cash and fixed assets earn no interest, and that version reads slightly higher. This app uses total assets, which is a cleaner, more comparable base. The result is a percentage that captures the whole spread business in one number. If a bank earns 5 percent on its loans and pays 1 percent on its deposits, and most of its assets are lending, its net interest margin will land somewhere near the gap between those two rates, adjusted for the share of assets that actually earn interest.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

For a bank, net interest margin is the engine of profitability. Most of a traditional bank's earnings come from the spread, so the margin is the cleanest single read on whether the lending business is healthy. A wide and stable margin means the bank funds itself cheaply and lends at attractive rates. A thin or shrinking margin means competition, low rates, or a poor asset mix are squeezing the core business.

The margin is also a good early signal of how a bank will perform as interest rates change. Banks borrow short and lend long, so when short-term rates rise faster than the bank can reprice its loans, the margin gets squeezed, and when the gap moves the other way, the margin widens. Watching net interest margin shows how exposed the bank is to the rate environment. Because it strips the business down to the spread, the margin also lets one bank be compared cleanly against another, which return on equity alone, with all its leverage effects, does not.

For non-lenders, the metric serves a different purpose: it confirms they are not banks. A near-zero net interest margin simply tells you the company earns its money some other way, and the metric should be set aside in favor of operating measures.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The level of interest rates is the biggest force. When rates rise, the yield on new loans climbs, but the cost of deposits often lags, so the margin can widen, at least for a while. When rates fall, loan yields drop and the margin can compress. The shape of the yield curve matters too: a steep curve, where long rates sit well above short rates, favors banks that borrow short and lend long.

The bank's asset mix is the second force. A bank weighted toward high-yield consumer loans earns a wider margin than one holding mostly low-yield government bonds or sitting on excess cash. The funding mix is the third. A bank funded by cheap, sticky deposits keeps its interest expense low and its margin wide, while one relying on expensive wholesale borrowing pays more and earns less. Competition presses on both sides, forcing higher deposit rates or lower loan rates. Credit quality also feeds in indirectly, since loans that stop paying interest pull down income even though the margin formula itself counts the assets.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Net interest margin is an industry-specific metric. It describes lenders and almost nothing else, so the only useful comparisons are among banks and similar spread-based institutions.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer and retail banks | Wide | High-yield consumer loans and cheap deposit funding produce a strong spread. |
| Large diversified banks | Moderate | A mix of lower-yield corporate lending and securities holds the margin down. |
| Investment banks | Thin or not central | Most income comes from fees and trading, not from a lending spread. |
| Insurers | Not applicable | Profit comes from underwriting and investment returns, not a deposit-to-loan spread. |
| Non-financial companies | Near zero | They do not lend as a business, so the metric does not describe them. |

Compare net interest margin only among lenders with similar business models. A consumer bank and a wholesale-funded corporate bank earn their spreads in different ways, so even within banking the comparison needs care.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

For a bank, a stable net interest margin across several years signals a well-managed spread business with reliable funding and disciplined lending. A widening margin can be good news, more profit per dollar of assets, but check whether it came from a richer asset mix or simply from rising rates that may reverse. A narrowing margin is a warning that competition, falling rates, or a shift into lower-yield assets is squeezing the core.

Read the margin against the interest-rate cycle, because much of its movement is driven by rates rather than by the bank itself. A margin that widened only because rates rose will give back the gain when rates fall. A margin that held steady through a falling-rate environment shows genuine funding strength. Watch also for a margin that looks healthy while loan losses are climbing, since the spread can look fine right up until borrowers stop paying. For a non-lender, the trend should simply stay near zero; any meaningful reading would suggest the metric is being misapplied.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return on Equity

**Why read the two together.** Net interest margin measures the profitability of the spread, while return on equity measures the profit earned on the shareholders' money. A bank turns its margin into return on equity by lending many dollars against each dollar of equity, so the two are linked through the bank's leverage.

**What the combination tells you.** A thin net interest margin can still produce a strong return on equity if the bank is highly leveraged, but that return rests on a large balance sheet and carries more risk. A wide margin with a modest return on equity may mean the bank is conservatively funded. Reading both shows whether profitability comes from the quality of the spread or from the scale of the lending, which matters for judging how safe the returns are.

### Cost of Equity

**Why read the two together.** For banks, the cost of equity is the proper hurdle, since the blended cost of capital is ill-defined for lenders. Net interest margin drives the bank's earnings, and the cost of equity sets the return those earnings must clear to create value for shareholders.

**What the combination tells you.** A bank can earn a wide net interest margin yet still fall short if its cost of equity is high, often because the market sees its lending as risky. Reading the margin next to the cost of equity connects the operating spread to the value test: the spread has to translate into a return on equity that beats the cost of equity. A wide margin paired with a high cost of equity is less reassuring than a moderate margin paired with a low one.

## Part 8: Limitations

Net interest margin captures only the spread, which means it ignores the rest of a modern bank's income and risks. Many banks earn large fees from cards, advisory work, and asset management, and the margin says nothing about those. More importantly, the margin says nothing about credit quality. A bank can post a healthy margin while making loans that will later default, and the spread will look fine until the losses arrive. A high margin earned by lending to risky borrowers is not the same as a high margin earned safely.

The base also matters. Using total assets includes cash and non-earning assets, which understates the spread on the assets that actually earn, while using earning assets reads higher; the two are not directly comparable across sources. The metric is meaningless for non-lenders, where a near-zero reading carries no information. And because so much of the margin is driven by the interest-rate cycle, a single year tells you as much about rates as about the bank. The margin should always be read with loan-loss and fee measures, never on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is this company actually a spread-based lender, or is a near-zero net interest margin simply telling us the metric does not apply?
2. Did a recent change in the margin come from the bank's asset and funding mix, or just from a move in interest rates?
3. Is a healthy margin being earned safely, or by lending to riskier borrowers who may default later?
4. How does the margin look against the interest-rate cycle, and would it survive a fall in rates?
5. How much of the bank's income comes from the spread versus fees, and does the margin describe most of the business?
6. Is the margin computed on total assets or earning assets, and is the comparison with peers on the same basis?
7. Is the funding cheap and sticky deposits, or more expensive wholesale borrowing that will press the margin?
8. Does a wide margin translate into a return on equity that clears the bank's cost of equity?
9. Are loan losses rising even while the margin looks stable, hinting at trouble the spread does not show?
10. How does the margin compare with banks of a similar model, lending the same way against similar funding?
