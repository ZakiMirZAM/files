# Reinvestment Rate

## Part 1: Definition

The reinvestment rate measures what share of a company's after-tax operating profit is put back into the business rather than left over for lenders and shareholders. After-tax operating profit is the profit from normal operations after tax but before any interest on debt. Reinvestment is the money spent on new equipment, buildings, and working capital to grow or sustain the business. Working capital is the money tied up in inventory and unpaid customer bills, less the bills the company has not yet paid suppliers. The rate divides reinvestment by after-tax operating profit.

The rate answers one question: of every dollar of operating profit the company earns, how much does it plow back to keep growing? A rate of 40 percent means forty cents of each after-tax operating dollar goes back into the business, leaving sixty cents for lenders and shareholders. A rate above 100 percent means the company invests more than it earns and funds the gap from its balance sheet. A negative rate means the company is releasing capital, shrinking its committed base while still earning a profit.

| Rate level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | The company is pulling capital out of the business. The committed base is shrinking. |
| 0 to 30 percent | Light reinvestment. Common for mature companies that return most of their profit to owners. |
| 30 to 70 percent | Moderate reinvestment. The company funds steady growth while still returning cash. |
| Above 70 percent | Heavy reinvestment. The company is plowing most of its profit back to grow quickly. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Reinvestment Rate = (Net Capex + Change in Working Capital) ÷ After-Tax Operating Profit`

The top number is total reinvestment. Net capex is capital spending less depreciation, the part of equipment and building spending that goes beyond replacing worn assets. The change in working capital is the extra money tied up in inventory and unpaid customer bills as the business grows. Adding the two gives the full amount of profit put back into both long-lived assets and day-to-day operations.

After-tax operating profit is the bottom number. After-tax operating profit is the profit the operations earn after tax but before interest, often called NOPAT, which is short for net operating profit after tax. Operating profit is used rather than net income because the rate is measuring what the whole business reinvests, before the split between lenders and shareholders. Dividing reinvestment by this profit shows the share of each operating dollar that the company chooses to put back to work.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The reinvestment rate is the bridge between profit and growth. A company grows its operating profit by reinvesting and by earning a good return on what it reinvests. Future growth in operating profit equals the reinvestment rate multiplied by the return on invested capital. The reinvestment rate is therefore one of the two ingredients that decide how fast a company can grow from its own operations.

The rate also shows what is left for owners. Profit that is reinvested is not available for dividends, buybacks, or paying down debt. A low reinvestment rate means more profit flows out to lenders and shareholders. A high rate means most of the profit stays inside the business in the hope of future growth. The rate tells the reader how a company is balancing growth against payout.

Reinvestment only creates value when the return on it beats the cost of capital. A high reinvestment rate is good news only if the company earns a strong return on what it reinvests. The same rate can build value at a company with high returns and destroy it at a company with low returns. The reinvestment rate must always be read next to the return the company earns.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The top number moves with the company's growth plans. A company building new capacity or pushing into new markets reinvests heavily, which raises the rate. Working capital swings also move it. Rapid growth ties up more cash in inventory and customer bills, lifting reinvestment, while a company that collects cash faster or runs down inventory can show negative reinvestment for a period. The bottom number moves with operating profit, so a weak profit year can push the rate up even when reinvestment is flat, because the same spending is divided by a smaller profit.

Accounting and timing choices can distort a single reading. Net capex depends on depreciation, which rests on the chosen useful lives of assets, so a company that records low depreciation shows higher net capex and a higher rate. Acquisitions are a form of reinvestment that ordinary capex misses, so a company that grows by buying others can show a low rate while reinvesting heavily through deals. One large project or one large working-capital swing can spike the rate in a single year, which is why the multi-year average matters more than any one year.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal reinvestment rate depends on how capital-hungry an industry is and on where a company sits in its life. Young, fast-growing companies reinvest heavily, while mature companies reinvest little and pay out the rest.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Early-stage growth companies | High, often above 70 percent | Most profit is plowed back to fund rapid expansion. |
| Capital-heavy industrials and utilities | Moderate to high | Large asset bases need steady investment to grow and to maintain. |
| Mature, cash-returning companies | Low, often below 30 percent | Limited growth means most profit is returned to owners. |
| Asset-light software and services | Often low or noisy | Little physical capital is needed, though acquisitions can lift the true figure. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Reinvestment is measured through regulatory capital rather than capex and working capital. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when two companies sit at very different stages of growth. Judge the rate against the company's own history and against the return it earns on what it reinvests.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady high reinvestment rate marks a company in a growth phase, funding expansion from its own profit. A steady low rate marks a mature company returning most of its profit to owners. A rate that climbs over time means the company is plowing back more of each profit dollar, which is good news only if the return on that reinvestment is holding up. A rate that falls over time can mean growth opportunities are running out, so the company is keeping less and paying out more.

A single spike or dip is often timing rather than a trend, because one large project or one working-capital swing can move the rate sharply in one year. A negative rate in a single year usually reflects a company releasing working capital or selling assets rather than a lasting shift. Read the rate next to the return on invested capital and to revenue growth. A rising reinvestment rate that produces no growth in operating profit is a warning that the reinvestment is earning poor returns.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** The reinvestment rate and the return on invested capital are the two ingredients of self-funded growth. Growth in operating profit equals the reinvestment rate multiplied by the return on invested capital. One sets how much profit is plowed back, the other sets how well that profit is put to work.

**What the combination tells you.** A high reinvestment rate creates value only when the return on invested capital is strong. A high rate paired with a low return means the company is plowing money into projects that earn little, which destroys value as it grows. A high rate paired with a high return is the signature of a company that compounds value. Reading both shows whether reinvestment is building wealth or burning it.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the cash left after reinvestment is paid out of operating cash flow. The reinvestment rate measures how much of operating profit that reinvestment consumes. The two are opposite sides of the same decision, because every dollar reinvested is a dollar that does not become free cash flow.

**What the combination tells you.** A high reinvestment rate leaves little free cash flow, which is acceptable when the reinvestment earns good returns. A low reinvestment rate leaves abundant free cash flow, which signals a mature company with limited growth ahead. Reading both shows whether thin free cash flow comes from heavy investment in the future or from a struggling business.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation measures physical investment against asset wear. The reinvestment rate measures total reinvestment, including working capital, against operating profit. Both describe how much the company is plowing back, with the reinvestment rate covering the broader picture.

**What the combination tells you.** A reinvestment rate and a capex-to-depreciation ratio that both run high confirm a genuine building phase. A high reinvestment rate with a capex-to-depreciation ratio near 1.0 means the reinvestment is going into working capital or acquisitions rather than new physical capacity. Reading both shows where the reinvested money is actually being put.

## Part 8: Limitations

The reinvestment rate uses operating profit as its base, and operating profit is an accounting figure that can swing for one-time reasons. A weak profit year inflates the rate even when spending is unchanged, because the same reinvestment is divided by a smaller profit. The rate also depends on net capex, which rests on depreciation, so the chosen useful lives of assets affect the reading. A company that records low depreciation shows a higher reinvestment rate without investing any more.

The rate misses reinvestment that flows through acquisitions rather than capex and working capital, so a company that grows by buying others can look as though it reinvests little while reinvesting heavily through deals. The rate also says nothing about the quality of the reinvestment. A high rate is only good when the return on that reinvestment beats the cost of capital, and the rate on its own cannot show that. A single year is easily distorted by one large project or one working-capital swing, so the rate is most reliable as a multi-year average read alongside the return the company earns.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current rate driven by genuine reinvestment, or by a weak profit year that shrank the base it is divided by?
2. What return is the company earning on what it reinvests? Does that return beat the cost of capital?
3. Is a high reinvestment rate producing the operating-profit growth it should, or is the reinvestment earning poor returns?
4. How much of the company's real reinvestment runs through acquisitions that the capex-and-working-capital figure misses?
5. Is the reinvestment going into physical capacity, into working capital, or into deals?
6. Does the rate look high only because the company records low depreciation through long asset lives?
7. Is a single-year spike or dip just timing from one large project, or a lasting change in strategy?
8. Has a falling rate over time signaled that growth opportunities are running out, with more profit now paid to owners?
9. How does the rate compare with direct competitors at the same stage of growth?
10. If the rate is negative, is the company releasing working capital or selling assets, and is that a one-time event or a real retreat?
