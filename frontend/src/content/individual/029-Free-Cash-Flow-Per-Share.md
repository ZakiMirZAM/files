# Free Cash Flow Per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Free cash flow per share measures how much truly spendable cash the business produced for each share of stock that exists. Free cash flow is the cash left after the company has paid all its operating costs and funded the equipment spending needed to keep running. Free cash flow is often called owner earnings, because free cash flow is the money owners could take out of the business each year without harming its operations. Dividing it across all the shares answers one question: how much distributable cash does a single share's slice of the business produce?

A figure of eight means the business produced eight dollars of free cash for every share, after both operating costs and necessary equipment spending. That cash is what funds dividends, buybacks, debt repayment, and reserves for the future. Free cash flow per share is the per-share form of the number this app's valuation framework is built on, because the value of a share ultimately rests on the cash the share can produce over time.

Common readings of the figure:

| Trend in free cash flow per share | What that trend usually means |
|---|---|
| Positive and rising | The business generates growing spendable cash for each owner after all necessary spending. |
| Positive but flat | Equipment spending is rising as fast as operating cash, so owners gain no extra cash. |
| Negative during growth | Heavy investment is consuming all the operating cash, common in a fast-expanding company. |
| Negative without growth | The business cannot fund its own equipment needs from operations, a serious warning. |
| Rising while total free cash flow is flat | The share count is shrinking through buybacks. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`FCF Per Share = Free Cash Flow ÷ Shares Outstanding`

Free cash flow is the top number. Free cash flow starts from operating cash flow, which is the cash the core business generated after paying suppliers, employees, interest, and taxes. Free cash flow then subtracts capital expenditures, written as capex, which is the cash spent on equipment, buildings, and other long-lived assets. Free cash flow belongs on top because capex is not optional. A business must keep replacing worn-out equipment, so the cash spent on it is not available to owners and must come out before the cash is called free.

Shares outstanding is the bottom number. Shares outstanding is the count of ownership units in public hands. The app uses the weighted average share count for the year, which averages the count across the months so that shares issued or bought back partway through the year are counted only for the part of the year they existed. Dilution is the rise in the share count when the company issues new shares through stock awards, option exercises, or stock sales. Dilution lowers this figure even when the free cash generated does not change.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Free cash flow per share is the closest measure to what an owner could actually pocket from a share each year. Earnings include non-cash estimates, and operating cash flow ignores the equipment the business must buy. Free cash flow per share corrects both, leaving the cash that is genuinely available after the business has funded everything it needs to keep running.

Free cash flow per share is the foundation of valuation. The value of any share is the cash it can produce for its owner over time, discounted back to today. This app's discounted cash flow models, its dividend models, and its intrinsic-value estimates all rest on free cash flow. Reading it on a per-share basis ties the company's whole cash engine to the single unit an investor buys.

Free cash flow per share also tests whether payouts are sustainable. Dividends and buybacks should be funded from free cash flow, not from borrowing or from selling assets. A company whose dividend exceeds its free cash flow per share is paying owners with money it does not have, which cannot continue forever.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Two forces move the top number. The first is operating cash flow. Operating cash flow rises with sales, with margins, and with faster collection of customer bills. Operating cash flow falls when cash is trapped in inventory or unpaid bills. The second is capex. A company that spends heavily on new equipment will show low or negative free cash flow even when its operating cash is strong. A company that cuts capex will show a jump in free cash flow, but cutting essential capex starves the future to flatter the present.

The split between maintenance capex and growth capex matters. Maintenance capex keeps the existing business running, while growth capex builds new capacity. The reported number combines both, so a company investing heavily for growth can show weak free cash flow that is actually a sign of opportunity, not weakness. The bottom number moves with the share count. Buybacks raise free cash flow per share, while stock-based pay and option exercises dilute it.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as strong free cash flow per share depends heavily on how much equipment the business needs. The comparison must stay inside one sector.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and asset-light firms | High free cash flow per share | Little equipment is needed, so most operating cash becomes free cash. |
| Heavy manufacturers and utilities | Low or thin free cash flow per share | Large, constant capex consumes most of the operating cash. |
| Fast-growing companies | Negative free cash flow per share | Growth capex exceeds operating cash while the company builds out. |
| Commodity producers | Highly volatile | Free cash flow swings between large positive and negative as prices and capex cycles move. |
| Retailers | Moderate and lumpy | Store expansion drives capex up in waves, then settles. |

A cross-sector comparison of the raw figure is meaningless because capex needs differ so widely. A software firm and a utility cannot be judged against each other on free cash flow per share. Compare the trend and the consistency within one sector, and judge a cyclical producer across a full cycle rather than in one year.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in free cash flow per share is the strongest signal a business can send, because it means the company is producing more genuinely spendable cash for each owner after all necessary spending. A flat line while operating cash flow rises means capex is climbing as fast as the cash coming in, so owners gain nothing extra. The key question on the chart is always whether free cash is growing or being absorbed by rising investment.

Negative free cash flow per share needs context. During a heavy growth phase, negative free cash flow can be a sign of opportunity, provided the investment eventually produces returns. Negative free cash flow without growth, or year after year, is a warning that the business cannot fund itself. A sudden jump in free cash flow often comes from a cut in capex, so check whether the cut reflects genuine efficiency or a starving of the future. A figure that rises while total free cash flow is flat is a buyback story, not a business story.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### OCF Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Operating cash flow per share counts cash before equipment spending, and free cash flow per share counts cash after it. Both divide by the same share count, so the gap between them is the per-share cost of capex. Reading the pair isolates how much of the operating cash the business must reinvest just to keep going.

**What the combination tells you.** A small gap means the business is asset-light and most of its operating cash reaches owners as free cash. A large gap means capex is consuming most of the operating cash before owners see any. A gap that widens over time warns that rising equipment costs are eroding the free cash that funds dividends and buybacks, even when operating cash flow still looks healthy.

### Capex Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Capex per share is the exact amount subtracted from operating cash flow to reach free cash flow per share. The two are directly linked, since free cash flow per share equals operating cash flow per share minus capex per share. Reading them together shows how much of the cash engine is being plowed back into physical assets.

**What the combination tells you.** Rising capex per share that lifts free cash flow per share later means the investment is paying off. Rising capex per share that never lifts free cash flow means the spending is failing to earn a return, or is merely maintaining a capital-hungry business. Watching the two lines together separates productive investment from a treadmill of spending that owners never benefit from.

### P/FCF

**Why read the two together.** The price-to-free-cash-flow ratio divides the share price by free cash flow per share, so this figure is its denominator. The two are mechanically linked, and the ratio shows how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of distributable cash. Reading them together connects the cash a share produces to the price the market sets on it.

**What the combination tells you.** A low ratio built on temporarily high free cash flow, perhaps from a capex cut, is a trap because the free cash will fall back. A high ratio paired with steadily rising free cash flow per share can be justified by the growth. Watching the quality and stability of free cash flow per share alongside the ratio prevents mistaking a one-time cash spike for cheapness.

## Part 8: Limitations

Free cash flow per share can be inflated by cutting the capex that the business actually needs. Slashing equipment spending raises free cash flow this year while quietly weakening the company's ability to compete later, and the reported number does not separate essential maintenance from optional growth investment. A single strong year can also be driven by a one-time release of working capital that will not repeat, so the figure should be read across several years rather than in isolation.

Free cash flow per share swings sharply for companies with lumpy investment cycles, where a big project can push it deeply negative for a year and then sharply positive afterward. The figure also says nothing about the debt behind the business or about whether the free cash is reinvested wisely. For a young company in a heavy growth phase, negative free cash flow can be healthy, which means the sign alone does not tell an owner whether the business is strong or weak.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is free cash flow per share positive and growing, or is rising capex absorbing the operating cash? Which way is the trend moving?
2. How large is the gap between operating cash flow per share and free cash flow per share? Is that gap widening as capex rises?
3. If free cash flow is negative, is the cause heavy investment for growth or a business that cannot fund its own equipment needs?
4. How much of the reported capex is maintenance to keep running, and how much is growth investment that could be paused?
5. Did a jump in free cash flow come from genuine efficiency or from cutting capex below the level the business needs?
6. Over the chart's span, did the share count rise or fall? Were buybacks lifting this figure, or was dilution holding it back?
7. Do dividends and buybacks fit inside free cash flow per share, or is the company paying owners with borrowed money?
8. In the company's worst year on the chart, did free cash flow stay positive? How does it hold up across a full business cycle?
9. How does free cash flow per share compare with direct competitors over the same years, after accounting for each one's capex needs?
10. Is a one-time working-capital swing flattering or depressing the latest figure? What does free cash flow per share look like averaged over several years?
