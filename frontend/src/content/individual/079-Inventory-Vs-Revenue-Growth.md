# Inventory Vs Revenue Growth

## Part 1: Definition

This check compares two growth rates that should move together. The first is the growth of inventory. Inventory is the stock of goods the company holds to sell, including raw materials, partly finished goods, and finished products. The second is the growth of revenue, which is the total value of sales the company recorded for the year. The check answers one question: is the stock of goods growing at the same speed as the sales it is meant to support?

In a normal business the two rise in proportion. The check passes when inventory grows in line with revenue. The check flags when inventory grows much faster, because that gap means goods are piling up faster than customers are buying them.

| Pattern | What that pattern usually means |
|---|---|
| Inventory and revenue grow at about the same rate | Goods are moving in step with sales. Ordinary. |
| Inventory grows somewhat faster than revenue | Stock is building ahead of demand. Worth a closer look. |
| Inventory grows far faster than revenue for one year | Often a planned build for a launch, a supply-shock restock, or an acquisition. Check the cause. |
| Inventory outruns revenue for several years in a row | A drift in one direction. The forensic literature treats this as a sign of weak demand or costs being hidden in goods. |

The check watches a different problem than its receivables twin. Receivables growing faster than revenue points at revenue quality, meaning sales booked ahead of reality. Inventory growing faster than revenue points at two things at once. Products may not be selling as planned, which is a demand problem. Or costs may be parked in inventory instead of flowing through earnings, which is an earnings-quality problem.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The check computes two growth rates and compares them:

`Divergence = Inventory Growth − Revenue Growth (flag when clearly positive)`

Inventory growth is the year-over-year percentage change in the inventory balance from the balance sheet, which is the statement listing what the company owns and owes at one date. Inventory belongs in the comparison because the goods a company holds should scale with the sales they support.

Revenue growth is the year-over-year percentage change in revenue from the income statement. Revenue is the yardstick because inventory exists to be sold. When inventory growth runs far ahead of revenue growth, the bulge in goods is probably not justified by demand.

This comparison is the matched-pair form of days inventory outstanding. Days inventory outstanding measures the average number of days goods sit in stock before they are sold, measured against the cost of goods sold rather than revenue. Inventory growing faster than revenue is the same fact as days inventory outstanding lengthening. The composition split is the deeper tool here. Raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods carry different meanings, so the footnote that breaks inventory into those three parts turns one flag into a direction. Finished goods building up points at a demand miss. Raw materials building up points at a purchasing decision. Work in progress building up points at a process or mix change.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The comparison carries two warnings. The first is about demand. Inventory piling up against plan is the earliest statement-level evidence that sell-through has disappointed, meaning goods are not reaching the final customer as expected. The buildup often appears quarters before the revenue slowdown and the discounting that confirm it. The sequence runs from accumulation to markdowns to write-downs. A write-down is a charge that lowers the recorded value of unsold goods.

The second warning is about earnings quality. Inventory is where costs can hide. A manufacturer can overproduce to spread fixed factory costs across more units, which parks part of the cost in the balance sheet and lifts today's reported margin. Obsolete stock can be left unwritten-down. Period costs can be capitalized into inventory, meaning recorded as part of the goods rather than as an immediate expense. Each trick inflates current profit by storing its cost in goods, and each leaves the same residue of inventory growing beyond what sales explain.

The check cannot tell the two warnings apart by itself. Both are bad in different ways, and separating them is the work of the diagnosis.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The demand-side causes build up finished goods. Sell-through can miss plan. Fashion and technology cycles can turn against a product. Channel partners can stop restocking while the factory keeps producing.

The accounting-side causes inflate margin now and confess later. Overproduction absorbs factory overhead into inventory, so the margin looks better today and the cost arrives when the units finally sell or are written down. Obsolescence can be recognized too slowly. Period costs can be capitalized into goods to keep them off the income statement.

Several innocent causes raise inventory growth for honest reasons. A company may build stock ahead of a dated, announced product launch. A company may hold more stock after a supply shock as a deliberate policy. An acquisition imports a whole inventory balance at once and breaks the comparison. Commodity-cost inflation raises the recorded value of inventory without any growth in units, so the price-versus-volume split is worth making. Long-cycle work in progress grows with the mix of contracts in process.

One artifact cuts the other way. A write-down year shrinks the inventory base, which flatters the following year's comparison. The cleanup can create the appearance of discipline where none has returned.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The check bites where physical goods are the business and says little where they are not.

| Industry type | How the check behaves | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Retail, apparel, consumer electronics, semiconductors, autos, manufacturing | Full weight | Goods are central, and each sector has a documented history of this signature, such as fashion risk or fast product cycles. |
| Services, software, financials | Silent | Inventory is tiny or absent, so the absence of a flag carries no information. |
| High-fixed-cost manufacturers | Watch for overproduction | Spreading factory overhead across more units makes the cost-parking trick structural. |
| Commodity-input producers such as food, chemicals | Split price from volume first | Input-cost inflation moves inventory dollars with no change in units, so a dollar-based reading misleads. |

Compare a company only against peers in the same sector. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two industries hold goods for different lengths of time, because the normal relationship between inventory and revenue differs by business model.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows this check across several years. A single flagged year with an announced, dated cause resolves as ordinary business. A launch build or an acquisition is the usual example, and the following quarters' sell-through is the verification.

The pattern that matters is persistence. Inventory outrunning revenue across consecutive years is a buildup that ordinary timing does not produce. The full demand sequence is readable across this page's charts. The inventory divergence comes first, then the gross margin softens as discounting begins, then the write-down completes it.

Watch the interplay with reported margins closely. The overproduction trick shows up as inventory diverging while margins are unusually strong, because the costs are hiding in the goods. That combination is more suspicious than either signal alone. The years after a write-down need the artifact adjustment, because the shrunken base flatters comparisons exactly when scrutiny should stay high.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Inventory Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days inventory outstanding measures the same fact as this check but expresses it as a number of days rather than a growth gap. Inventory growing faster than revenue is identical to days inventory outstanding rising. The days inventory outstanding entry carries the composition split and the full diagnostics this check only summarizes. Reading the two together converts a flag into a direction.

**What the combination tells you.** When this check flags and days inventory outstanding is also climbing, the buildup is confirmed. The composition split then shows whether finished goods, raw materials, or work in progress is driving it. When this check flags but days inventory outstanding is flat, an acquisition or a measurement change is the more likely cause. The pair separates a real buildup from an artifact. Together they point to exactly which goods to examine.

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross margin is revenue minus the cost of goods sold, divided by revenue, and it is both the next domino and the hiding place. A demand-driven inventory buildup usually leads to discounting, which softens the margin later. A cost-parking buildup props the margin up now by keeping costs in inventory. The same inventory divergence can precede a margin fall or accompany a margin that is suspiciously strong.

**What the combination tells you.** Inventory diverging followed by a softening margin confirms the demand story, because unsold goods are being marked down. Inventory diverging alongside an unusually strong margin flags the cost-parking story, because the costs that should lower the margin are sitting in the goods. A stable margin with a one-off inventory build points to a planned, explainable event. The margin tells you which of the two inventory warnings is in play.

### COGS / Inventory

**Why read the two together.** The ratio of cost of goods sold to inventory measures how many times the company sells through its stock in a year, which is the velocity of the inventory. This check sees the buildup as a growth gap, while the velocity ratio sees it as slowing turnover. The two describe the same accumulation from different angles.

**What the combination tells you.** When inventory diverges and the velocity ratio is falling, the goods are genuinely moving more slowly, which strengthens the demand concern. When inventory diverges but velocity holds, the higher stock is still selling through at the usual pace, which softens the concern. A sharp drop in velocity alongside the divergence is the clearest sign of a real buildup. The pair confirms whether the extra goods are actually stuck.

### Receivables Vs Revenue Growth

**Why read the two together.** The receivables check is the matched-pair twin on the sales side, comparing unpaid invoices against revenue. This check compares goods against revenue. Both describe a part of the working-capital cycle growing faster than sales. Channel stuffing tends to show in both at once, because product pushed to distributors swells goods and the unpaid invoices for them swell receivables.

**What the combination tells you.** When both checks diverge together, the combination is the full channel-stuffing signature, meaning goods pushed out and not paid for. When only inventory diverges, the problem is more likely weak demand or production running ahead of sales. When only receivables diverge, the problem sits on the revenue-recognition side instead. Reading the pair tells you whether the trouble is in the goods, the sales, or both.

## Part 8: Limitations

The aggregate comparison misses what the composition split reveals, so the footnote breakdown into raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods is the real instrument. Cost inflation moves inventory dollars without moving units, so the price-versus-volume split is mandatory at commodity-input companies. Acquisitions and write-downs both break the series, in opposite directions.

The cost-parking trick is visible to the check only at scale, because modest overproduction hides inside ordinary variance. The check is silent at inventory-light businesses, and that silence carries no information. A flag demands the physical questions that ratios cannot answer, such as what the goods are, where they sit, and what they will sell for. The check is the demand warning and the cost-parking detector in one comparison. The inventory entries around it are the investigation.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How many consecutive years has inventory outrun revenue, and is the gap widening or closing?
2. What does the composition split show across raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods, and which layer is driving the buildup?
3. Is there an announced, dated explanation such as a launch build or a stocking policy, and did later sell-through confirm it?
4. How much of the inventory growth is input-cost inflation rather than units, and does a volume story survive the price-versus-volume split?
5. Is the divergence accompanied by an unusually strong gross margin, the cost-parking signature, and what would a normal margin imply about costs waiting in the goods?
6. Is the receivables twin diverging at the same time, the full channel-stuffing pattern, and what does channel inventory evidence show?
7. For a manufacturer, how does production volume compare with shipment volume, and is output being run to absorb overhead rather than to meet demand?
8. How quickly could the accumulating goods become obsolete, and what would honest markdown timing recognize as an expense this year?
9. Did a past write-down shrink the comparison base anywhere on the chart, and is recent apparent discipline just the cleanup artifact?
10. If the excess inventory cleared at realistic markdowns, what gross margin and earnings would result, and does the current valuation survive that adjustment?
