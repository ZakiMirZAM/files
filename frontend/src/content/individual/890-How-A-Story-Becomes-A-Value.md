# How A Story Becomes A Value

## What it is

A story-based valuation starts with a plain-words account of how a business will develop and ends with a value per share. The value is intrinsic value, which is today's worth of the cash the business is expected to produce. Every number in the valuation carries a story link, a note saying which part of the story produced the number. The method answers one question: what is the company worth if the story you believe comes true?

The method runs in five steps. The story comes first because the numbers are only as good as the story behind them.

| Step | What happens | Where the step happens on this page |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Tell the story | Describe in plain words how the business will develop over the next ten years. Cover the market and the company's place in it. Then cover what that place earns, what growth costs and how the story could end. | The story questions in the Your Story section |
| 2. Test the story | Check that the story is possible, plausible and probable. A possible story breaks no rule of arithmetic. A plausible story has evidence that it can happen. A probable story is one you expect, with a reason behind the expectation. | The possible, plausible and probable question after the story questions |
| 3. Turn the story into value drivers | Every part of the story lands on exactly one valuation input. | The "Turn my story into numbers" button and its table |
| 4. Run the numbers | Once the inputs are set, getting from inputs to value is mechanical. | FCFF Firm Valuation with the switch on "My story's numbers" |
| 5. Keep the loop open | Find the weakest link and the strongest counter-story. Change the story when the facts change. | The lines under the conversion table |

## Where each part of a story lands

| If the story is about... | ...it shows up in |
|---|---|
| A big market | the total market, which becomes revenue growth |
| Network effects or dominance | market share |
| A lasting competitive advantage | the operating margin |
| Easy or costly scaling | sales to capital, which measures how much must be invested per dollar of new sales |
| Low or high risk | the cost of capital |
| Something that could end the business | a probability of failure, handled separately and not by raising the discount rate |
| How long the advantage lasts after growth ends | the return on capital in the terminal value |

A network effect exists when each new customer makes the product more useful to the other customers. A competitive advantage is anything that lets a company charge more than its rivals or produce for less, in a way rivals cannot copy. The operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue. The cost of capital is the yearly return that lenders and shareholders require for the risk they take. The terminal value is the value of every year after the ten forecast years, and the return on capital is the operating profit earned per dollar invested in the business.

## How this section works

The reader tells the story by answering the story questions in words. No question asks the reader to type a number. The questions follow the order of the table above. The first three cover the market, the growth of that market and whether size helps the company win. The next three cover what lets the company earn more, what growth requires and what the company cannot control. The last two cover what could end the story and how long the advantage lasts. A final question asks the reader to write the whole story in one piece.

The "Turn my story into numbers" button becomes active once the eight answers are saved. The AI then reads the answers alongside the company's own history, the range of results across its industry, and the model's current assumptions. The AI can also search the site's library of finance books for the rule behind each number. The AI returns one table with one row per input. Each row shows the words of the story the input came from, the number, and a proof that says why the number follows from the story.

The site then checks every proof against the figures the site computed itself. A row whose proof holds shows as "Verified". A row whose proof cites a figure that does not match the data, or cites no figure the site can check, shows as "Not supported". A "Not supported" input keeps the model's own number, so the AI never moves the valuation without proof.

The site also applies hard limits of its own, and the AI cannot override them. An input that breaks a limit keeps the model's number, and the table names the rule that was broken.

| Hard limit | Why the limit exists |
|---|---|
| Market share in year 10 cannot exceed 100 percent of the market the story named | No company can sell more than its whole market. |
| Growth after year 10 cannot exceed the risk-free rate | The risk-free rate stands in for the growth of the whole economy, and no company outgrows its economy forever. |
| The target operating margin must stay below 100 percent | Operating profit can never exceed revenue. |
| The return on capital after growth ends must be above the growth after year 10 | A lower return would require reinvesting all of the profit or more just to hold that growth. |

Beside the conversion table the site shows three more lines. The implied market share in year 10 is the projected revenue as a share of the market the story named. The weakest link is the one answer with the least evidence behind it. The strongest counter-story is the most plausible different story for the same company, told in two sentences with no numbers. The site also shows the return that the story's new investment would earn, beside the cost of capital, as a plain figure with no verdict attached.

The cost of capital itself never comes from the AI. The site builds the cost of capital from the company's beta, the live equity risk premium and the company's debt. The answer about outside forces is shown to the AI only so the AI can point out a risk the reader named that the calculation does not capture.

Once the story has been turned into numbers, a switch appears above FCFF Firm Valuation. The switch reads "Model's numbers" or "My story's numbers", and the page shows one value per share at a time. In story mode the projection always uses five years of high growth and then five years that fade in equal steps to stable growth. The existing assumption tiles, the Projected Statements table, the value bridge and the sensitivity grid all update to the story's numbers. When the story includes a chance that the business ends, the value per share weighs the value if the story works against what shareholders would receive if the story ends.

## Why it matters

Different values for the same company almost always come from different stories rather than from a disagreement about one input number. The same business can be worth a small amount or a very large amount depending only on which market it is in, how fast that market grows, whether size helps it win and what growth costs. Seeing the story behind each number turns a vague disagreement about value into a specific disagreement about a market, an advantage or a cost.

A story also keeps the inputs consistent with each other. Growth requires reinvestment, and faster growth usually brings more risk. A story that combines high growth with low reinvestment and low risk makes the company look valuable, but the parts of that story contradict each other. Tying every number to one story exposes the contradiction.

The story link also removes the easiest way to reach any value you want. When every input has a reason behind it, changing an input means retelling the story. A retold story must pass the same possible, plausible and probable test as the first one, so an input cannot quietly change without a new reason.

## Common traps

The first trap is a story built on hopes. At some point in a weak story the storyteller lets hopes replace expectations. A sentence with no reason behind it from the questions is a hope, and the conversion turns a hope into a number that looks as precise as any other number. A story built on hopes is the story most likely to break, so remove every sentence that has no reason behind it before converting.

The second trap is reading one number without its story link. A target margin of 30 percent means nothing on its own. The margin is either the result of a named advantage that survives competition or a wish. Read each number together with the words it came from and the proof beside it. Remember also that a "Not supported" row keeps the model's number, so the story value can mix story numbers with model numbers.

The third trap is treating the counter-story as a verdict. The counter-story is the most plausible different story, offered as something to argue against. The counter-story is not a prediction and not a ruling that your story is wrong. Listening to a counter-story does not require giving up your own story. Change your story when the facts change, not because a different story exists.
