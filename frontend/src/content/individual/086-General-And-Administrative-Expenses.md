# General And Administrative Expenses

## Part 1: Definition

General and administrative expenses are the costs of running the company that are not tied to making the product or selling it. The line covers head-office overhead. Examples are the salaries of senior managers, the finance and legal departments, office rent, and general insurance. The line is often shortened to G&A. The line is sometimes combined with selling costs into one line called SG&A, short for selling, general, and administrative. General and administrative expenses answer one question: how much does it cost just to keep the company organized and running, apart from making and selling its goods?

In the vertical analysis table this app shows, general and administrative appears as a percentage of revenue. A reading of 10 percent means ten cents of every revenue dollar went to running the head office. G&A is an operating expense, so the line sits below gross profit and reduces operating income. Much of this cost is fixed, meaning it stays roughly the same whether sales rise or fall, which makes the line important for understanding how profit reacts to changing revenue.

Common readings of the general and administrative line:

| G&A as a share of revenue | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Low single-digit percent | A lean, well-run head office that does not consume much of each sales dollar. |
| Mid to high single-digit percent | A normal range for many companies, depending on how complex the business is. |
| High, into double digits | A heavy overhead burden, which can signal complexity, weak cost control, or a small revenue base. |
| Falling as a share of revenue while sales grow | The company is spreading fixed overhead across more sales, a sign of operating leverage at work. |
| Rising as a share of revenue | Overhead is growing faster than sales, which warns of bloat or weakening cost discipline. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

General and administrative is not a ratio. The line is the total of all overhead costs the company classified as general and administrative during the period:

`General And Administrative Expenses = Total Overhead Cost Not Tied To Production Or Selling`

The line gathers the pay of executives and head-office staff. The line also gathers the cost of the finance and legal functions, office rent and utilities, professional fees, and general insurance. These are the costs of running the organization rather than the costs of making the product. The product costs sit in cost of revenue, and the costs of selling and marketing sit in their own line. The depreciation of head-office buildings and equipment also lands here, where depreciation is the yearly charge that spreads an asset's cost over its useful life.

The defining feature of this line is that most of it is fixed. A salaried manager is paid the same whether the company sells a little or a lot, so the cost does not move with each sale the way materials do. Because the cost is largely fixed, the line behaves differently from cost of revenue. When sales rise, fixed overhead is spread across more revenue, so the line shrinks as a share of revenue even though the dollar amount barely changes. The company decides which costs it classifies as general and administrative and discloses the choice in its annual report, and that boundary involves judgment.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

General and administrative expenses measure how efficiently the company is run away from the product itself. A lean overhead means more of each sales dollar survives as profit, while a bloated overhead drains profit no matter how good the product is. Reading the line tells an investor whether management keeps a tight grip on the cost of simply existing as an organization.

The line is also where management discipline shows up most clearly. Many head-office costs are discretionary and easy to grow when times are good, so a steadily rising general and administrative share can reveal a company losing its cost discipline. Because companies have room to present this figure flatteringly, an investor should study the trend in detail and compare it against similar companies rather than accept the reported number at face value.

The fixed nature of the line makes it central to understanding operating leverage, which is how sharply profit moves when revenue moves. A company with heavy fixed overhead sees profit jump when sales rise, because the overhead is already paid, but it also sees profit fall hard when sales drop, because the overhead still has to be covered. Reading general and administrative tells an investor how much of the cost base is fixed and therefore how the company's profit will breathe through good years and bad.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The main driver is the size and complexity of the organization. A company with many divisions, many countries, and a large head office carries heavy general and administrative costs, while a focused single-product company runs lean. Management's spending discipline sets the level directly, because much of the overhead is discretionary and can be raised or trimmed by decision. Wage inflation pushes the line up over time, since most of the cost is salaries. A merger or acquisition can swell the line by adding a second head office, which is why combined companies often promise to cut overlapping overhead afterward.

Several reporting choices move the number without the underlying cost changing. The boundary between general and administrative, selling costs, and cost of revenue is a judgment, so a company can shift the same cost between lines and change how the figure looks. Where the company places depreciation and certain shared costs affects the line. One-time items can swell a single year and should be separated from the ongoing run rate before reading a trend. Such items include legal settlements, restructuring charges, and executive severance.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a high or low general and administrative level depends on how the business is built.

| Industry type | Typical general and administrative behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and asset-light services | Often a large share of revenue | With little product cost, overhead and people become the main expense. |
| Manufacturers | Moderate share of revenue | Most cost sits in production, so head-office overhead is a smaller slice. |
| Retail | Low to moderate share of revenue | Cost is concentrated in goods and stores, leaving overhead a modest part of sales. |
| Conglomerates with many divisions | Higher share of revenue | Running many separate businesses multiplies head-office cost. |
| Banks and insurers | Reported differently | Overhead is large but blends into operating costs that are not split the same way as a product company. |

Comparing the general and administrative share across different industries is misleading, because each model carries a different natural level and each draws the boundary between cost lines differently. The comparison is only meaningful between direct competitors of similar size and structure. Even then, separate one-time charges from the ongoing overhead before reading the gap.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows general and administrative as a multi-year line and as a percent of revenue in the vertical table. The percent-of-revenue view is the most revealing, because it shows whether overhead is growing in step with the business. A general and administrative share that falls steadily while revenue grows is the sign an investor wants, because it means the company is spreading its mostly fixed overhead across more sales and turning growth into profit. This declining share is operating leverage working in the company's favor.

A general and administrative share that rises over time is a warning. Overhead is growing faster than sales, which points to bloat, weakening cost discipline, or a revenue base that has stopped growing while costs keep climbing. Watch for one-time items that fake a trend. A single year with a large legal settlement or restructuring charge can spike the line and then fall back, so a one-off year should not be read as a lasting change. Read the raw dollar line alongside the percentage, because a rising share with flat dollars usually means falling revenue rather than rising cost. Compare the trend against revenue to judge whether management is holding overhead steady as the company grows.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Leverage (DOL)

**Why read the two together.** General and administrative is mostly fixed cost, and fixed cost is what creates operating leverage. Operating leverage measures how sharply operating income moves when revenue moves. A company with heavy fixed overhead has high operating leverage, so reading the two together shows how the overhead structure will amplify changes in sales. The size of the fixed general and administrative base is one of the main reasons profit swings.

**What the combination tells you.** Heavy general and administrative with high operating leverage means profit will rise fast when sales grow but fall hard when sales shrink, which is powerful in good years and dangerous in bad ones. Light general and administrative with low operating leverage means profit moves gently with sales, which is steadier but slower to reward growth. Watching the overhead share fall as revenue rises confirms the leverage is working for owners. The pair shows how much of today's profit depends on sales staying high.

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** General and administrative is largely fixed, so it should not grow much when revenue grows. Reading the two lines together tests whether management is holding overhead steady as the business expands. If overhead grows as fast as revenue, the company is not gaining the efficiency that scale should bring. The relationship between the two reveals cost discipline directly.

**What the combination tells you.** Revenue rising while general and administrative stays roughly flat means the company is scaling efficiently and each new sale drops more profit to the bottom line. Revenue rising while general and administrative grows just as fast means the overhead is not really fixed, or management is letting cost creep, so growth is not creating leverage. Revenue falling while general and administrative stays high is the dangerous case, because the fixed cost crushes profit. The pair shows whether growth is being converted into efficiency.

### ROS

**Why read the two together.** ROS, the return on sales, is the profit left from each revenue dollar after all operating costs, including general and administrative. Overhead is one of the largest expenses standing between gross profit and operating profit. Reading the two together shows how much the head-office cost is eating into the final operating margin. A heavy overhead directly drags ROS down.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling general and administrative share lifting ROS means the company is converting better cost control into higher profitability. A rising general and administrative share dragging ROS down means overhead is the cause of a profit squeeze, which points the investor to where to cut. A stable overhead share with steady ROS confirms a disciplined, predictable business. The pair connects the overhead line to the operating profit it helps determine.

## Part 8: Limitations

General and administrative depends on where the company draws the boundary between overhead, selling costs, and product costs, and companies draw that boundary differently. A firm can shift the same cost between lines, which changes how lean the overhead appears without changing total profit. The reclassification makes the line treacherous to compare across companies without checking the accounting policy. The line is also a place companies can present flatteringly, so the reported figure should be studied against peers rather than taken at face value.

The line mixes ongoing overhead with one-time items, so a single year can be distorted by a legal settlement, a restructuring charge, or executive severance that will not repeat. Separating the recurring run rate from the one-offs is necessary before reading a trend. A low general and administrative share does not by itself prove a healthy company, because a business can run a lean head office while still failing at the product level. General and administrative is a useful measure of organizational discipline and never a complete measure of how good the business is.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is general and administrative rising, flat, or falling as a share of revenue over the years on the chart? Is overhead growing in step with sales or faster?
2. As revenue has grown, has the company spread its fixed overhead across more sales, or has the overhead grown just as fast?
3. How much of a recent change in the line came from one-time items such as legal settlements, restructuring, or severance, and what is the ongoing run rate without them?
4. How does the general and administrative share compare with direct competitors of similar size and structure? Is the company leaner or heavier?
5. Does the company classify overhead the same way as its peers, or does an accounting boundary make the comparison unreliable?
6. How much of the general and administrative cost is fixed, and how would profit hold up if revenue fell sharply for a year?
7. Did a recent acquisition swell the overhead with a second head office? Has the promised cost saving actually appeared in the line?
8. Has the overhead share crept up during good years in a way that suggests weakening cost discipline?
9. How did general and administrative behave in past downturns? Did management cut overhead to defend profit, or did the fixed cost crush operating income?
10. Does the trend in general and administrative line up with the trend in ROS, confirming that overhead is a real driver of the company's operating profit?
