# Implied Market Share

## Part 1: Definition

The implied market share check translates a growth rate into the scale it eventually demands. Compounding is deceptive: a yearly percentage sounds modest while the multiplication it performs does not. A company growing 26 percent a year grows tenfold in about ten years, and tenfold again in the ten after that. The check states the time to a tenfold in plain years, then places that pace beside the growth of the whole economy.

The check answers one question: whose revenue does this growth take? An economy growing 4 percent a year cannot contain many companies growing 26 percent for long. Every fast grower is either expanding its market or taking share from someone, and at tenfold scale the someone runs out. The engine shows this check as a warning beside any measured growth rate above 15 percent, and the warning never changes the valuation.

| Growth rate | Years to grow tenfold |
|---|---|
| 15 percent | About 16 years |
| 25 percent | About 10 years |
| 40 percent | About 7 years |
| 60 percent | About 5 years |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Years To Tenfold = ln(10) ÷ ln(1 + Growth Rate)`

The symbol ln is the natural logarithm, the mathematical inverse of compounding. The formula solves for the number of years at which one plus the growth rate, multiplied by itself that many times, reaches ten. No estimate is involved; the result is pure arithmetic on the growth rate the engine measured.

The comparison side uses the live growth of the economy. The app reads current nominal GDP growth from its market data, where nominal means measured in current money with inflation included, and GDP is the total output of the economy. When the live figure is unavailable the warning names the risk-free rate instead, since the risk-free rate is the long-run proxy for nominal economic growth. The warning then states both numbers side by side: the company's years to tenfold and the pace of the economy it must grow inside.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Growth assumptions fail at scale, not at the start. A 30 percent growth rate can be genuinely observed in a company's recent history and still be impossible to sustain, because the observation was made when the base was small. The check exposes the scale problem before it is priced into a valuation, by showing what the rate does to the company's size within an ordinary holding period.

The comparison against the economy supplies the missing ceiling. A company cannot outgrow its economy forever without becoming it, so a decade of tenfolding must be funded by market share taken from named competitors or by a market that is itself expanding violently. Either claim can be checked, and the warning tells the reader to check it.

The check also protects the terminal phase indirectly. A user startled by the tenfold arithmetic will look harder at the high-growth window and the transition, which is where the engine's structure absorbs a fading advantage.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Only the measured growth rate moves the years-to-tenfold figure, and the relationship is steeply nonlinear. Cutting growth from 40 to 25 percent adds only three years to the tenfold, while cutting from 20 to 15 percent adds five. Small differences in extreme rates matter less than they appear, and small differences in moderate rates matter more.

The benchmark side moves with the economy. In a high-inflation period nominal GDP growth is elevated and the same company growth looks less extreme against it. The comparison is nominal against nominal by design, since the company's measured growth is also stated in current money.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | How to read the warning | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and internet platforms | The market itself may be expanding | Tenfolding inside a category that is tenfolding takes no one's share. The claim shifts to the category's ceiling. |
| Consumer staples and utilities | The warning bites hard | End markets grow with population and prices, so extreme growth must be share taken from peers. |
| Semiconductors and hardware cycles | Judge across a full cycle | A rebound year can post extreme growth that is recovery rather than expansion. |
| Small companies anywhere | More room before the ceiling | A small base can tenfold inside a niche without straining the economy. The check matters most as the base grows. |
| Retail roll-ups | Count the physical ceiling | Store counts and locations give the tenfold a concrete limit that can be checked directly. |

The cross-check is meaningless for a company whose revenue is tied to an economy other than the benchmark's. A firm earning mostly abroad should be judged against the growth of the economies it actually sells into.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the company's own revenue history as a compounding record. A company that has already tenfolded once shows what its last decade of extreme growth required: the acquisitions, the geographic expansion, the capital raised. Asking whether all of that can happen again is far more concrete than debating a percentage.

Watch the growth rate's own trajectory too. Extreme growth that is already decelerating year by year is following the pattern the engine's transition phase assumes. Extreme growth that is stable or accelerating on a large base is the rarer and more fragile observation, and the warning deserves more weight there.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Revenue is the base the growth rate compounds from, and the check's meaning depends entirely on that base. A tenfold from fifty million is a niche success while a tenfold from fifty billion rearranges an industry. The warning states the pace; the revenue line states the starting scale.

**What the combination tells you.** A small base with extreme growth is the normal profile of a young winner, and the check mostly cautions about the later years of the forecast. A large base with extreme growth is the profile that breaks against the economy's ceiling fastest. Multiplying today's revenue by ten and asking who loses that amount makes the claim concrete.

### DCF intrinsic value per share

**Why read the two together.** The intrinsic value is built on the same measured growth rate the check interrogates. When the warning fires, part of the value rests on the years in which the tenfold arithmetic is at work. The pair connects the doubt to the number it should discipline.

**What the combination tells you.** A value that survives testing at a lower growth rate can absorb the market-share concern. A value that collapses without the extreme years is a bet on the tenfold happening, and the margin of safety should be read accordingly. The stage variants show this directly, since the shorter structures give the extreme rate less time to compound.

### Reinvestment Rate

**Why read the two together.** Growth at tenfold pace must be funded, and the reinvestment rate shows whether the company is paying for the growth it reports. Measured growth with matching reinvestment is a business scaling; measured growth without it will trip the separate imputed-return check. The two warnings triangulate the same doubt from different sides.

**What the combination tells you.** Extreme growth with heavy reinvestment is at least internally consistent, and the question becomes whether the market can absorb the output. Extreme growth with light reinvestment is doubly suspect, since neither the funding nor the market share adds up. Consistent funding does not make the tenfold plausible, but its absence makes the growth rate itself doubtful.

## Part 8: Limitations

The check compares against the whole economy, which is the bluntest possible market definition. A company can tenfold inside a fast-growing category without taking anyone's share, and the warning cannot see category boundaries. The benchmark is also a single domestic figure, while many companies grow across many economies at once.

The arithmetic says nothing about profitability. A company can win the implied market share and still create no value if the winning requires ruinous prices. The check flags scale, and scale is only one of the ways an extreme growth assumption can fail.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. At the measured growth rate, in what year does the company reach ten times today's revenue, and what market size does that imply?
2. Which competitors lose the revenue this growth takes, and what stops them from responding?
3. Is the company's addressable market itself growing fast enough to contain this pace without share gains?
4. How much of the company's revenue comes from economies growing faster than the benchmark used in the warning?
5. Has the company's growth rate already begun decelerating, and does the forecast's transition phase match that observed fade?
6. What did the company's last period of extreme growth require in capital and acquisitions, and can those conditions repeat at today's larger base?
7. How does the intrinsic value change when the growth rate is tested at half the measured level?
8. Does the reinvestment rate fund the claimed growth, or does the imputed-return check flag the same years this warning does?
