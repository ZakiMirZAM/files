# Share of Operating Cash Flow

## Definition

The portion of a year's operating cash flow that management directed to one particular use, expressed as a percentage. It converts a dollar amount into a priority.

## Formula

```
Share of operating cash flow = Use of cash / Operating cash flow
```

Where the use of cash is capital expenditure, research and development, acquisitions, share buybacks, dividends, or debt repayment for that fiscal year.

## Inputs

| Input | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Use of cash | The cash flow statement line for that use, taken as a positive amount of cash that left the company |
| Operating cash flow | Net cash provided by operating activities for the same fiscal year |

## Worked example

A company generates 100 billion of operating cash flow. It spends 12 billion on capital expenditure and 90 billion repurchasing its own stock.

```
Capex share    = 12 / 100 = 12%
Buyback share  = 90 / 100 = 90%
```

The dollar figures say the company invested twelve billion, which sounds substantial. The shares say something different and more useful. For every dollar of cash the business produced, twelve cents went back into the business and ninety cents went to buying its own stock. That is the priority, stated plainly.

## How to read it

| Pattern | Reading |
|---|---|
| Capex share rising while return on capital rises | Reinvestment is working. The additional spending is earning its keep |
| Capex share rising while return on capital falls | Overinvestment. Capital is going into projects below the cost of capital |
| Capex share falling and buyback share rising, with returns holding | A mature business rationally returning cash it cannot reinvest well |
| Acquisitions share rising while capex share falls | The company is buying growth rather than building it, at market prices |
| Dividends plus buybacks above 80% while capex sits below depreciation | Harvest. The asset base is being consumed rather than renewed |

The level in any single year says little. The drift across ten years says a great deal, because it shows how management's priorities changed as the business changed.

## Limits

The percentage is only meaningful when operating cash flow is positive. When a company burns cash, the denominator is negative and every share turns negative, which reads as the opposite of what happened. In those years no percentage is shown and only the dollar amounts are displayed.

The shares do not sum to 100%. A company can spend more than it earned in a year by drawing on cash reserves or by borrowing, and it can spend less and let cash accumulate. The residual is retained liquidity, and the gap between total uses and operating cash flow is itself informative.

## Why it matters

A dollar figure grows when the company grows. It cannot tell you whether management's priorities shifted. A share can. This is the reason the capital allocation literature works in percentages of operating cash flow rather than in absolute spending, and why the first chart in this section is the percentage view.
