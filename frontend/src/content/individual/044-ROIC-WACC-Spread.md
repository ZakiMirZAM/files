# ROIC − WACC Spread

## Part 1: Definition

The ROIC minus WACC spread measures whether a company earns more on its money than that money costs. ROIC is short for return on invested capital, the rate of profit the company makes on the capital invested in its operations. WACC is short for weighted average cost of capital, the rate that capital costs the company. Invested capital is the money supplied by lenders and shareholders to run the business. The spread is one rate minus the other, stated in percentage points.

The spread answers one question: does the company create value with the capital it uses, or just pass it through? A positive spread means each dollar invested earns more than it costs, so the business adds value for its owners. A spread of zero means the company earns exactly what its capital costs and adds nothing. A negative spread means the capital would have earned more invested somewhere else at the same risk.

| Spread level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | The company earns less than its capital costs. Growth at this level destroys value. |
| About zero | The company covers its capital cost and no more. Owners gain nothing beyond a fair return. |
| 1 to 5 points | A real surplus over the cost of capital. A healthy result for most companies. |
| Above 5 points | A wide surplus. The level usually signals a durable competitive advantage. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`ROIC − WACC Spread = Return on Invested Capital − Weighted Average Cost of Capital`

The first term is the return on invested capital. Return on invested capital is the company's after-tax operating profit divided by the capital invested in the business. The figure shows the rate the operations earn on the money put into them. A higher return means each invested dollar produces more profit.

The second term is the weighted average cost of capital. The cost of capital is the return that lenders and shareholders together require for supplying the money. Lenders require interest and shareholders require a return for the risk they take, and the cost of capital blends the two into one rate. The spread subtracts this required rate from the earned rate. What is left is the surplus return, the part of the company's return that exceeds what the capital demanded.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A high return on its own does not prove a company creates value. A company can earn a 12 percent return while its capital costs 14 percent, and that company is losing ground for its owners. The spread settles the question by placing the earned return next to the required return. Only the gap between them counts as value created.

The spread also reveals the quality of growth. Growth funded by capital that earns more than it costs raises the company's value. Growth funded by capital that earns less than it costs lowers it, even though revenue and profit may rise. A company can grow its operating income quickly and still destroy value if the new investments earn below the cost of capital. The spread shows which kind of growth is taking place.

A wide and steady positive spread usually points to a competitive advantage that keeps returns above the cost of capital year after year. Competition tends to push returns down toward the cost of capital over time. A spread that stays wide is a sign the company is protecting its position.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The spread moves with both of its parts. The return on invested capital rises with stronger margins or with more sales squeezed from the same capital. The return falls when profit weakens or when the capital base grows faster than profit. A large acquisition can lower the return by loading the capital base with goodwill, the premium paid above the value of the assets bought. The cost of capital moves with interest rates and with the risk investors attach to the business. When market interest rates rise, the cost of capital rises, and the spread narrows even if the company's return is unchanged.

Accounting choices can distort the return on invested capital, which distorts the spread. Old equipment carried at a low book value can make the capital base look small and the return look high. One-time gains or losses inside operating profit move the return for reasons unrelated to the ongoing business. The cost of capital is an estimate rather than a measured number, so a low estimate widens the spread and a high estimate shrinks it without any change in the business.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

A positive spread is the same goal in every industry, but the size that is achievable differs. Industries with strong brands or patents tend to hold wider spreads. Commodity industries tend to run spreads near zero because competition strips away any surplus return.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and branded consumer goods | Often wide and durable | Patents, brands, and switching costs protect returns above the cost of capital. |
| Established industrials | Modest and cyclical | Returns clear the cost of capital in good years and can fall below it in downturns. |
| Commodity producers | Near zero over a cycle | Prices are set by the market, so no producer holds a lasting surplus return. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Thin by design | Regulators allow a return close to the cost of capital and little more. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Capital and financing are the product itself, so the standard cost-of-capital comparison does not apply. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when two companies face very different capital costs and competitive structures. Judge the spread against direct competitors and across a full business cycle.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady positive spread that holds year after year is the strongest pattern. A durable spread means the company keeps earning above its capital cost despite competition, which usually marks a real advantage. A spread that narrows over time is a warning. A narrowing spread means returns are falling toward the cost of capital, often because competitors are catching up or because the company is investing in projects that earn less than the old ones.

A step change usually marks an event. A large acquisition can drop the spread in one year by diluting the return on capital. A rise in market interest rates lifts the cost of capital and narrows every company's spread at once, so a falling spread across a whole sector may reflect rates rather than any single business. High volatility points to a cyclical company whose return swings with the economy. Watch whether a positive spread survives the worst year on the chart, because a spread that turns negative in downturns sizes the real risk of the business.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** The return on invested capital is the first term of the spread. Reading the return on its own shows the raw rate the company earns before the cost of capital is subtracted. The spread then nets that return against what the capital costs.

**What the combination tells you.** A high return with a narrow spread means the cost of capital is also high, often because the business is risky or rates have risen. A modest return with a positive spread means the capital is cheap enough that even a moderate return creates value. Reading both separates how much the company earns from how much it had to clear.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** The weighted average cost of capital is the second term of the spread. Reading the cost of capital on its own shows the hurdle the company's return must clear. The spread reports by how much the return clears it or falls short.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising cost of capital narrows the spread even when the return holds steady, so a shrinking spread can come entirely from the cost side. A falling cost of capital widens the spread without any operating improvement. Watching the cost of capital next to the spread shows whether a change in value creation came from the business or from the financial markets.

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** The EVA value is the dollar version of the spread. EVA equals the spread multiplied by the capital invested, so the two move together. The spread states value creation as a rate, and EVA states the same value creation as a dollar amount.

**What the combination tells you.** A positive spread on a small capital base produces a modest EVA, while the same spread on a large base produces a large EVA. A narrow spread on a huge capital base can still create substantial value in dollars. Reading both shows whether value creation is wide per dollar or large in total.

## Part 8: Limitations

The spread depends on an estimated cost of capital. The cost of capital is not observed directly, so a small change in the estimate moves the spread. A low estimate makes almost any company look like a value creator, and a high estimate makes almost any company look like a value destroyer. Both terms also rest on accounting figures. Old assets carried at low book value can inflate the return on invested capital, which inflates the spread, so a wide spread can reflect an aged asset base rather than a strong business.

The spread is a measure of value created on capital already in place, and it says nothing on its own about how much capital the company can deploy at that spread in the future. A wide spread on a small business is worth less than a modest spread on a large one. The spread also uses a single cost of capital for the whole company, even though different parts of a company can face different risks. A negative spread in one year does not condemn a business that is early in a heavy investment phase whose returns have not yet arrived.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current spread driven by a high return on capital, a low cost of capital, or both? What does the spread become if the cost of capital is set higher?
2. How much of the invested capital is goodwill from acquisitions? What is the spread on the operating assets alone?
3. Has the spread stayed positive across the worst year on the chart, or does it turn negative in downturns?
4. Is the return on invested capital inflated by an old, heavily depreciated asset base?
5. Is operating profit being lifted by one-time gains that will not repeat? What is the spread without them?
6. Is the company growing while the spread narrows? Are the new investments earning less than the cost of capital?
7. How does the spread compare with direct competitors that face similar capital costs?
8. Has a recent rise in market interest rates narrowed the spread, or did the narrowing come from the business itself?
9. How much new capital can the company deploy at the current spread? Is the advantage scalable or limited to a small base?
10. If the spread is wide, what protects it from competition, and how long has it stayed wide on the chart?
