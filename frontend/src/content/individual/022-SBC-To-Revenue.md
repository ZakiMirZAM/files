# SBC To Revenue

## Part 1: Definition

The SBC To Revenue ratio measures how much of a company's sales is paid out as stock-based compensation. Stock-based compensation is shortened to SBC. SBC is the value of shares, stock options, and restricted stock units granted to employees as pay instead of cash. A stock option is the right to buy a share later at a fixed price. A restricted stock unit, shortened to RSU, is a promise to deliver shares to an employee after a waiting period. The ratio answers one question: how heavily does the company rely on giving away ownership to pay its people?

A ratio of 5 percent means the company hands employees stock worth five cents for every dollar of sales. A ratio of 20 percent means one fifth of all sales value is paid out in stock. The figure is shown as a percent of revenue so companies of different sizes can be compared on the same scale.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 2 percent | Stock pay is a minor cost. Most mature companies sit here. |
| 2 to 5 percent | A noticeable but normal level for many growing companies. |
| 5 to 10 percent | Stock pay is a large cost. Common in technology firms and worth close attention. |
| Above 10 to 15 percent | Stock pay is very heavy. The company leans hard on ownership to pay staff, and dilution is likely significant. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`SBC To Revenue = Stock-Based Compensation ÷ Revenue`

Stock-based compensation is the top number. SBC is the expense a company records for the shares, options, and RSUs granted to employees during the period. SBC is a non-cash expense, which means no cash leaves the company when the expense is booked. The company hands over shares rather than money, so the cost is real even though it never appears as a cash payment.

Revenue is the bottom number. Revenue is the total sales the company made during the period. Revenue belongs in the formula because it scales the stock cost against the size of the business. A large stock-pay figure means little until it is measured against how much the company actually sells.

SBC is found on the income statement as part of operating expenses. SBC is also added back on the cash flow statement, because it was subtracted as an expense yet no cash was spent.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Stock-based compensation is a real, recurring cost of running the business. The cost escapes casual readings because it is non-cash, so it does not lower the cash a company reports as spent. The SBC To Revenue ratio drags this hidden cost back into view and sizes it against sales.

The ratio answers a question that cash-based measures hide. When a company pays staff in stock, it transfers ownership from existing shareholders to employees. The transfer is called dilution. Dilution means the number of shares rises, so each existing share owns a smaller slice of the company.

A high ratio warns that the owners are slowly handing the company to its workforce. A low ratio means most pay is cash, so ownership stays with the shareholders. The ratio lets an investor judge how fast that ownership is leaking away.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move the number right now. The pace of hiring matters, because more employees usually means more grants. The mix of cash pay versus stock pay matters too, since a company can choose to pay more in stock to save cash. A younger or fast-growing company often leans on stock to attract talent it could not afford in cash.

The company's share price also moves the ratio. Grants are often set at a fixed dollar value, so a falling share price means the company must hand over more shares to deliver that same value. The sector matters as well, because some industries pay in stock far more than others.

Buybacks do not remove the SBC expense. A buyback is when a company purchases its own shares in the market and cancels them. Buybacks only offset the rise in share count caused by new grants. The expense still sits on the income statement, so the ratio does not fall just because the company is buying shares back.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level depends heavily on the industry. Stock pay is common in some sectors and rare in others.

| Industry type | Typical SBC/Revenue behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and technology | High, often 5 to 20 percent or more | Talent is scarce and expensive. Stock grants attract staff while saving cash. |
| Mature industrials and consumer staples | Low, often under 2 percent | Most pay is cash. The workforce is not bid up by stock-heavy rivals. |
| Early-stage and pre-profit firms | Very high, can exceed profit margins | These firms hold little cash, so they pay heavily in stock and the ratio can run extreme. |
| Banks and capital-intensive firms | Low | Revenue is large relative to staff, so stock pay forms a small share of sales. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two companies sit in different pay cultures. Always compare a company against direct peers in the same industry, never a software firm against a utility.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in the ratio means stock pay is growing faster than sales. The company is leaning more on ownership to pay staff each year, and dilution is building. A steady decline means the opposite, that stock pay is shrinking against sales or that the company is shifting back toward cash.

A step change, where the ratio jumps in one year, often points to a large new grant or a sudden change in pay policy. High volatility from year to year makes the ratio hard to trust on any single reading.

Watch for base effects. A falling share price can inflate the ratio without the company granting any more generously. The company hands over more shares to deliver the same dollar value, so the expense rises even though the policy has not changed. Always check whether a rising ratio reflects more generosity or just a weaker share price.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### EPS

**Why read the two together.** EPS means earnings per share, the company's profit divided by the number of shares. Stock-based compensation creates dilution, which raises the share count over time. A rising share count pushes EPS down even when total profit holds steady. Reading SBC To Revenue beside EPS shows whether stock pay is quietly eroding the per-share result.

**What the combination tells you.** A high SBC To Revenue ratio paired with flat or falling EPS means dilution is eating the gains that shareholders should see. A low ratio with rising EPS means profit growth is reaching each share cleanly. When EPS climbs while heavy stock pay continues, check whether buybacks are masking the dilution rather than the business truly improving per share.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the cash a company has left after running the business and paying for equipment. SBC is added back to operating cash flow because it used no cash, so it flatters free cash flow. A company can show strong free cash flow while paying staff heavily in stock that never appears as a cash cost.

**What the combination tells you.** A high SBC To Revenue ratio alongside strong free cash flow means part of that cash strength comes from not paying employees in cash. The free cash flow looks better than the true economic cost of the workforce. Reading the pair shows how much of the reported cash strength rests on shifting pay into stock instead of cash.

### Net Margin

**Why read the two together.** Net margin is profit after all costs shown as a percent of revenue. SBC is expensed on the income statement, so it is already subtracted inside net margin. Reading SBC To Revenue beside net margin shows how large a bite stock pay takes out of profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A high SBC To Revenue ratio sitting inside a thin net margin means stock pay is a major reason profit is small. If the ratio is large and the margin would look healthy without it, the company depends on stock pay to function. Comparing the two over time shows whether the company is growing into its stock costs or being weighed down by them.

### Revenue Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Revenue per share is total sales divided by the number of shares. Stock-based compensation raises the share count through dilution, which drags revenue per share down. Reading SBC To Revenue beside revenue per share shows whether sales growth is reaching each share or being diluted away.

**What the combination tells you.** A high SBC To Revenue ratio with stagnant revenue per share means new shares are being created as fast as sales grow. Shareholders see little benefit per share despite a bigger business. A low ratio with rising revenue per share means sales growth is concentrating into each existing share rather than spreading thin across new ones.

## Part 8: Limitations

The SBC To Revenue ratio rests on a non-cash cost, so it is excluded from many cash measures and can understate the true cost of pay there. The expense value itself is an estimate, produced by option-pricing models that guess how much a grant is worth. Two companies can value similar grants differently, which weakens comparison across firms.

The ratio says nothing about who receives the stock. A package aimed mostly at a few senior executives reads the same as one spread widely across staff. The ratio also ignores the actual dilution that remains after buybacks offset some of the new shares. A company buying back stock can dilute owners far less than the raw SBC figure suggests, and the ratio cannot see that.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Why is the SBC To Revenue ratio at its current level? Is it driven by heavy hiring, a deliberate choice to pay in stock, or a falling share price that inflates the figure?
2. Has the ratio risen or fallen over the past several years? Does the trend reflect a real change in pay policy or just a moving share price?
3. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors in the same industry? Is the company more or less reliant on stock pay than its peers?
4. After buybacks are subtracted, how much net dilution actually remains? Is the share count still rising despite the company repurchasing stock?
5. Who receives most of the grants? Is the stock concentrated among a few senior executives or spread across the wider workforce?
6. Is the company's free cash flow flattered by adding SBC back? How much weaker would the cash picture look if stock pay were treated as a cash cost?
7. Does the net margin still hold up once the size of the stock cost is considered? Would the margin look very different without SBC?
8. Is revenue per share growing or being diluted away by new shares created through grants?
9. How does the company describe its stock pay in its filings? Does management treat SBC as a normal cost or downplay it as a one-off?
10. If the share price fell sharply, how much would the company need to increase grants to keep delivering the same dollar value of pay?
