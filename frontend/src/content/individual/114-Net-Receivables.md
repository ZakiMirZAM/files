# Net Receivables

## Part 1: Definition

Net receivables is the money customers owe the company for goods or services already delivered but not yet paid for. Most sales between businesses happen on credit. The company ships the product, sends an invoice, and waits to be paid, often within 30 to 90 days. Until the customer pays, the unpaid invoice is an asset. The word net means the figure has been reduced by an estimate of the amounts the company expects never to collect. That estimate is called the allowance for doubtful accounts. Net receivables is the gross amount owed minus that allowance.

The balance sheet is the statement that lists what a company owns, what it owes, and what is left for owners. Net receivables sits in the current assets, the assets expected to convert to cash within one year. The line is the company's side of the credit it extends to customers as a cost of doing business. In the vertical analysis table the app shows net receivables as a percent of total assets, the sum of everything the company owns. The percent shows how much of the whole company is tied up in money owed but not yet collected.

Common readings of the line as a percent of total assets:

| Receivables share of total assets | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The company sells mostly for cash or upfront, common in retail and subscriptions. |
| 5 to 15 percent | A normal level for companies that sell to other businesses on credit. |
| 15 to 30 percent | A credit-heavy business. Collection speed and customer quality matter a great deal. |
| Rising fast versus sales | A warning. Receivables growing faster than revenue can signal collection or revenue-quality problems. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Net receivables is reported directly, not computed from a ratio. The line is the gross amount owed minus the allowance:

`Net Receivables = Gross Accounts Receivable − Allowance For Doubtful Accounts`

Gross accounts receivable is the full amount customers owe on delivered sales. The figure adds up every unpaid invoice on the books at the reporting date. The amount belongs as an asset because the company has earned the revenue and expects to collect the cash.

The allowance for doubtful accounts is the company's own estimate of the share of those invoices that will never be paid. Some customers go bankrupt or dispute the bill. Subtracting the allowance gives a figure closer to the cash the company actually expects to receive. The allowance is an estimate set by management. The estimate is a place where judgment, and sometimes manipulation, enters the balance sheet.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Net receivables shows how much of the company's reported sales is still sitting as a promise rather than cash. A sale only becomes useful when the cash arrives. Revenue that piles up in receivables instead of converting to cash is weaker than revenue that is collected quickly. The line lets the investor see how much of recent business is still uncollected.

The line is a leading clue to revenue quality. A company under pressure to show growth can book sales to weak customers or relax its payment terms. Those tactics inflate revenue while loading the receivables line. Reading receivables against revenue is one of the oldest tests for sales that may not be real or may not be collected.

The line also measures working capital tied up in customers. Working capital is the money locked in day-to-day operations. Every dollar owed by a customer is a dollar the company has funded on the customer's behalf. A business with high receivables is lending to its customers interest-free, which ties up cash that could be used elsewhere.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Sales volume and credit terms move the line first. More sales on credit lift receivables in step with revenue. Longer payment terms, offered to win or keep customers, raise the line even when sales are flat. Tightening terms or pushing for faster payment lowers it.

Collection performance moves it next. A company that collects on time keeps the line in proportion to sales. A company whose customers pay late, or whose customers are weakening, lets the line swell. A sudden jump in receivables without a matching jump in sales often points to slowing collection.

Accounting estimates can distort the line. The allowance for doubtful accounts is set by management. Shrinking the allowance lifts net receivables and flatters profit, even though no more cash will be collected. A company can also sell its receivables to a third party for immediate cash, which is called factoring. Factoring shrinks the line near the reporting date and can hide a slow-collection problem.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

How large receivables should be depends on whether the business sells on credit and to whom.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Business-to-business manufacturers | High receivables share | Selling to other firms on 30 to 90 day terms builds a large balance. |
| Retailers and restaurants | Very low receivables share | Customers pay at the point of sale, so little is owed. |
| Subscription and software firms | Low to moderate share | Many collect upfront or monthly, though enterprise deals add receivables. |
| Construction and project firms | High and lumpy share | Large contracts bill in stages, so balances swing with project timing. |
| Banks and lenders | Not comparable | Loans to customers are the core product, governed by separate rules. |

A cross-sector comparison of receivables share is meaningless when one business sells on credit and another collects at the till. Compare a company with direct peers, and always read receivables against the company's own revenue trend rather than in isolation.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows a multi-year chart of net receivables. The single most useful test is the comparison with revenue. Receivables should grow at roughly the same pace as sales. A steady rise in receivables in line with revenue is normal and healthy. A rise in receivables that outpaces revenue is a warning, because the company is booking sales it is collecting more slowly, or to customers less able to pay.

A steady decline relative to sales usually means faster collection or tighter credit terms, which frees up cash. A step change in one year can mark a new large customer, a change in payment terms, or a sale of receivables to a third party. High volatility points to a project-based or seasonal business. The base effect to watch is total assets, since the receivables percent can fall even when the dollar balance grows if total assets grow faster. Read the dollar balance, the percent of total assets, and the comparison with revenue together. The app's days sales outstanding line turns this comparison into a single number of days.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Sales Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days sales outstanding measures the average number of days the company waits to collect a sale. The metric is built directly from the net receivables balance and revenue. Net receivables is the stock of money owed, and days sales outstanding turns that stock into a collection speed. Reading the two together converts a balance into a time.

**What the combination tells you.** A receivables balance that grows while days sales outstanding holds steady is simply tracking higher sales and is no cause for concern. A receivables balance that grows while days sales outstanding rises means collection is slowing and customers are paying later. Reading the two together separates healthy growth from a deteriorating collection problem that the raw balance alone could hide.

### Receivables Turnover

**Why read the two together.** Receivables turnover counts how many times in a year the company collects its average receivables balance. The metric is the mirror image of days sales outstanding and uses the same net receivables figure. A high turnover means fast collection, and a low turnover means slow collection. Reading turnover beside the balance shows the efficiency behind the stock of money owed.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling turnover alongside a rising receivables balance confirms that the company is collecting its sales more slowly over time. A stable turnover means the larger balance is just the natural result of a larger business. Reading the two together tells the investor whether the receivables line reflects growth or a worsening collection cycle.

### Income Quality (CFO/NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income quality compares cash from operations with reported net income. A buildup of receivables is a leading cause of profit that has not turned into cash. When receivables swell, reported income can run ahead of the cash actually collected. Reading the two together links the balance-sheet stock of unpaid sales to the cash backing of profit.

**What the combination tells you.** Low income quality alongside a fast-rising receivables balance is a strong signal that reported profit is being inflated by uncollected sales. Healthy income quality with a stable receivables balance means profit is converting to cash as it should. Reading the two together shows whether the receivables line is quietly hollowing out the quality of earnings.

## Part 8: Limitations

Net receivables depends on an estimate. The allowance for doubtful accounts is set by management, so the net figure can be flattered by shrinking the allowance even when collection is getting worse. The line shows a balance on one date and says nothing about which customers owe the money or how concentrated the exposure is. A single large customer who delays payment can distort the whole line, and the balance sheet does not reveal that risk on its own.

The line can also be managed near the reporting date. Selling receivables to a third party for cash shrinks the balance and can hide slow collection. Offering customers early discounts or pulling sales forward can move the figure between periods. Net receivables is a useful first read on collection and revenue quality. The line is a poor last word. The deeper risks live in the customer mix, the allowance assumptions, and the collection trend that a single number cannot show.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is net receivables growing faster than revenue over the years on the chart? If so, is the company collecting more slowly, selling to weaker customers, or pulling sales forward?
2. What has happened to days sales outstanding over the same period? Is the collection cycle stable, or is it lengthening?
3. How large is the allowance for doubtful accounts relative to gross receivables, and has it been shrinking? Could a smaller allowance be flattering both net receivables and profit?
4. How concentrated are the receivables among a few large customers? What would happen to the line if one major customer delayed or failed to pay?
5. Does the company sell or factor its receivables for cash near the reporting date? If so, is the reported balance hiding a slower true collection cycle?
6. Are payment terms with customers stable, or has the company extended terms to win sales? How much of the recent growth in receivables comes from looser terms?
7. How does the receivables share of total assets compare with direct competitors? Is the company extending more credit than its peers to sell the same product?
8. Does income quality confirm that profit is turning into cash, or is reported income running ahead of cash collected as receivables build?
9. Is the receivables line lumpy because of large staged contracts, and if so, where in the project cycle does the company stand now?
10. Have there been recent write-offs of bad receivables? Do they suggest the allowance was set too low in prior years?
