# EBITDA

## Part 1: Definition

EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA starts with operating income and adds back two non-cash charges. Operating income is the profit the business earns from normal operations before interest and taxes. Depreciation and amortization are the charges that spread the cost of long-lived assets across the years those assets are used. EBITDA answers one question: how much cash do the operations throw off before any claims from lenders, the government, or the wearing-out of assets?

EBITDA is a measure of operating cash-generating power, not a profit the company can keep. Interest, taxes, and the cost of replacing worn assets are all real, and EBITDA ignores all three on purpose. In the vertical analysis table EBITDA shows as a percent of revenue. That percent is called the EBITDA margin, and the margin measures how much of each sales dollar survives the operating costs before the capital and tax layers.

| EBITDA margin | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 10 percent | Thin operating economics. Common in low-margin retail and distribution. |
| 10 to 20 percent | A normal range for many established companies. |
| 20 to 40 percent | Strong operating economics, common in branded consumer and industrial firms. |
| Above 40 percent | Very high, common in software, media, and asset-light models. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`EBITDA = Operating Income + Depreciation + Amortization`

Operating income is the starting point. Operating income is revenue minus every operating cost. Those costs include the cost of goods, selling, research, administration, and the depreciation and amortization charge. The figure is measured before interest and taxes, so the figure already strips out the financing and tax layers. EBITDA begins here because the goal is to measure the operations alone.

Depreciation and amortization are added back. Both are non-cash charges, which means no cash leaves the company in the year they are recorded. Both were already subtracted inside operating income, so adding them back removes their effect. The result is a figure that ignores how the company's past asset spending is being charged off. EBITDA can also be built from net income. The reader adds back interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization in turn, which gives the same answer.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

EBITDA strips out four items that depend on choices outside the core operations. Interest depends on how much debt the company carries. Taxes depend on where the company operates and how it is structured. Depreciation and amortization depend on accounting choices about useful lives. Removing all four lets an investor compare the operating economics of two companies without those differences getting in the way.

EBITDA is the standard base for debt analysis. Lenders measure debt against EBITDA because EBITDA approximates the cash the operations produce to service that debt. The leverage and coverage measures elsewhere on this page, such as Net Debt / EBITDA, use EBITDA as their base for this reason.

EBITDA gives a quick read on operating strength, but EBITDA is not free cash. The figure ignores the cash the company must spend to replace its assets and the cash it owes in interest and tax. An investor uses EBITDA as a starting point, not as the final word on what the business earns.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The operating drivers move EBITDA first. Rising revenue, better pricing, and tighter cost control all lift the figure. Falling volumes and rising input costs pull it down. Because EBITDA sits above the depreciation charge, a company can show a rising EBITDA even while it lets its plant wear out, since the wearing-out is exactly what EBITDA adds back.

Accounting and reporting choices change EBITDA more than they change real cash. A company that capitalizes a cost, meaning it records the cost as an asset instead of an expense, keeps that cost out of operating income and therefore out of EBITDA. Many companies also report an adjusted EBITDA, where they add back items they call one-time, such as restructuring or stock-based pay. Adjusted EBITDA can be far higher than the plain figure, and the add-backs deserve a close look. One-off gains inside operating income lift EBITDA without any real change in the business.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

A high EBITDA margin is normal in some sectors and impossible in others.

| Industry type | Typical EBITDA margin | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and media | Very high, often above 30 percent | Few physical assets and low cost to serve each extra customer. |
| Branded consumer goods | 15 to 30 percent | Strong pricing power on a moderate cost base. |
| Retail and distribution | Low, often below 10 percent | High volume on thin margins. |
| Telecom and utilities | High EBITDA margin, but misleading | Huge depreciation is added back, so EBITDA overstates true profit. |
| Banks | Not used | Interest is the core business, so removing it makes no sense. |

A cross-sector comparison of EBITDA margin is meaningless when the two businesses need very different amounts of plant. A telecom firm and a software firm can show similar EBITDA margins while one must spend heavily to replace its network and the other spends almost nothing. Compare EBITDA margin only within a sector.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read EBITDA as a margin across the multi-year chart, not only as a raw number. A steady rise in the EBITDA margin means the operations are getting more efficient, through better pricing or tighter costs. A steady decline means the opposite, and the cost lines deserve a closer look. A flat margin on rising revenue means the business is scaling without losing efficiency, which is healthy.

A step change calls for a direct check of what changed. A sudden jump often follows an acquisition, a change in what the company adds back, or a one-off gain inside operating income. A sudden drop can follow a cost shock or the loss of a high-margin product. Watch the gap between EBITDA and operating income over time, because that gap is the depreciation and amortization charge. A widening gap means the asset base is growing, so EBITDA is increasingly hiding a real cost. Compare the plain EBITDA with any adjusted EBITDA the company reports, and treat a wide and growing gap between them as a warning that the add-backs are doing the heavy lifting.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net Debt / EBITDA measures the size of the company's debt in years of EBITDA. EBITDA is the bottom of that ratio. The ratio asks how many years of operating cash it would take to clear the net debt. A reader cannot judge whether a debt load is heavy without knowing the EBITDA that supports it.

**What the combination tells you.** A low ratio means the debt is small next to the operating cash the business produces. A high ratio means the debt is large and the company depends on EBITDA staying strong. When EBITDA is inflated by aggressive add-backs, the ratio understates the true debt burden. Reading the two together shows whether the debt is safe and whether the EBITDA that supports it is real.

### Depreciation And Amortization

**Why read the two together.** EBITDA adds depreciation and amortization back to operating income. The depreciation and amortization line is the exact amount added back. The size of that line decides how far EBITDA sits above true operating profit. A large charge makes EBITDA look much stronger than operating income.

**What the combination tells you.** A large and growing depreciation line means EBITDA increasingly overstates the profit the company can keep, because the company must keep spending to replace the assets being charged off. A small line means EBITDA is close to real operating profit. Reading the two together shows how much of the EBITDA strength is genuine and how much is an accounting add-back.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** EBITDA ignores the cash the company spends on equipment, the interest it owes, and the tax it pays. Free cash flow subtracts all of those. Comparing the two shows how much of the headline operating cash actually reaches the owners after the real bills are paid.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide gap between EBITDA and free cash flow means heavy capital spending, interest, or tax is consuming most of the operating cash. A narrow gap means EBITDA is a fair guide to cash generation. A company that reports strong EBITDA but weak free cash flow year after year is far less healthy than the EBITDA alone suggests.

## Part 8: Limitations

EBITDA leaves out three real costs, and the costs left out are the ones that decide whether a company survives. Interest must be paid or the company defaults. Taxes must be paid. Worn assets must be replaced or the business shrinks. A company can show strong EBITDA and still run out of cash once those bills are met. EBITDA is a starting measure of operating power, never a measure of what the business truly earns.

EBITDA is also easy to flatter. There is no single legal definition of adjusted EBITDA, so companies choose what to add back. Items called one-time can recur year after year. A company under stress often points to EBITDA precisely because EBITDA hides the interest and asset costs that are pressing on it. The figure tells the reader about the operations. The figure says nothing about debt, taxes, or the cash needed to keep the plant running.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is driving the current EBITDA margin: better pricing, tighter costs, or a one-off gain inside operating income?
2. How far does EBITDA sit above operating income? Is that gap, the depreciation and amortization charge, growing as the asset base grows?
3. Does the company report an adjusted EBITDA? What exactly is added back, and do those add-backs recur every year?
4. How much of EBITDA survives as free cash flow after capital spending, interest, and tax? Is the gap widening?
5. What was the EBITDA margin in the company's worst year on the chart? How far did the operations fall in that year?
6. Is EBITDA being lifted by capitalizing costs that competitors expense?
7. How does the EBITDA margin compare with direct competitors in the same sector at the same point in the cycle?
8. Does heavy depreciation make the EBITDA margin look strong while true profit after asset replacement is thin?
9. Have recent acquisitions raised EBITDA in absolute terms while the margin stayed flat or fell?
10. Is management presenting EBITDA to draw attention away from heavy interest, tax, or capital costs sitting below it?
