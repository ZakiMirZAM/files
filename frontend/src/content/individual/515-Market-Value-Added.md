# Market Value Added

## Part 1: Definition

Market Value Added is the present value of all the economic value added a company is expected to produce in the future. Economic value added, written EVA, is the profit a company earns above the full cost of the money invested in it. Present value means today's worth of a future amount, found by discounting that amount back at a rate. Market Value Added is also the premium of the whole firm's value over the money that has been put into the firm.

Firm value in this model equals invested capital plus Market Value Added. Invested capital is the debt and equity funding tied up in the business. Cost of capital is the yearly return that lenders and shareholders require for supplying that funding. A positive figure means the market expects the company to earn more than its cost of capital. Market Value Added is the place where a company's competitive advantage shows up as a number, because only returns above the cost of capital can lift a firm's value above the capital already invested in it.

Common readings of the figure:

| Figure level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The company is expected to earn about its cost of capital. The firm is worth close to the money invested in it. |
| Large and positive | The company is expected to earn above its cost of capital for years. The premium is the value added. |
| Negative | The company is expected to earn less than its cost of capital. The market values the firm below the money invested in it. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Market Value Added = Present Value of All Future EVA`

The right side is a stream of yearly economic profits brought to today's value. The app forecasts EVA for each year ahead. Each year's EVA is discounted back to the present at the weighted average cost of capital. The weighted average cost of capital, written WACC, is the blended yearly return that lenders and shareholders require. Beyond the explicit forecast the app adds a terminal piece, the present value of the economic profit assumed for all the later years. Adding the discounted forecast years and the terminal piece gives the figure.

The same figure can be reached two ways:

| Route | How it is measured | What the route shows |
|---|---|---|
| Forecast route | Discount each projected year's EVA, then add the terminal piece | The premium the model's assumptions justify |
| Market route | Enterprise value minus invested capital | The premium today's price is paying |

Enterprise value is the value of the whole business to all funders, debt and equity together. The forecast route is the one the app's EVA section computes. When the market route sits above the forecast route, the price is paying for more excess return than the model assumes.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Market Value Added splits a company's worth into two clean parts. One part is the capital already committed to operations. The other part is the value the company is expected to add on top of that capital. A stock priced far above its invested capital is, in this frame, paying mostly for added value rather than for substance in place.

The figure answers a question that a raw price or a raw profit number cannot. Is the premium over invested capital justified by returns above the cost of capital? A company that earns exactly its cost of capital creates no Market Value Added, and in this model is worth only the money put into it. Any value above that has to be earned by beating the cost of capital.

The figure also states the competitive-advantage question as a measurable amount. A durable advantage lets a company keep earning above its cost of capital, and that persistence is what a large positive figure prices in. When Market Value Added is negative, the market is saying the company is expected to earn less than its cost of capital.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three forces move the figure. The first is the gap between the return on invested capital and the cost of capital. Return on invested capital, written ROIC, is the after-tax operating profit a company earns for each dollar of capital employed. A wider gap between ROIC and the cost of capital lifts every year's economic profit and raises the figure. The second force is how long that gap is expected to last. The figure rewards growth only when the growth earns more than the cost of capital, so growth at the cost of capital adds nothing. The third force is WACC itself. A higher WACC raises the bar each year's profit must clear and discounts the future stream harder, so the figure falls twice over.

Accounting choices move the figure without the business changing. The way invested capital is measured shifts the split between substance and added value. Including goodwill, the premium paid above fair value in past acquisitions, charges the company for the prices it paid on deals and lowers the figure. Treating research spending as an expense rather than an investment shrinks invested capital and inflates the premium sitting on top of it. A single unusually strong or weak profit year moves the starting point the whole forecast is built on.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The figure means the most when a company has a clear, measurable capital base. Where the real capital is hard to count, the split between substance and premium blurs.

| Industry type | Typical behavior of the figure | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | Modest premium over a large capital base | Regulated returns sit near the cost of capital, so little value is added. |
| Capital-heavy industrials | Meaningful and measurable | A real capital base and steady returns let the figure discipline the value. |
| Acquisitive companies | Reveals whether deals paid off | Goodwill in the base tests whether past acquisitions earn their price. |
| Capital-light software and brands | Very large premium over a tiny base | Almost all the value is added value, which is real but leaves little capital to anchor it. |
| Banks and insurers | Distorted | Capital and its cost are set by regulation, so the charge behind EVA is not a clean operating measure. |
| Deep cyclical producers | Swings with the cycle | Profit at one point in the cycle distorts the excess return the figure capitalizes. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the companies use very different amounts of real capital. A software firm and a utility cannot be ranked on the same scale by this figure, because one is almost all premium and the other is almost all capital base.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The figure looks forward, but the app shows the realized economic profit that feeds it. The EVA Margin tile's history, multiplied by revenue, is the realized EVA trend. The realized trend shows whether the company has actually earned the excess that the current figure assumes will continue. A company that has never sustained a positive economic profit is a weak candidate for a large positive figure.

Read the market's premium against the model's premium. The market's premium is enterprise value minus invested capital. A steady rise in the model's figure means the expected excess return is growing, from a wider spread or a longer runway. A steady decline means competition is expected to erode the advantage. A sharp step usually traces to a change in WACC or in the terminal assumption rather than to the business itself. When the market's premium sits well above the model's, the price assumes more excess return than the forecast allows.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** EVA is the single-year building block of this figure. Each year's economic value added is one dollar amount of profit above the cost of capital. Market Value Added is the whole future stream of those yearly amounts brought to today's value. Reading the latest EVA shows the current size of the profit the figure projects forward.

**What the combination tells you.** A large positive EVA today supports a large positive figure, because the stream starts from a high base. An EVA near zero says the figure should sit close to invested capital, with little premium. A negative EVA says the company is destroying value right now, so any positive figure rests entirely on an assumed recovery. Watching the two together shows whether the premium is built on current results or on hope.

### ROIC − WACC Spread

**Why read the two together.** The ROIC minus WACC spread is the percentage engine behind the figure. ROIC is the return on invested capital, and the spread is that return minus the cost of capital. Market Value Added is this spread turned into dollars, projected forward and discounted. The live spread shows the current strength of the engine.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide positive spread today supports a projection that keeps the figure large for years. A spread near zero says the figure should be small, whatever the headline growth rate. A negative spread says the market's premium is betting on a turnaround the company has not yet delivered. The spread's track record calibrates how much faith a large figure deserves.

### Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Invested capital is the base the figure sits on top of. Invested capital is the debt and equity funding tied up in operations, usually counted net of spare cash. Firm value in this model is invested capital plus the figure. Reading the two together shows how much of the company's worth is substance already in place and how much is expected added value.

**What the combination tells you.** A figure that is small next to a large invested capital base describes a capital-heavy business earning close to its cost of capital. A figure that dwarfs a tiny invested capital base describes a capital-light business whose worth is almost all expected advantage. A figure growing faster than invested capital is the healthy pattern, where added value outpaces the money tied up. Invested capital growing faster than the figure warns that capital is piling up ahead of the profit it earns.

### Enterprise Value

**Why read the two together.** Enterprise value is the value of the whole business to all funders, debt and equity together. Market Value Added measured from the market is exactly enterprise value minus invested capital. The model produces its own figure from a forecast, so the two versions can be compared directly. The comparison shows whether the market pays more premium or less premium than the model justifies.

**What the combination tells you.** When enterprise value minus invested capital sits above the model's figure, the price is paying for more excess return than the forecast supports. When it sits below, the market is more cautious than the model about the company's advantage. A large gap in either direction is the disagreement to investigate before trusting the price or the model. Reading the pair turns the figure into a check on whether the stock is cheap or expensive.

## Part 8: Limitations

The figure inherits every uncertainty of the forecast behind it. Market Value Added rests on projected profit, an estimated cost of capital, and a terminal assumption that can dominate the total. A small error in WACC is costly here, because WACC sets both the yearly charge and the discount rate. The measurement of invested capital adds a second source of error, since the base can be defined several ways and the choice moves the split between substance and premium.

The market version carries its own warning. Enterprise value minus invested capital reflects the mood of the market as much as the intrinsic worth of the business, so a high market premium is not proof of a real advantage. A negative figure does not always mean permanent value destruction, because a young company can show a negative figure while it builds toward returns above its cost of capital. The figure is a strong frame for the moat question and a poor substitute for judgment about whether the moat will last.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How does the company's value split between invested capital and Market Value Added? Is the price paying mostly for substance already in place or mostly for future added value?
2. Has the company actually earned a positive economic profit in past years, or does a large figure rest on excess returns it has never sustained?
3. What is the market's own premium, enterprise value minus invested capital, and how does it compare with the model's figure?
4. What terminal assumption sits behind the figure? What does the figure become if economic profit is assumed to fade to zero over time?
5. How wide is the current ROIC minus WACC spread, and how many years of that spread would justify today's premium?
6. Does the figure grow because the spread is widening, because the advantage is expected to last longer, or only because WACC was lowered?
7. How does the goodwill treatment move the base, and are past acquisitions earning the charge that goodwill adds?
8. Would a research-adjusted invested capital base change the split for this company's research intensity? Which version better describes the real economics?
9. Is Market Value Added rising or falling across the years the app shows, and does the direction match the trend in the realized economic profit?
10. At a cost of capital one point higher, with its double effect on the charge and the discount, does the premium survive?
