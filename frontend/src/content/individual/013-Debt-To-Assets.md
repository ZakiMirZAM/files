# Debt To Assets

## Part 1: Definition

The debt to assets ratio measures how much of everything a company owns was paid for with borrowed money. Debt is money the company has borrowed and must repay, such as bank loans and bonds. Assets are everything the company owns that has value, such as cash, inventory, and equipment. The ratio answers one question: what share of the company's assets is funded by lenders rather than by owners?

A ratio of 0.3 means thirty cents of every dollar of assets was funded by debt. A ratio of 0.6 means sixty cents of every dollar of assets was funded by debt, leaving only forty cents funded by owners. A higher number means lenders have a larger claim on what the company owns, which matters most if the company is ever forced to sell assets to repay debt.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.3 | Lenders fund a small share of the assets. The balance sheet is conservative. |
| 0.3 to 0.5 | A moderate level of debt funding. This app's quality score awards a pass at 0.50 or lower. |
| 0.5 to 0.7 | Lenders fund most of the assets. The level is normal in asset-heavy industries and risky elsewhere. |
| Above 0.7 | Heavy debt funding. A drop in asset values or earnings can leave little cushion for owners. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Debt To Assets = Total Debt ÷ Total Assets`

Total debt is the top number. Total debt is the sum of all interest-bearing borrowings, including short-term loans, long-term bonds, and the borrowing portion of leases. Some versions of the ratio use total liabilities instead, which adds non-borrowing items such as supplier bills and deferred taxes. This app uses interest-bearing debt so the ratio measures borrowing specifically rather than every obligation.

Total assets is the bottom number. Total assets is the recorded value of everything the company owns, taken straight from the balance sheet. Most assets are recorded at original cost minus depreciation, which is the gradual write-down of an asset's value as it ages. The recorded value can drift away from what the assets would actually sell for, especially for old property or for brands the company built rather than bought.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The debt to assets ratio shows how much of the company belongs to lenders before owners. If the company were wound down and its assets sold, lenders would be paid first. The ratio estimates how large that first claim is relative to the whole pile of assets, which is a direct measure of how much cushion protects the owners.

The ratio also frames the safety question from the asset side rather than the earnings side. Earnings can swing year to year, but the stock of assets changes slowly. A low ratio means the company could lose a meaningful share of its asset value and still cover its debt from what remains.

Lenders use the ratio to judge how much protection their loans have. A lower ratio means more asset value stands behind each dollar of debt, which lowers the risk that lenders are not fully repaid in a forced sale.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move the ratio. New borrowing raises the top number, and if the borrowed cash is held or spent on assets the bottom number rises too. Repaying debt lowers the top number and lowers the ratio. Buying assets with cash already on hand can lower the ratio because total assets rise while debt stays flat.

Accounting choices move the bottom number. Asset writedowns shrink total assets and push the ratio up overnight, even though no new debt was taken on. Depreciation gradually lowers the recorded value of equipment and buildings, which slowly raises the ratio over an asset's life. Assets the company built itself, such as brands and software, often never appear on the balance sheet. A company rich in such assets can therefore show a higher ratio than its true position warrants.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The acceptable level depends on how heavy and how stable the company's assets are. Industries with large, durable, pledgeable assets can carry a higher ratio safely.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and real estate | Often above 0.5 | Assets are tangible, long-lived, and easy to pledge, so lenders fund a large share. |
| Capital-intensive industrials | Moderate to high | Heavy plant and equipment support meaningful borrowing against the asset base. |
| Capital-light software and services | Often very low | Few recorded assets, so the ratio is low and tells little about the business. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Borrowing and lending are the core business, so the ordinary ratio does not apply. |
| Cyclical producers | Kept lower on purpose | Asset values and earnings fall together in a downturn, so a thinner debt layer is needed. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one company holds mostly recorded tangible assets and the other runs on brands and software that the balance sheet cannot see. Compare a company against direct peers with similar asset profiles.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A flat low line means the company funds growth mainly with owner money and retained profit. A flat high line means management funds the business with heavy debt on purpose, common in asset-heavy industries. The pattern that deserves the most attention is a steady climb. A rising ratio means debt is growing faster than the asset base. The cause is usually borrowing to fund losses, dividends, or buybacks.

A sudden jump in a single year often comes from an asset writedown that shrank the bottom number rather than from new borrowing. Check whether total assets fell or debt rose to find the cause. A ratio that falls steadily usually means the company is repaying debt or growing its asset base through retained profit, both of which strengthen the balance sheet.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** The debt to assets ratio measures debt against everything the company owns. The debt to equity ratio measures the same debt against the owners' stake alone. The two ratios describe the same borrowing from two angles, and reading both shows how the funding splits between lenders and owners.

**What the combination tells you.** The two ratios always move together, because more debt raises one and lowers the cushion behind the other. Debt to assets is steadier when equity has been distorted by buybacks or writedowns, because the asset base is harder to reshape than the equity base. When the two ratios disagree about how leveraged a company looks, the equity figure is usually the one being distorted.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** The debt to assets ratio measures how much of the company lenders have funded. Interest coverage measures whether earnings can pay the yearly interest on that debt. One looks at the asset cushion, the other at the ability to service the debt from profit, and a full view needs both.

**What the combination tells you.** A high debt to assets ratio paired with strong interest coverage means a large debt load that earnings comfortably service. A high ratio paired with weak coverage is the dangerous combination, because the company owes a lot and can barely afford the interest. Reading both separates the size of the claim on assets from the affordability of the debt.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** The debt to assets ratio compares debt with the recorded value of assets. Net Debt / EBITDA compares debt with yearly operating earnings and subtracts cash from the debt. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA is a rough measure of operating cash generation.

**What the combination tells you.** A company can show a low debt to assets ratio while Net Debt / EBITDA is high if its assets are large but earn little. Reading both shows whether the asset base that backs the debt actually generates the cash to repay it. Agreement between the two metrics gives a fuller picture of leverage than either alone.

### Total Assets

**Why read the two together.** Total Assets is the bottom number of this ratio, shown as its own line in the app's balance-sheet analysis. A change in the ratio can come from the debt rising or from the asset base shrinking, and only the Total Assets line shows which. Reading the line directly reveals what is driving the ratio.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising debt to assets ratio with a falling Total Assets line points to writedowns or shrinkage rather than fresh borrowing. A rising ratio with a flat asset line points to genuine new debt. Watching the two together tells you whether leverage is climbing because the company borrowed more or because its assets lost recorded value.

## Part 8: Limitations

The bottom number is recorded at accounting value rather than market value, so the ratio can mislead in both directions. Old property carried at original cost can be worth far more than the books show, which makes the company look more leveraged than it is. Assets the company built itself, such as brands and software, often never appear at all. The omission inflates the ratio for capital-light firms. Writedowns can spike the ratio overnight without any change in actual borrowing.

The top number carries its own gaps. Definitions of debt differ between companies, and leases and off-balance-sheet arrangements can hide real obligations. The ratio assumes assets could be sold near their recorded value in a crisis, which is rarely true, because a forced sale usually fetches far less. The ratio shows the size of the lenders' claim but says nothing about when the debt comes due or whether earnings can cover it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio driven by the amount of debt, or by an asset base that writedowns have shrunk? What does the ratio become if assets are adjusted back?
2. Are the company's recorded assets carried near their real value, or does old original cost understate the asset base?
3. How much of the company's value sits in brands or software that the balance sheet does not record? Does that make the ratio look worse than reality?
4. How does the ratio compare with direct peers that hold a similar mix of assets? Which peer relies most on debt funding?
5. In a forced sale, how much of the recorded asset value would the company actually recover? Would the proceeds cover the debt?
6. How much of the debt comes due within three years? Could the company repay or refinance it without selling assets?
7. Has the asset base grown through retained profit, or through borrowing that raised the ratio?
8. Does the broader total liabilities figure tell the same story as the debt to assets ratio? Or are non-borrowing obligations large?
9. Would the ratio stay safe if asset values fell in a downturn at the same time as earnings? How much cushion protects the owners?
10. Does management have a stated target for leverage on the asset base? Is the current ratio above or below it, and why?
