# Days Payables Outstanding

## Part 1: Definition

Days payables outstanding measures the average number of days a company takes to pay its suppliers after buying goods or services on credit. Buying on credit means receiving the goods now and agreeing to pay later. The unpaid bills owed to suppliers sit on the balance sheet as accounts payable. The metric answers one question: how long does the company hold on to a supplier's money before handing over the cash?

A reading of 45 means the company pays its suppliers about 45 days after the purchase. A reading of 90 means the company waits about three months. A longer wait keeps cash inside the business for longer, because the company is using the supplier as a short-term source of funding. The metric is also called the average payment period.

| Reading | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 20 days | The company pays suppliers fast. The company may be missing the free financing that slower payment would provide, or suppliers may demand quick payment. |
| 30 to 60 days | A common range. The company is using normal trade credit on terms most suppliers offer. |
| 60 to 90 days | The company stretches payment well past standard terms. Strong buyers can do this, but weak buyers risk angry suppliers. |
| Above 100 days | The company holds supplier cash for a long time. The long wait signals either heavy bargaining power or a cash shortage that delays payment. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Days Payables Outstanding = (Accounts Payable ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × 365`

Accounts payable is the top number. Accounts payable is the total unpaid bills the company owes its suppliers on the measurement date. The figure comes from the balance sheet. A larger payables balance relative to purchases means the company is sitting on more unpaid bills, which lengthens the payment period.

Cost of goods sold is the bottom number, scaled to a daily rate by the 365 in the formula. Cost of goods sold is the direct cost of the products the company sold during the year, mostly the cost of materials and goods bought from suppliers. Purchases are the truest input, but purchases are rarely reported, so cost of goods sold stands in for them. Dividing the year of cost by 365 gives the cost per day, and dividing payables by that daily cost converts the payables balance into a number of days.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Suppliers who let a company pay later are lending the company money at no interest. The longer the company waits to pay, the more of its operations the suppliers are quietly financing. Days payables outstanding states the length of that free loan in days, so an investor can see how much short-term funding the company pulls from its suppliers.

The metric also reveals bargaining power. A company that dominates its suppliers can dictate long payment terms and keep its own cash longer. A small or weak buyer must pay on the supplier's schedule. Reading the metric next to competitors shows who holds the upper hand in the supply chain.

The metric also flags cash strain. A sudden jump in the payment period can mean the company is short of cash and is delaying payment to conserve it. The investor then knows to check whether the slowdown reflects strength or distress.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move the number. Supplier terms set the baseline, because a company offered 60-day terms will naturally show a longer period than one offered 30-day terms. Bargaining power lets a large buyer push terms longer. A deliberate policy to delay payment and hold cash stretches the period further. A decision to pay early to win a discount shortens it.

Accounting timing can distort a single reading. The payables balance is measured on one date, so a large purchase made just before year end inflates the balance and the period. Cost of goods sold sits in the denominator. A company with low cost of goods sold relative to its purchases can show a period that looks longer than the true payment habit. A change in the supplier mix toward vendors with different terms also shifts the number without any change in financial health.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal payment period depends on the trade customs of the sector and on how much the company buys from suppliers.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Large retailers | Long, often 50 to 90 days | Powerful buyers force suppliers to wait, which funds a large part of store inventory. |
| Grocery and fast-moving goods | Long relative to inventory days | Goods sell fast while bills are paid slowly, which produces a strong cash position. |
| Manufacturers | Moderate, near standard terms | Steady purchasing on conventional supplier terms. |
| Services and software | Short or hard to read | Few physical goods are bought, so cost of goods sold is small and the metric carries little meaning. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | These firms do not buy inventory from trade suppliers, so the metric does not describe them. |

Compare the period only against companies in the same sector that buy similar inputs. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one company carries heavy physical purchasing and the other buys almost nothing from suppliers.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady, stable line means the company pays on consistent terms year after year. A steady rise means the company is taking longer to pay, which can reflect growing bargaining power that frees up cash, or a cash shortage that forces delay. The two causes look identical on the chart, so the rise must be checked against the company's cash position and profit. A rise paired with healthy profit usually signals strength, while a rise paired with weak cash usually signals strain.

A steady decline means the company is paying suppliers faster, which ties up more cash but may capture early-payment discounts or repair strained supplier relationships. A sharp one-year jump often reflects a timing quirk, such as a large purchase near the reporting date, rather than a real change in habit. Read this line next to the days inventory and days sales lines, because the three together form the cash conversion cycle that the next section explains.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** Days payables outstanding is one of the three pieces of the cash conversion cycle. The cash conversion cycle measures the days between paying suppliers for goods and collecting cash from customers. A longer payment period shrinks the cash conversion cycle, because the company holds supplier money for more of the gap. Reading the payment period inside the full cycle shows how much it eases the company's funding need.

**What the combination tells you.** A long payment period combined with a short cash conversion cycle means suppliers fund most of the working capital, which is a strong cash position. A short payment period combined with a long cash conversion cycle means the company funds its own operations and needs more outside cash. When the payment period stretches only because the cycle is already strained, the delay may be a sign of distress rather than strength. The pair separates healthy supplier financing from a forced delay in paying bills.

### Days Inventory Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days inventory outstanding measures how long goods sit in stock before they sell. Days payables outstanding measures how long the company waits to pay for those goods. Comparing the two shows whether the company pays for inventory before or after it sells. The gap between them drives how much cash the business must tie up.

**What the combination tells you.** When the payment period is longer than the inventory period, suppliers fund the stock and the company sells goods before the bill comes due. When the inventory period is longer, the company pays for goods that are still sitting unsold, which drains cash. A widening payment period against a stable inventory period shows the company improving its cash position through supplier terms. Reading both reveals whether the supply chain works for or against the company's cash.

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio compares short-term assets to short-term bills due within a year, and accounts payable is one of those bills. A longer payment period builds up the payables balance, which raises short-term bills and lowers the current ratio. The payment period therefore helps explain why the current ratio sits where it does.

**What the combination tells you.** A long payment period paired with a low current ratio can look risky, yet it may be safe if the company collects cash quickly and uses supplier credit on purpose. A short payment period paired with a high current ratio means the company funds itself and keeps a thick cushion. Reading both shows whether a low current ratio reflects real weakness or a deliberate use of supplier financing. The combination prevents a false alarm from the current ratio alone.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the cash left after the company pays for operations and equipment. Stretching the payment period releases cash and lifts free cash flow in that year. The boost is temporary, because the bills still come due later. Reading both shows whether strong cash flow rests on the real business or on delaying supplier payments.

**What the combination tells you.** Rising free cash flow paired with a sharply rising payment period means part of the cash gain comes from paying suppliers later, not from a better business. Stable free cash flow with a stable payment period means the cash is coming from operations. When the payment period cannot stretch any further, the cash flow lift from delaying payment disappears. The pair separates durable cash generation from a one-time release of supplier cash.

## Part 8: Limitations

The metric uses cost of goods sold in place of purchases, because purchases are rarely reported. A company that buys inventory but holds it instead of selling it will show a cost of goods sold that understates true purchases, which distorts the period. A company with very low cost of goods sold relative to what it buys can show a period that looks far longer than the real payment habit. The payables balance is also a single-date snapshot, so a purchase made just before year end can inflate the reading for reasons unrelated to normal practice.

The metric cannot tell strength from distress on its own. A long period may mean the company wields power over suppliers, or it may mean the company cannot pay on time. The two causes produce the same number and must be separated by checking cash and profit. The metric also ignores early-payment discounts, so a company that pays fast to capture discounts can look weak on this metric while making a smart financial choice. The metric is a useful clue about supplier financing, not a verdict on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What standard payment terms do the company's main suppliers offer, and is the current period close to those terms or far beyond them?
2. Has the period risen because the company gained bargaining power, or because the company is short of cash and delaying payment? What does the cash balance say?
3. How does the period compare with direct competitors that buy similar inputs? Does the company pay slower or faster than its peers?
4. Is the latest reading distorted by a large purchase made near the reporting date? What does the period look like averaged over the full year?
5. How does the payment period compare with the days inventory period? Does the company pay for goods before or after selling them?
6. Has any recent rise in free cash flow come partly from stretching the payment period rather than from the operating business?
7. Is the company passing up early-payment discounts by paying slowly, and does that cost more than the value of holding the cash?
8. Have suppliers reacted to slow payment by tightening terms, demanding deposits, or cutting the company off?
9. Does the company depend on a few large suppliers who could force faster payment, or is the supplier base spread widely enough to protect the long period?
10. If the period stopped stretching and returned to standard terms, how much cash would the company need to find, and could it raise that cash easily?
