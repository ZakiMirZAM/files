# 10-K & 10-Q

## What they are

A public company is a business whose shares trade on a stock exchange and can be bought by anyone. Every public company in the United States must file regular reports with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The Securities and Exchange Commission is the United States market regulator, the government body that oversees stock markets and enforces disclosure rules. Two of these reports matter most to an investor.

The 10-K is the audited annual report. Audited means an independent accounting firm has examined the numbers and confirmed they follow standard accounting rules. The 10-Q is the shorter unaudited report filed after each of the first three quarters of the company's year. Unaudited means no outside firm has examined the numbers before the company files them. Both reports state the company's financial results for the period and its financial condition at the end of the period.

## What each report contains

A fiscal year is the twelve-month period a company uses for its accounts, and it may not line up with the calendar year. A fiscal quarter is one three-month part of that year.

| Report | Period covered | Audited | What to read first |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10-K | The full fiscal year | Yes | The financial statements, then the notes |
| 10-Q | One fiscal quarter, for the first three quarters | No | The financial statements and the discussion |

An investor reads four parts of these reports most closely. The financial statements are the core of each report. The three main statements are the income statement, the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement. The income statement shows revenue and profit over the period. The balance sheet lists what the company owns and what it owes at the period end. The cash flow statement shows the cash that came in and went out.

The management discussion and analysis is the section where managers explain the results in their own words. Managers describe what drove the results and what they expect to happen next. The risk factors section lists the events that could harm the business. Examples include new competition, a major lawsuit, or the loss of a large customer. The notes are the detailed explanations printed after the statements. The notes explain the accounting choices behind the headline numbers. The notes often reveal debts, leases, and legal disputes that the main statements only summarize.

## Why they matter for valuation

Valuation means estimating what a business is truly worth, apart from its share price. Every serious valuation starts from real figures, and the 10-K and 10-Q are where those figures come from. The audited 10-K gives the most reliable yearly record, since an outside firm has checked it. The 10-Q shows whether the latest quarter is improving or weakening, months before the next annual report arrives. Reading both together lets an investor track the trend and confirm that the numbers behind a valuation are real. The written sections add what the numbers alone cannot show. Risk factors and the management discussion reveal the threats and plans that will shape future profit, and future profit is what a valuation tries to estimate.

## Common traps

1. **Reading only the headline numbers.** The revenue and profit figures sit at the top and draw the eye first. The notes and the risk factors sit further down, and they often hold the warnings that the headline numbers hide. Skipping them means missing debts, disputes, and accounting choices that can change the whole picture.
2. **Trusting the 10-Q like the 10-K.** The 10-Q is unaudited, so no outside firm has checked its numbers before the company files it. Treat a single quarter as an early signal rather than a settled fact, and wait for the audited 10-K to confirm a surprising result.
