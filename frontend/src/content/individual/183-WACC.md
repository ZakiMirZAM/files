# WACC

## Part 1: Definition

WACC is short for weighted average cost of capital. It measures the blended return that the providers of a company's money require each year. A company is funded by two groups: lenders, who supply debt and want interest, and shareholders, who supply equity and want a return on their investment. Each group demands a different rate, and each supplies a different share of the total funding. WACC blends the two rates together, weighting each by how much of the financing it provides.

The result is a single percentage that stands for the cost of the whole pool of capital. It is the rate the business must earn on the money invested in it just to keep both groups satisfied. In plain terms, WACC is the hurdle. If the company earns more than its WACC on the capital it uses, it creates value. If it earns less, it destroys value, even when it still reports an accounting profit.

| WACC level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Low (mid single digits) | A safe, stable business with cheap debt and patient shareholders, or a low interest-rate environment. |
| Moderate (high single digits) | A typical company with a normal mix of debt and equity and average risk. |
| High (low double digits or more) | A risky or volatile business, heavy reliance on costly equity, or a high interest-rate environment. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`WACC = Ke × (E/V) + Kd × (1 − tax) × (D/V)`

Ke is the cost of equity, the return shareholders require. Kd is the cost of debt, the interest rate the company pays on its borrowing. E is the market value of equity, D is the value of debt, and V is the total of the two, so E/V and D/V are the shares of financing that come from equity and debt. Each cost is multiplied by its weight, and the two are added together.

The term (1 − tax) applies only to the debt side. Interest is tax-deductible, so borrowing costs the company less than the headline rate, because the tax bill falls when interest is paid. Multiplying Kd by one minus the tax rate captures that saving. Equity has no such shield, so the cost of equity enters at full weight. This app builds the cost of equity from CAPM, which sets Ke as the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium, so the cost of equity rises with the stock's sensitivity to the market.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

WACC is the benchmark that gives meaning to a company's returns. A return on invested capital of 10 percent sounds fine until you learn the WACC is 12 percent, at which point the company is losing ground. The same 10 percent return against a WACC of 6 percent is excellent. Returns cannot be judged in isolation. WACC is the line they must beat, and the gap between the return and the WACC is the true measure of value creation.

WACC is also the discount rate in valuation. Future cash flows are worth less the longer you wait and the riskier they are, and WACC sets how much they are marked down. A higher WACC lowers the present value of every future dollar, so a riskier company is worth less for the same expected cash flows. This is why WACC sits at the center of discounted cash flow models and of economic value added, where it sets the capital charge subtracted from operating profit.

Because WACC reflects both the cost of borrowing and the risk shareholders perceive, it is a compact summary of how the market prices the company's risk. A rising WACC signals that capital is becoming more expensive for this business, whether from higher rates or higher risk.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The cost of equity is usually the largest piece, because shareholders demand more than lenders for taking more risk. It rises with the stock's beta, with the general level of interest rates through the risk-free rate, and with the equity risk premium investors require to hold stocks at all. A volatile, high-beta company carries a high cost of equity and therefore a high WACC.

The cost of debt depends on interest rates and on the company's creditworthiness. A heavily indebted or shaky borrower pays more, lifting Kd. The tax rate matters because it sets the size of the interest shield, so a higher tax rate makes debt cheaper after tax. The mix of debt and equity moves the blend: debt is cheaper than equity, so adding debt can lower WACC at first, but too much debt raises the risk of both lenders and shareholders and eventually pushes WACC back up. Market movements also feed in, because the equity weight uses the market value of the shares, which changes with the stock price.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

WACC varies by industry because risk and the natural mix of financing vary. Stable, asset-backed businesses can borrow cheaply and carry low betas, while volatile or early-stage businesses face a high cost of equity.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | Low WACC | Steady regulated cash flows support heavy, cheap debt and a low beta. |
| Consumer staples | Low to moderate | Stable demand keeps both the cost of equity and the cost of debt down. |
| Industrial and manufacturing | Moderate | Cyclical profits raise beta and the cost of equity somewhat. |
| Technology and growth | High | High betas and reliance on equity rather than debt lift the cost of capital. |
| Banks and insurers | Not standard | Capital and financing are the raw material of the business, so WACC is not well defined and the cost of equity is used instead. |

Compare WACC within an industry. For banks and insurers, WACC does not apply cleanly, and the cost of equity is the right hurdle to use in its place.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

WACC tends to move slowly because it reflects long-run risk and financing choices, so a sharp change deserves a look. A rising WACC usually means rates have climbed, the company has taken on more risk, or its beta has increased. A falling WACC can come from cheaper debt, a lower beta, or a calmer market. Because the equity weight uses the market value of the shares, a large move in the stock price will shift WACC on its own, which is one reason the line is not perfectly stable.

What matters most is WACC read against the company's return on invested capital over the same years. A WACC that is flat while returns climb means a widening value-creation gap. A WACC that rises past the return is a warning that the company has stopped clearing its hurdle. Watch for years when WACC moved mostly because of the assumed inputs, such as a change in the risk-free rate or the equity risk premium, rather than anything inside the business itself.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Cost of Equity

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity is the equity half of WACC, and it is usually the larger half. Reading it on its own shows what shareholders alone require, before the cheaper debt is blended in. WACC then combines that with the cost of debt.

**What the combination tells you.** A large gap between the cost of equity and WACC means debt is pulling the blended cost down, which can flatter WACC for a heavily indebted company. If WACC looks low only because of heavy borrowing, the cost of equity reveals the real risk shareholders are carrying. For banks and insurers, where WACC is not well defined, the cost of equity is the hurdle to use directly.

### Return on Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** WACC is the hurdle, and return on invested capital is the actual return. Neither means much alone. The whole point of WACC is to give the return something to beat.

**What the combination tells you.** When the return on invested capital sits above WACC, the company creates value, and the wider the gap the more it creates. When the return falls below WACC, the company destroys value even if it still posts a profit. Reading the two side by side turns a raw return into a verdict on whether the business is worth more than the capital it consumes.

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** EVA uses WACC to build its capital charge. It multiplies WACC by the invested capital and subtracts the result from operating profit. WACC is the rate, EVA is the dollar surplus left after applying it.

**What the combination tells you.** A small change in WACC can flip EVA from positive to negative, so reading both shows how sensitive the company's value creation is to its cost of capital. A positive EVA earned against a high WACC is more convincing than the same EVA against a low one. If EVA turned negative in a year WACC rose, the cause may be the rate rather than the operations.

## Part 8: Limitations

WACC is an estimate, not an observed number. The cost of equity comes from a model that requires a beta, a risk-free rate, and an equity risk premium, each of which is itself estimated and debated. A small change in any of these inputs moves WACC, and with it every valuation and capital charge that depends on it. Two analysts can produce two different WACCs for the same company simply by choosing different inputs.

The market-value weights also shift with the stock price, so WACC can move from quarter to quarter without anything changing inside the business. Beta is usually measured from past price movements, which may not describe future risk. The cost of debt assumes the company can keep borrowing at current rates, which may not hold if conditions tighten. And WACC does not apply cleanly to banks and insurers, where debt is part of the product rather than a financing choice. Because so much rests on assumptions, WACC is best treated as a reasonable range rather than a precise figure.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current WACC driven mainly by the cost of equity or by the cost of debt, and which input would change it most?
2. How does WACC compare with the company's return on invested capital, and is the gap positive or negative?
3. Has a recent move in WACC come from the business itself, or from a change in the assumed risk-free rate or equity risk premium?
4. How much of the low WACC, if it is low, is the result of heavy borrowing rather than genuinely low risk?
5. What beta is feeding the cost of equity, and does it reflect the company's true risk or just past price swings?
6. Would a one or two point rise in WACC push the company below its hurdle, and how close is it now?
7. Has the stock price moved enough to shift the equity weight and change WACC on its own?
8. For a bank or insurer, is the cost of equity being used as the hurdle instead of WACC?
9. Does the cost of debt assume the company can keep refinancing at today's rates, and is that realistic?
10. Is the WACC used here consistent across the years shown, or do changing assumptions make the trend hard to read?
