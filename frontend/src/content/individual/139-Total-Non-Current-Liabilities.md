# Total Non Current Liabilities

## Part 1: Definition

Total non current liabilities is the sum of everything a company owes that comes due more than one year from now. A liability is something the company owes. The word non current means the obligation is settled beyond the next twelve months. The line gathers all the long-term claims on the company in one figure. The line answers one question: how heavy is the company's long-term load of obligations?

The line adds together the named long-term obligations and the catchall. The main pieces are long-term debt, deferred revenue non current, deferred tax liabilities non current, and other non current liabilities. Long-term debt is loans and bonds repaid beyond a year. Deferred revenue non current is cash collected for work to be delivered beyond a year. Deferred tax liabilities non current is tax postponed to future years. Other non current liabilities is the catchall for leftover long-term items such as pensions and leases.

The table below reads common situations for this line.

| What you see | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| A large balance | The company funds itself heavily with long-term obligations. |
| A small balance | The company carries little long-term debt or few long-term claims. |
| A balance growing faster than assets | Long-term obligations are rising as a share of the business. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Total non current liabilities is not a calculated ratio. The line is a subtotal reported on the balance sheet. The balance sheet is the statement that lists what a company owns and owes on a single date. The line adds up the long-term obligation lines above it.

`Total Non Current Liabilities = Long-Term Debt + Deferred Revenue Non Current + Deferred Tax Liabilities Non Current + Other Non Current Liabilities`

Each input is a long-term obligation in its own right. Long-term debt is usually the largest piece for most companies. Deferred revenue non current and deferred tax liabilities non current can be large for subscription businesses and capital-heavy businesses. Other non current liabilities holds pensions, leases, and smaller items. The exact lines a company sums can vary, but every input shares one trait. Each input is owed beyond twelve months.

The balance rises when the company borrows long-term, collects more advance payments, defers more tax, or books new long-term reserves. The balance falls when long-term debt is repaid, deferrals reverse, or reserves are settled. Because the line is a sum, an investor must check which piece drove a change.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Total non current liabilities shows the long-term obligations the company must service over many years. The figure separates patient obligations from the bills due within twelve months. A company can carry a large long-term load and still be safe, because the obligations are spread out. The figure frames how much the business owes over the long horizon.

The line also feeds the leverage ratios investors rely on. Long-term obligations are the part of the balance sheet that determines financial risk over time. The size of this line, set against assets and equity, tells the investor how aggressively the company is financed.

The mix inside the line matters as much as the size. Long-term debt carries interest and fixed repayment dates. Deferred revenue is settled by doing work. Deferred tax can roll forward for years. The same total can mean very different risk depending on the mix.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Borrowing decisions are the first driver. New long-term loans or bonds raise the line. Repaying old debt lowers it. A company funding an acquisition with long-term debt will see this line jump.

The deferred items are the second driver. A subscription business with longer prepaid contracts builds deferred revenue non current. A capital-heavy business investing in equipment builds deferred tax liabilities non current. Both lift the total without adding any interest-bearing debt.

Long-term operating obligations are the third driver. Pension obligations rise when the discount rate used to value them falls. New long-term leases add to other non current liabilities. These items move with assumptions and commitments rather than with fresh borrowing, so the total can grow even when debt is flat.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The size of this line depends on how the business funds itself and what long-term obligations it carries. The table shows the pattern by sector.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and telecom | Large | Heavy long-term debt funds massive long-lived assets. |
| Capital-intensive industry | Large | Long-term debt and deferred tax both run high. |
| Software and subscription | Moderate | Deferred revenue can be sizable while debt stays low. |
| Asset-light services | Small | Few long-term obligations of any kind. |
| Banks and insurers | Distorted | The balance sheet is structured differently, so the line is not comparable. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is meaningless. A utility funded with long-term debt is not riskier than a software firm simply because the line is larger. The line only carries meaning against companies in the same sector and against the company's own assets and equity.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the total non current liabilities line as a multi-year series and as a percent of total assets. Total assets is everything the company owns. The vertical analysis on this page shows each balance-sheet line as a share of total assets, so a rising raw number can still be a flat share if the company is growing.

A steady rise in the balance that outpaces total assets means long-term obligations are growing faster than the business. A rise of that kind raises financial risk. A steady decline means the company is paying down long-term obligations and deleveraging. A sudden step up usually follows a large debt-funded acquisition or a new long-term financing. High volatility is unusual for this line and points to large one-off financings. One base effect can fake a trend. A rise driven by deferred tax from a pension assumption change is gentler than the same rise driven by new interest-bearing debt, so always check which piece moved. Read the trend alongside the four sibling lines to see what is actually driving it.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Total Liabilities

**Why read the two together.** Total liabilities is everything the company owes, both short-term and long-term. Total non current liabilities is the long-term portion of that figure. Reading both shows how the company's obligations split between near-term bills and patient long-term claims.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose obligations are mostly long-term has more breathing room than one whose obligations are mostly due within a year. A large total non current liabilities share of total liabilities points to patient financing. A small share means the company leans on short-term obligations that must be settled soon. The pair shows how much time pressure sits behind the company's total load.

### Debt To Assets

**Why read the two together.** Debt to assets measures how much of the asset base is funded by debt. Total non current liabilities contains the long-term debt that drives most of that ratio. Reading both connects the raw long-term obligation figure to a standardized measure of leverage.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising total non current liabilities balance usually pushes debt to assets higher, which signals growing financial risk. A large long-term obligation funded by deferred tax rather than debt may lift the total without lifting debt to assets much. Reading both separates obligations that carry interest from those that do not. The pair shows whether the long-term load is the costly borrowed kind or the gentler deferred kind.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest coverage tests whether operating income can pay the yearly interest on the company's debt. Total non current liabilities holds the long-term debt that generates that interest. Reading both connects the size of the long-term load to the company's ability to carry its cost.

**What the combination tells you.** A large total non current liabilities balance is safe only if interest coverage stays comfortable. A rising long-term load paired with falling interest coverage warns that the obligations are outrunning earnings. A high coverage ratio means the company can carry even a large long-term load. The pair separates the size of the long-term obligations from the company's ability to service them.

## Part 8: Limitations

Total non current liabilities is a sum, so the figure hides its own mix. The same total can be mostly interest-bearing debt at one company and mostly deferred revenue or deferred tax at another. Those mixes carry very different risk, yet the subtotal treats them as one. An investor must open the four sibling lines to know what the figure really contains.

The line also depends on where the twelve-month boundary falls. A long-term debt that matures in thirteen months sits here, but the same debt sits in current liabilities a month later, with no change in the business. Reporting choices shift items between the named lines and the catchall, which blurs comparisons across companies. The line says nothing about when the obligations come due within the long-term window or what interest they carry. The line is a useful summary of the long-term load and a weak guide to its risk without the detail beneath it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which of the four sibling lines makes up most of total non current liabilities: long-term debt, deferred revenue non current, deferred tax liabilities non current, or other non current liabilities?
2. Is the current balance produced by interest-bearing debt, by deferred items that carry no interest, or by a mix? How does that change the risk?
3. Did the balance jump in any recent year because of a debt-funded acquisition or a major new financing?
4. How fast is total non current liabilities growing compared with total assets and with equity?
5. How much of the long-term debt inside this line matures soon, and would refinancing be costly at current rates?
6. Does a large pension or lease obligation sit in other non current liabilities and add to this total beyond formal debt?
7. How does total non current liabilities as a percent of total assets compare with direct competitors in the same sector?
8. Is the company deleveraging by paying down long-term obligations, or adding to them year after year?
9. Does interest coverage remain comfortable given the size of the long-term debt inside this line?
10. Did any reclassification between current and non current obligations move this line without a real change in what the company owes?
