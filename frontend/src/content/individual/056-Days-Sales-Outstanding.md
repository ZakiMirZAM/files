# Days Sales Outstanding

## Part 1: Definition

Days sales outstanding measures the average number of days a company waits to collect cash after making a sale on credit. A sale on credit means the customer takes the goods now and pays later. The figure is stated in days. A lower number means customers pay quickly and cash returns fast. A higher number means cash stays parked in unpaid customer bills for longer. The metric answers one question: how long does the company's cash sit in customers' hands before it comes back?

This metric is also called the average collection period. The unpaid customer bills are called accounts receivable, the money owed to the company by customers who have bought but not yet paid. The vault describes the average collection period as the length of time from a sale on credit until the payment becomes usable funds. A company that collects in 30 days recovers its cash twice as fast as one that takes 60 days.

Common readings of the metric:

| Days level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Under 15 days | Very fast collection. Common where most sales settle in cash or by card. |
| 15 to 45 days | Typical for companies offering standard 30-day payment terms. |
| 45 to 90 days | Slower collection. Normal for business-to-business sales with longer terms. |
| Above 90 days | Cash is tied up for months. May signal loose credit terms or customers in trouble. |
| Rising over time | Often an early warning that customers are paying later or struggling to pay. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Days Sales Outstanding = (Accounts Receivable ÷ Revenue) × 365`

The first input is accounts receivable, the total amount customers owe the company at the measurement date. Many analysts use average receivables, the start-of-year balance plus the end-of-year balance divided by two, to smooth a figure that moves during the year. A larger receivables balance relative to sales means more cash is waiting to be collected.

The second input is revenue, the company's sales for the year. Dividing receivables by revenue shows what share of a year's sales is still uncollected. Multiplying by 365 converts that share into a number of days. Some analysts use only credit sales rather than total sales in the bottom, because cash sales are collected immediately and never become receivables. The app uses total revenue where credit sales are not separately reported.

| Variant | What the variant uses | Effect on the reading |
|---|---|---|
| Total revenue | All sales in the denominator | Simple, but understates collection days when many sales are cash. |
| Credit sales only | Only sales made on credit | More precise, but the figure is often not disclosed separately. |
| Average receivables | Start and end balances averaged | Smooths seasonal swings in the receivables balance. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A sale is not finished until the cash arrives. Days sales outstanding shows how long that final step takes. The longer customers take to pay, the more cash the company must tie up to keep operating, and that cash often has to be financed. The metric turns the speed of collection into a single, comparable figure.

The metric answers a question that the income statement hides. A company records a sale as revenue the moment the goods ship, before any cash changes hands. Days sales outstanding reveals whether that recorded revenue is actually being collected. A company can report rising sales while its collection period quietly stretches, which means the growth is going onto customers' tabs rather than into the bank.

The metric is also a check on earnings quality. A company can inflate reported sales by offering very generous payment terms or by selling to weak customers. Both tactics push days sales outstanding up. A rising collection period alongside rising sales is a sign that the sales may be lower quality than they look.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Credit policy is the main driver. A company that offers longer payment terms to win business will show a higher figure. A company that tightens terms or enforces collection harder will show a lower one. The mix of customers matters too. Selling more to large, slow-paying corporate buyers raises the figure, while selling more to cash or card customers lowers it.

Customer health and timing distort single readings. When customers fall into financial trouble, they pay later, and the figure climbs even though the company's own policy has not changed. A surge of sales late in the year inflates receivables at the measurement date and overstates the collection period for that single reading. A company can also flatter the figure by selling its receivables to a third party for immediate cash, which removes the balance from the books without the customers actually paying faster.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as fast or slow depends on how the industry sells.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Grocery and consumer retail | Very low, often under 5 days | Customers pay at the register, so almost no receivables build up. |
| Restaurants and fast food | Very low | Sales settle immediately in cash or card. |
| Business-to-business manufacturing | Moderate, often 30 to 60 days | Corporate customers buy on standard credit terms. |
| Construction and heavy projects | High | Large projects are billed in stages and paid slowly. |
| Software and subscriptions | Low when prepaid, higher for enterprise deals | Consumer plans prepay, while large enterprise contracts pay on terms. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | These firms have no normal sales-and-collection cycle. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one business sells for cash and another sells on long credit terms. Compare a company only against direct peers that sell on similar terms to similar customers.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady figure means collection practice and customer quality are stable. A steady decline is usually a good sign, showing faster collection or a shift toward customers who pay sooner. A decline can also follow a one-time sale of receivables, so confirm that the improvement reflects real collection rather than an accounting move.

A steady rise is the pattern that deserves the most attention. A lengthening collection period means cash is taking longer to come back, whether because the company loosened terms or because customers are paying late. Read the trend against sales growth. A collection period that rises faster than sales suggests the company is buying growth by extending credit. A sudden spike in one year can be a base effect from a late-year sales surge rather than a real change, so compare the same quarter across years.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** Days sales outstanding is the collection component of the cash conversion cycle. The cycle adds inventory days and collection days, then subtracts supplier-payment days. Reading collection days inside the full cycle shows how much of the company's total cash delay comes from customers rather than from stock or suppliers. This pinpoints where cash is getting stuck.

**What the combination tells you.** A lengthening cycle driven mainly by rising collection days points to a customer or credit-policy problem rather than an inventory one. A steady collection figure inside a moving cycle clears receivables of blame and sends attention elsewhere. Reading the component against the whole tells a beginner exactly which lever moved the cycle.

### Days Inventory Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days Inventory Outstanding measures how long goods sit before they sell, while days sales outstanding measures how long customers take to pay after the sale. The two together form the operating cycle, the full span from buying goods to collecting the cash. Reading both shows whether the company's cash is held up more in stock or in unpaid bills.

**What the combination tells you.** Long inventory days with short collection days means cash is trapped in the warehouse, not at the customer. Short inventory days with long collection days means the opposite. Reading the pair separates a selling-speed problem from a collection problem, which call for very different fixes.

### Income Quality (CFO/NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income Quality compares cash from operations against reported net income to test whether profit is turning into cash. A rising days sales outstanding figure is one reason cash can lag profit, because sales recorded as income are still sitting in receivables. Reading the two together shows whether stretched collection is the cause of weak cash conversion.

**What the combination tells you.** Low income quality alongside a rising collection period points to revenue that is being booked faster than it is collected. Stable income quality with a steady collection period suggests profits are converting to cash normally. Reading the pair helps a beginner judge whether reported earnings are backed by real cash.

### Revenue Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Revenue Per Share tracks the top-line sales attributed to each share. Days sales outstanding tests how much of that revenue has actually been collected. Strong revenue growth means little if the new sales are piling up unpaid in receivables. Reading the two together checks whether headline growth is turning into cash.

**What the combination tells you.** Rising revenue per share with a stable collection period signals healthy, cash-backed growth. Rising revenue per share with a climbing collection period warns that the growth is being financed by extending credit to customers. Reading the pair separates genuine demand from sales bought with loose terms.

## Part 8: Limitations

The figure depends heavily on the single balance-sheet date and on whether total or credit sales sit in the denominator. A late-year sales surge inflates receivables and overstates the collection period for that reading. Using total revenue when many sales are cash understates the true credit-collection speed, which makes raw comparison between companies unreliable.

The metric can be flattered without any real improvement in collection. Selling receivables to a third party for immediate cash removes the balance from the books while customers still pay slowly. The average also hides the spread of the receivables. A company can show a reasonable figure while a few large customers are dangerously overdue, because their lateness is buried inside the average. The metric says nothing about how likely each unpaid bill is to be collected at all.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Why is the current figure at its level: standard credit terms, a shift in customer mix, or customers paying later than before?
2. Is the figure based on total revenue or credit sales only? How much of the company's sales are actually made on credit?
3. Has the collection period risen faster than sales over the last several years? Is the company buying growth by extending credit?
4. Is the figure measured after a late-year sales surge that inflated the receivables balance? What does the same quarter show across years?
5. Has the company sold or factored its receivables, which would lower the figure without customers paying any faster?
6. Is the receivables balance concentrated in a few large customers? How overdue are the biggest accounts?
7. Does a rising collection period line up with weakening income quality, suggesting sales are outrunning cash collection?
8. Are any of the company's major customers in financial trouble, which would push collection out regardless of policy?
9. How does the figure compare with direct competitors selling on similar terms to similar customers? Which peer collects fastest?
10. Has management changed its credit terms or collection effort recently? What effect has that change had on the trend?
