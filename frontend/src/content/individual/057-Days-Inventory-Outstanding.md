# Days Inventory Outstanding

## Part 1: Definition

Days inventory outstanding measures the average number of days a company holds its goods before selling them. The figure is stated in days. A lower number means goods move off the shelf quickly. A higher number means goods sit in storage for longer before they sell. The metric answers one question: how long does the company's cash stay locked inside unsold goods before a sale frees it?

Inventory is the stock of unsold goods a company holds, including raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods waiting to be sold. This metric is also called the average age of inventory. The vault names this figure the first component of the cash conversion cycle and describes it as the average age of inventory. A company holding 30 days of inventory turns its stock far faster than one holding 120 days.

Common readings of the metric:

| Days level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Under 20 days | Very fast. Common for perishable goods that must sell quickly. |
| 20 to 60 days | Brisk turnover typical of general retail and consumer goods. |
| 60 to 120 days | A moderate pace common in manufacturing and distribution. |
| Above 120 days | Goods sit for months. Normal for big-ticket or slow-selling products. |
| Very high | Common for aged goods such as spirits, where long holding is intended. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Days Inventory Outstanding = (Inventory ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × 365`

The first input is inventory, the value of unsold goods the company holds at the measurement date. Many analysts use average inventory, the start-of-year balance plus the end-of-year balance divided by two, to smooth a figure that swings during the year. A larger inventory balance relative to the cost of sales means more cash is tied up in goods waiting to sell.

The second input is cost of goods sold, the direct cost of the products the company sold during the year. Cost rather than sales price is used because inventory on the balance sheet is recorded at cost, so the top and bottom both reflect cost. Dividing inventory by cost of goods sold shows what share of a year's cost is sitting unsold. Multiplying by 365 converts that share into a number of days. This metric is the inverse of the inventory turnover ratio expressed in days.

| Variant | What the variant uses | Effect on the reading |
|---|---|---|
| Ending inventory | The balance at year end | Simple, but a seasonal high or low distorts the figure. |
| Average inventory | Start and end balances averaged | Smooths seasonal swings and is the form most textbooks prefer. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Inventory is cash that has not yet returned. Money spent on goods stays idle until those goods sell. Days inventory outstanding shows how long that cash stays frozen, which is a direct read on how well the company manages its stock. Holding fewer days of inventory frees cash that would otherwise need outside financing.

The metric answers a question that the income statement does not. Profit margins show how much a company earns per sale, but they say nothing about how long goods linger first. Days inventory outstanding measures that holding time. A company can earn a healthy margin while its cash sits trapped in slow-moving stock.

The metric is also an early warning. A rising figure means goods are piling up faster than they sell, which often signals weakening demand or overbuying. The signal frequently appears before the trouble reaches the sales line or forces a writedown, which is a reduction in the recorded value of goods the company can no longer sell at full price.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Demand is the largest driver. Strong sales clear goods quickly and lower the figure, while a slowdown leaves stock on the shelf and raises it. Purchasing decisions matter too. A company that overbuys ahead of expected demand lifts inventory and raises the figure until the goods sell. A move to lean, just-in-time stock lowers the figure because less inventory sits on hand at any moment.

Accounting and timing choices distort comparisons. The inventory-valuation method, such as first-in-first-out or last-in-first-out, changes both the inventory balance and the cost figure, so two firms with identical operations can report different days. Seasonality moves the number. A retailer measured right after a holiday buildup shows a high figure that does not reflect the full-year pace. A writedown cuts the inventory balance suddenly and lowers the figure for a reason unrelated to faster selling.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as fast or slow depends entirely on what the company sells.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Grocery and fresh food | Very low | Perishable goods must sell within days before spoiling. |
| General and discount retail | Low to moderate | Shelves restock often, but seasonal lines lift the average. |
| Apparel and fashion | Moderate | Stock must clear before the season ends, yet unsold lines linger. |
| Manufacturing and industrial | Moderate to high | Goods move through production and sit before shipping. |
| Heavy machinery and autos | High | Large, costly units sell slowly and sit in stock for months. |
| Aged goods such as spirits and lumber | Very high | Goods gain value or readiness only after long storage. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two businesses hold fundamentally different kinds of goods. Compare a company only against direct peers that sell similar products, and judge the trend in its own figure over time.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady figure means inventory management is stable and supply is matching demand. A steady decline is usually a good sign, showing faster selling or leaner stock. A decline driven by a shrinking inventory balance can also warn of thin stock that risks stockouts and lost sales, so read a falling figure together with revenue to tell efficiency from scarcity.

A steady rise is the pattern that deserves the most attention. A lengthening holding period means goods are accumulating faster than they sell, which often precedes a writedown or a demand slowdown. A sudden jump in one year can be a base effect rather than a real change. A writedown, a sell-off of slow stock, or a year-end measured at a seasonal low can all move the figure without any real change in selling speed, so compare the same quarter across years.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### COGS / Inventory

**Why read the two together.** Days inventory outstanding and COGS / Inventory measure the same thing from opposite directions. COGS / Inventory is the inventory turnover ratio, counting how many times stock cycles in a year. Days inventory outstanding restates that count as the average number of days a good sits before selling. The two always move in opposite directions, so reading them together confirms the same story in two units.

**What the combination tells you.** A turnover ratio of 6 corresponds to roughly 60 days of inventory, so the two should always agree. The days figure is often easier to compare against a company's selling season and supplier terms because the figure is stated in days. Reading both lets a beginner check that a change is real and not a measurement quirk, since both must move together if the true selling pace changed.

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** Days inventory outstanding is the first of the three building blocks of the cash conversion cycle. The cycle adds inventory days and collection days, then subtracts supplier-payment days. Reading the inventory component inside the full cycle shows how much of the company's total cash delay comes from goods sitting in stock rather than from customers or suppliers.

**What the combination tells you.** A lengthening cycle driven mainly by rising inventory days points to slowing sales or overbuying rather than a collection problem. A steady inventory figure inside a moving cycle clears stock of blame and sends attention to receivables or payables. Reading the component against the whole cycle tells a beginner exactly which lever moved.

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross Margin shows how much profit remains from each sale after the cost of the goods sold. Days inventory outstanding shows how long goods wait before that sale happens. The two combine into the real return on stock, because a company earns its margin only when the goods finally sell, and a long holding period delays every one of those earnings.

**What the combination tells you.** A short holding period with a healthy margin means stock turns into profitable sales quickly. A long holding period can erode returns even at a high margin, because the cash is tied up for longer. A days figure that falls only because the company is discounting heavily will show up as a falling gross margin, so reading both catches a hollow improvement.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free Cash Flow is the cash left after the company funds its operations and its equipment. A rising days inventory outstanding figure traps cash in unsold goods and pulls free cash flow down. A falling figure releases cash and lifts it. The holding period explains part of why free cash flow swings from year to year.

**What the combination tells you.** Weak free cash flow alongside a rising inventory holding period shows that cash is getting stuck in the warehouse rather than lost to poor profits. Strong free cash flow with a falling holding period confirms that leaner stock is feeding the cash pile. Reading the pair links the timing measure to the actual cash the company keeps.

## Part 8: Limitations

The figure is highly sensitive to the inventory-valuation method and to the single balance-sheet date used. A company measured at a seasonal high shows a misleading figure that does not reflect the full year. Two companies that operate identically can report different days simply because they value inventory differently, which makes cross-company comparison harder than the single number suggests.

The metric says nothing about the quality or freshness of the stock. A low figure can hide thin inventory that is causing lost sales through frequent stockouts. A high figure can reflect a deliberate strategy, such as aging a product, rather than poor management. The average also hides the mix, so a fast-selling line can mask a pile of dead stock sitting underneath it. The metric measures speed, not the value of what is actually being held.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Why is the current figure at its level: strong demand, lean purchasing, a writedown, or a seasonal measurement date?
2. Is the figure based on year-end inventory or average inventory? Does using the average change the reading much?
3. Has the figure been falling because the company sells faster, or because the inventory balance is shrinking toward stockout territory? What does revenue say?
4. Which inventory-valuation method does the company use? How does that choice compare with the method its closest peers use?
5. What was the figure across the last several years, and does the trend point toward goods piling up or clearing faster?
6. Does the figure match what the COGS / Inventory turnover ratio implies? If the two disagree, what explains the gap?
7. Has the company taken inventory writedowns recently? Did a writedown lower the figure without any real change in selling pace?
8. Is the inventory concentrated in fast-selling lines, or is slow, possibly outdated stock hidden inside the average?
9. How does the figure compare with direct competitors that sell similar goods? Which peer holds the least inventory for the same sales?
10. Does a falling figure line up with steady or improving gross margin, or is the company clearing stock only by discounting?
