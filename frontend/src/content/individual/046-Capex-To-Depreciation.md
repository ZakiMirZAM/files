# Capex To Depreciation

## Part 1: Definition

Capex to depreciation compares what a company spends on long-lived assets this year against what its accounting says those assets wore out. Capex is short for capital expenditures, the money spent on equipment, buildings, and other assets that last for years. Depreciation is the yearly accounting charge that spreads the cost of those assets over their useful lives. The ratio divides one by the other. The result shows whether the company is investing faster or slower than its assets are being used up.

The ratio answers one question: is the company building its asset base, holding it steady, or letting it shrink? A ratio of 1.0 means new spending exactly matches the wear recorded that year, so the asset base holds steady. A ratio of 2.0 means the company is investing twice as fast as its assets depreciate, so the base is growing. A ratio of 0.6 means spending is well below the recorded wear, so the asset base is quietly shrinking.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | Spending is below recorded wear. The asset base is shrinking. Reported profit may look better than the underlying business. |
| About 1.0 | Spending matches wear. The company is maintaining its asset base without expanding it. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | The company is investing for growth, adding capacity beyond simple replacement. |
| Above 2.0 | Heavy expansion. The company is building capacity well ahead of current needs. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Capex To Depreciation = Capital Expenditures ÷ Depreciation`

Capex is the top number. Capital expenditures is the cash spent during the year on equipment, buildings, and other long-lived assets, taken from the cash flow statement. Capex sits on top because the ratio is testing the pace of fresh investment.

Depreciation is the bottom number. Depreciation is the accounting estimate of how much of the existing asset base was used up during the year. The charge is non-cash, meaning no money leaves the company when it is recorded. Depreciation serves as a rough yardstick for the spending needed just to replace worn assets. Dividing this year's spending by this year's wear shows whether the company is investing above or below the replacement level. Some companies group depreciation with amortization, the matching charge for intangible assets. The cleanest version of this ratio compares capex with depreciation on physical assets alone.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A single year of capex tells little because spending is lumpy. A company may spend almost nothing for several years and then make one large replacement. Setting capex against depreciation smooths that out by measuring spending against a steady yardstick of wear. The ratio shows whether, over time, the company is keeping its productive assets intact.

The ratio also exposes a profit that is being flattered. A company can hold reported earnings up by spending less than its assets wear out. Lower spending leaves more cash in the short run and keeps the asset base looking unchanged on the surface. A ratio below 1.0 held for several years warns that the company is living off its existing assets, and that maintenance spending is being delayed into the future.

The ratio gauges reinvestment intensity, the rate at which a company puts money back into its physical base. High reinvestment can signal growth or a capital-hungry business model. Low reinvestment can signal a mature business returning cash to owners, or a company starving itself of the spending it needs.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The top number moves with the company's investment decisions. A growth phase, a new plant, or a major equipment upgrade lifts capex and the ratio. A pause in spending lowers both. Acquisitions can complicate the reading, because assets bought through a deal arrive without showing up as ordinary capex. The bottom number moves more slowly. Depreciation reflects past spending and the chosen useful lives of assets, so it changes only as old assets roll off and new ones come on.

Accounting choices can distort the ratio. A company that assumes long useful lives records low depreciation each year, which raises the ratio even with steady spending. Inflation distorts the comparison over time. Depreciation is based on the older, lower cost of assets bought years ago, while capex is spent at today's higher prices, so a ratio near 1.0 may still mean the asset base is shrinking in real terms. Write-downs reduce future depreciation, which lifts the ratio for reasons unrelated to fresh investment.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level of the ratio depends on how capital-hungry the industry is and on whether the company is growing or mature. A growing company in any industry runs the ratio above 1.0, while a mature one tends toward 1.0 or below.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Early-stage growth companies | Well above 1.0 | Heavy building of new capacity far outpaces the wear on a young asset base. |
| Capital-heavy industrials and utilities | Near 1.0 over time | Large asset bases require steady replacement just to keep running. |
| Mature, cash-returning companies | At or below 1.0 | Limited growth means spending settles near replacement or below it. |
| Asset-light software and services | Often low or noisy | Little physical capital means the ratio carries little meaning. |
| Commodity producers | Swings with the price cycle | Investment surges when prices are high and collapses when they fall. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when two companies have very different asset lives and growth stages. Judge the ratio against the company's own history and against direct competitors at the same point in their growth.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A ratio that holds steady above 1.0 means the company is investing for growth year after year. A ratio that holds near 1.0 means steady maintenance with little expansion. The pattern that deserves the most attention is a ratio that drifts below 1.0 and stays there. A sustained reading below replacement means the asset base is shrinking, and the spending being skipped today will have to be made up later or the business will slowly decline.

A single low year is often just timing, because large projects cluster and a quiet year can follow a heavy one. A single high year can mark one large project rather than a new trend. Read the ratio across several years to separate lumpy timing from a real shift in investment. Watch the ratio next to revenue. A ratio above 1.0 while revenue is flat can mean the company is spending heavily without the growth to show for it, which is a different concern from underspending.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Reinvestment Rate

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation measures physical investment against wear. The reinvestment rate measures total reinvestment, including working capital, against after-tax operating profit. Both describe how much the company is plowing back into the business, but from different angles.

**What the combination tells you.** A high capex-to-depreciation ratio with a high reinvestment rate confirms a company in a genuine building phase. A high ratio with a low reinvestment rate can mean the spending is being funded by shrinking working capital rather than profit. Reading both shows whether physical investment is part of a broader, well-funded growth plan or an isolated push.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the cash left after capex is paid out of operating cash flow. Capex to depreciation shows the pace of that capex against wear. The two are linked because heavy capex lowers free cash flow.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio above 1.0 alongside weak free cash flow means growth spending is consuming the company's cash, which can be healthy if the spending earns good returns. A ratio below 1.0 alongside strong free cash flow can mean the company is harvesting cash by underinvesting, which flatters today's cash flow at the cost of tomorrow's. Reading both shows whether reported cash flow is being earned or borrowed from the future.

### Capex Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Capex per share states the same spending on a per-share basis, so it can be tracked against earnings per share and dividends per share. Capex to depreciation puts that spending in the context of asset wear. One shows the size of spending per owner, the other its purpose.

**What the combination tells you.** Rising capex per share with a ratio above 1.0 means the company is investing more per owner to expand the asset base. Rising capex per share with a ratio near 1.0 means the extra spending is going to replacement rather than growth. Reading both connects the cash being committed to whether it builds the business or merely maintains it.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio rests on depreciation, which is an accounting estimate rather than a measure of real wear. A company that chooses long useful lives records low depreciation and shows a high ratio, even if its assets are aging. Inflation makes the comparison unreliable over long periods, because depreciation reflects old, lower asset costs while capex is spent at today's prices. A ratio of exactly 1.0 can still leave the real asset base shrinking once inflation is taken into account.

The ratio does not separate growth spending from maintenance spending. A high reading could mean the company is expanding, or it could mean replacement costs have simply risen. The ratio also misses assets that arrive through acquisitions rather than ordinary capex, so a serial acquirer can show a low ratio while its real asset base grows. For asset-light businesses the ratio carries little signal, because so little of their value sits in physical assets. The ratio is a useful gauge of reinvestment intensity and a poor measure of investment quality, since it says nothing about whether the spending will earn a good return.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio above or below 1.0, and has it stayed there long enough to signal a real trend rather than lumpy timing?
2. If the ratio is below 1.0, is the company deliberately harvesting cash, or is it delaying maintenance it will have to make up later?
3. How long are the useful lives the company assigns to its assets, and do they make depreciation look low and the ratio look high?
4. Once inflation is considered, does a ratio near 1.0 actually keep the asset base intact, or is it shrinking in real terms?
5. Is recent asset growth coming through capex on the chart, or arriving through acquisitions that bypass capex?
6. Does the ratio sit above 1.0 while revenue is flat, suggesting spending without growth to show for it?
7. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors at the same stage of growth?
8. Does the high spending behind a ratio above 1.0 line up with a high reinvestment rate, or is it funded another way?
9. Is the spending behind the ratio earning a good return, or is the company investing heavily into low-return projects?
10. Has a recent write-down reduced depreciation and lifted the ratio without any change in actual investment?
