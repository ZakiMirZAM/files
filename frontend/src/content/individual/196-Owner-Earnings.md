# Owner Earnings

## Part 1: Definition

Owner earnings estimate the cash a shareholder could take out of a business each year without weakening the company's competitive position. Warren Buffett proposed the measure in his 1986 letter to shareholders. Reported profit is an accounting result, and cash flow figures mix upkeep spending with expansion spending. Owner earnings sit between the two: the measure starts from reported profit, adds back non-cash charges, and then subtracts only the spending needed to stand still.

The metric answers one question: what does this business truly earn for its owners after paying for its own upkeep? Net income is the profit left after all expenses and taxes. Depreciation and amortization are non-cash charges that spread the cost of long-lived assets across the years those assets serve. Capital spending is the money paid for equipment, buildings, and other long-lived assets. Working capital is the money tied up in day-to-day operations, such as inventory and unpaid customer bills. Free cash flow is the cash from operations left after all capital spending.

Common relationships worth reading:

| Pattern | What the pattern usually means |
|---|---|
| Owner earnings close to net income | Upkeep costs match the depreciation charge and working capital is calm. Reported profit is a fair picture of owner cash. |
| Owner earnings well below net income | Working capital keeps absorbing cash as the business grows or collects slowly. Profit exists on paper before it exists in the bank. |
| Owner earnings well above free cash flow | A large share of capital spending is expansion rather than upkeep. The business earns well and reinvests heavily by choice. |
| Owner earnings swinging year to year | Working capital changes dominate the figure. Judge the average across several years instead of any single year. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Owner earnings = net income + depreciation and amortization − maintenance capital spending + change in working capital`

Net income is the starting point, the bottom line of the income statement. Depreciation and amortization are added back because those charges take no cash in the current year. Maintenance capital spending is then subtracted, meaning the part of capital spending that only keeps existing operations running at current volume. Buffett's original definition also counts any extra working capital the business must absorb to hold its position, and the app applies the change in working capital exactly as the cash-flow statement reports it: positive when operations released cash and negative when operations absorbed cash.

Maintenance capital spending appears in no financial statement, and Buffett himself called the number a guess. The app estimates maintenance spending with depreciation and amortization itself and states that assumption openly on the page. The proxy treats the wear the accounts recognize as the cost of standing still, so any capital spending beyond the proxy counts as growth investment. The app shows free cash flow beside owner earnings, along with the growth share of capital spending and a five-year history of the figure. A per-share version uses the diluted share count, which includes stock options and other claims that can become shares.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Buffett's argument targets both standard measures at once. Reported earnings overstate what owners can withdraw whenever true upkeep costs exceed the depreciation charge, which happens routinely when inflation makes replacement equipment cost more than the old equipment being written off. Free cash flow errs in the other direction by charging today's owner for expansion that benefits future years. Owner earnings charge the business for standing still and nothing more, which is the figure a valuation actually needs.

The measure also accepts imprecision on purpose. Maintenance spending is an estimate, so owner earnings never carry the false exactness of reported profit. Buffett preferred a roughly right measure of owner cash to a precisely wrong one, and the app follows the same choice by stating its proxy instead of hiding it.

A related idea travels with the metric. The app raises a flag when a large share of pretax profit arrives from outside normal operations, because part of the true earnings may then sit in holdings the statements never consolidate. Buffett called the full version look-through earnings. Accounting rules record only the dividends received from holdings below 20 percent ownership, so the investor's share of the profits those companies retain goes unrecorded. Look-through earnings add that unrecorded share back. The app cannot compute the number, since investee retained earnings are not in its data, so the flag marks companies where reported figures understate the earning power of what the company owns.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Net income carries every accounting choice made above it, so one-time gains and unusual tax years pass straight into owner earnings. The working capital term is the most volatile input. A year of heavy inventory building or slow customer collection absorbs cash and drags the figure down, while a year that releases working capital inflates the figure without any change in earning power. The depreciation add-back and the maintenance subtraction cancel each other under the app's proxy, which quietly makes the working capital swing the main difference between owner earnings and net income in any single year.

The proxy itself is an influence. When a company's true upkeep cost exceeds depreciation, owner earnings as computed here overstate owner cash, and the error grows with asset age and inflation. When depreciation runs above true upkeep, common right after heavy building years, the figure understates owner cash. The growth share of capital spending shown beside the metric helps size the gap: a company spending twice its depreciation is either growing fast or quietly paying upkeep the proxy misses.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-light software and services | Owner earnings track net income closely | Little physical equipment means the maintenance proxy is small and mostly harmless. Working capital is the term to watch. |
| Heavy manufacturing and transport | Proxy most likely to flatter | Long-lived equipment bought years ago depreciates at old prices while replacement happens at new prices, so true upkeep exceeds the charge. |
| Retail and distribution | Working capital dominates | Inventory swings move the figure sharply from year to year, and the multi-year average matters more than any single reading. |
| Commodity producers | Weak fit | Standing still means replacing depleted reserves, and depreciation is a poor guide to what replacement actually costs. |
| Banks and insurers | Concept breaks down | Capital spending and working capital have no clean meaning for financial companies, so read the figure as noise there. |
| Serial acquirers | Understated cost of standing still | Acquisitions that substitute for capital spending sit in the investing section the formula never touches, so part of the true upkeep is missing from the calculation. |

Comparing owner earnings margins across sectors mainly compares capital intensity and inventory habits. The honest use is within one company across time or between direct competitors with similar assets.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The five-year history answers a different question than any single year. Owner earnings running persistently below net income mark a business whose paper profit keeps outpacing its cash, and the gap deserves an explanation before the earnings are trusted. Persistent readings above net income usually mean working capital keeps releasing cash, and a release cannot continue forever. A single spike or crash is usually a working capital event rather than a change in earning power.

Compare the owner earnings line with the free cash flow line the app shows beside it. A stable gap between the two is the growth spending choice, made year after year. A widening gap with flat revenue is the warning pattern, because spending labeled growth that produces no growth is probably misclassified upkeep.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow subtracts every dollar of capital spending, while owner earnings subtract only the estimated upkeep portion. The difference between the two figures is exactly the spending the company chose to make for growth. The app displays both so the split is visible rather than buried.

**What the combination tells you.** Owner earnings far above free cash flow describe a business investing heavily by choice, and the investment record decides whether that gap is being spent well. Nearly equal figures mean the company spends little beyond upkeep, so the cash is real but the growth ambitions are modest. Free cash flow above owner earnings is rare and usually temporary, since it means spending has fallen below even the estimated cost of standing still.

### Income Quality (CFO / NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income quality compares operating cash flow with net income and asks whether reported profit turns into cash. Owner earnings start from that same net income and adjust it toward an owner's perspective. Both metrics attack the same weakness of reported profit from different angles, one through cash conversion and one through upkeep cost.

**What the combination tells you.** Weak income quality alongside owner earnings below net income is a double warning that profit exists mostly on paper. Strong income quality with healthy owner earnings confirms the profit is cash and the cash survives upkeep. When the two disagree the working capital line usually explains why, since working capital sits inside both calculations.

### Capex To Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation measures total capital spending against the depreciation charge, which is precisely the ratio behind this metric's maintenance proxy. A ratio near 1 means the company spends about what the proxy assumes upkeep costs. A ratio far above 1 means the formula is classifying a large block of spending as growth.

**What the combination tells you.** Owner earnings deserve more trust when capex to depreciation sits near 1, because the guesswork in the maintenance proxy then matters little. A high ratio with strong revenue growth supports the growth label the formula applies. A high ratio with stagnant revenue suggests part of that spending is really upkeep, which means owner earnings are overstated and the proxy is too generous.

### EPS

**Why read the two together.** Earnings per share is net income divided by the number of shares outstanding, and EPS is the profit figure most prices and headlines quote. Owner earnings per share restate that same profit after the cost of standing still and the cash absorbed by operations. Setting the two side by side shows how much of the headline number an owner could actually have taken out.

**What the combination tells you.** A persistent gap where owner earnings per share run below EPS means the market may be capitalizing profit the owners never see. Matching figures across several years support paying normal earnings multiples with confidence. The direction of the gap over time matters most, since a widening shortfall often precedes disappointments that EPS alone reveals late.

## Part 8: Limitations

The maintenance estimate is the weakest input in the calculation. Depreciation is an accounting schedule driven by asset lives and purchase prices from years ago, and nothing forces the schedule to match what upkeep actually costs today. Buffett's own definition calls for the average spending needed to preserve competitive position over the long run, and no formula extracts that number from filings. The app's proxy is stated honestly and remains a guess, exactly as the original essay warned.

The other inputs bring familiar problems. Net income carries one-time items and accounting judgment. The working capital term makes any single year noisy, which is why the five-year history exists. The look-through flag marks unconsolidated earning power but cannot measure it, so companies with large investment holdings stay understated. Owner earnings sharpen the question of what a business really earns for its owners. The figure does not settle the question by itself.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Why do owner earnings sit above or below net income this year? Is the gap a working capital event or a lasting feature of the business?
2. Does depreciation look like an honest estimate of upkeep here? How old is the asset base, and what has inflation done to replacement prices since those assets were bought?
3. What share of capital spending does the proxy label as growth, and has revenue actually grown in line with that supposed growth spending?
4. Is the working capital change this year a release or an absorption? Which specific line moved, inventory or receivables or payables?
5. Would owner earnings averaged over five years tell a different story than the latest year? Which of the two is the fairer base for valuing the company?
6. Does the look-through flag appear for this company? What holdings produce the outside income, and what earnings do those holdings retain that never reach these statements?
7. Do acquisitions substitute for capital spending here? Would adding acquisition spending to the upkeep estimate change the conclusion?
8. How does owner earnings per share compare with EPS across the whole history shown? Is the gap stable, widening, or narrowing?
9. If the company stopped all growth spending tomorrow, what would be left for owners each year, and does today's price look reasonable against that number?
10. Which management statements or disclosures confirm how much spending maintenance actually requires, and do those statements agree with the depreciation-based proxy?
