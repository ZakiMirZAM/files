# Excess Return (ROE − Ke)

## Part 1: Definition

Excess return measures whether a company earns more on its shareholders' money than those shareholders require. It takes the return on equity and subtracts the cost of equity. Return on equity, written ROE, is the profit the company earns as a percentage of the equity the owners have put in. The cost of equity, written Ke, is the return those owners require for taking the risk of owning the stock. The difference between the two is the excess return, the surplus the company earns above the shareholders' hurdle.

This is the most direct test of value creation for shareholders. A positive excess return means the company earns more than its owners demand, so it is adding to their wealth. A negative excess return means the company earns less than its owners demand, so it is destroying value even if it still reports a profit. Because the test compares the actual return with the required return, it gives a clear yes-or-no answer where return on equity alone only gives a number.

| Excess return | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Clearly positive | The company earns well above what shareholders require. It creates value, and its price-to-book should sit above one. |
| About zero | The company earns roughly what shareholders require. Owners are no better or worse off than in a similar investment. |
| Negative | The company earns less than shareholders require. It destroys value, and its price-to-book should sit below one. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Excess Return = ROE − Ke`

Return on equity is net income divided by shareholders' equity, the profit earned for every dollar of owners' money. The cost of equity in this app comes from CAPM, the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium. Both are percentages, so the excess return is simply one percentage minus the other, stated in percentage points.

If return on equity is 15 percent and the cost of equity is 10 percent, the excess return is 5 percentage points. The company earned 5 points more than its owners required. If return on equity were 8 percent against the same 10 percent cost of equity, the excess return would be negative 2 points, a sign the company fell short of the hurdle. The calculation is plain, but the meaning is sharp: the sign of the number tells you whether value was created or destroyed, and the size tells you by how much.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Return on equity on its own can mislead. A 12 percent return on equity sounds good, but if shareholders require 14 percent for the risk they carry, the company is actually failing them. Excess return fixes this by putting return on equity against the right benchmark. It is the equity equivalent of the value-creation test that economic value added applies to total capital. Where economic value added asks whether the whole business beats its cost of capital, excess return asks the same question for the shareholders alone.

The metric also connects directly to how the stock should be valued. A company that consistently earns more than its cost of equity deserves to trade above its book value, because each dollar of equity is producing surplus returns. A company that earns less than its cost of equity deserves to trade below book value, because each dollar of equity is being eroded. So excess return is not just a scorecard, it is the engine behind the justified price-to-book multiple.

For banks and insurers, where the blended cost of capital is ill-defined, excess return is especially useful. The cost of equity is the natural hurdle for these businesses, so return on equity minus the cost of equity is the cleanest available value-creation test.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Excess return moves with both of its parts. On the return-on-equity side, anything that lifts profit relative to equity helps: wider margins, faster asset turnover, or more financial leverage. Leverage is worth watching, because borrowing can raise return on equity and the excess return without making the business itself any better, while quietly adding risk. A high excess return built mostly on debt is more fragile than the same excess return built on operating strength.

On the cost-of-equity side, the hurdle rises with the risk-free rate, with the company's beta, and with the equity risk premium. When interest rates climb, the cost of equity climbs, and the excess return shrinks even if return on equity holds steady. A more volatile stock with a higher beta carries a higher hurdle and a thinner excess return. Because the cost of equity is partly set by the market, the excess return can fall in a year when nothing in the operations changed, simply because the required return went up.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The excess return depends on how profitable an industry's equity is and how risky the market judges it to be. High-return, low-risk businesses show wide excess returns, while capital-heavy or volatile ones struggle to clear the hurdle.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and asset-light services | Often wide and positive | High return on equity and moderate betas leave a comfortable surplus over the hurdle. |
| Consumer brands with pricing power | Positive | Strong, steady return on equity clears a low-to-moderate cost of equity. |
| Industrial and manufacturing | Thin and cyclical | Return on equity swings with the cycle while the cost of equity stays elevated by a higher beta. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Small but stable | Regulated returns on equity sit just above a low cost of equity. |
| Banks and insurers | The key value test | The cost of equity is the proper hurdle, so excess return is the main measure of value creation. |

Compare excess returns within an industry. A negative excess return is more worrying in a stable industry than in a deeply cyclical one caught in a downturn, where the figure may recover with the cycle.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steadily positive excess return that holds across good years and bad is the mark of a durable franchise. It means the company keeps earning more than shareholders require regardless of conditions. A rising excess return shows the company is widening the gap between what it earns and what its owners demand, either by lifting return on equity or by operating in a calmer, cheaper cost-of-equity environment.

A declining excess return is a warning. If it falls because return on equity is sliding, the business is weakening. If it falls because the cost of equity is rising with interest rates, the cause is external, and the gap may recover when rates ease. Separate the two by checking which side moved. Watch also for an excess return propped up by rising leverage, where return on equity climbs only because the company keeps borrowing. That kind of improvement adds risk rather than quality, and it tends to reverse sharply when conditions turn.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Justified P/B

**Why read the two together.** Excess return and justified price-to-book are two faces of the same fundamentals. A positive excess return is what pushes the justified price-to-book above one, and a negative excess return is what pulls it below one. The excess return is the driver, and the justified multiple is the result.

**What the combination tells you.** When the excess return is positive, check whether the actual price-to-book is above one, as it should be, and whether it sits below the justified level, which would make it cheap. A positive excess return with an actual price-to-book below the justified figure is a promising combination. A negative excess return with a price-to-book still above one suggests the market is paying for value the company is not creating.

### Cost of Equity

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity is the hurdle subtracted inside the excess return. It sets the bar that return on equity has to clear. Reading it separately shows whether a change in the excess return came from the company's profitability or from a shift in what shareholders require.

**What the combination tells you.** If the excess return shrank while the cost of equity rose, the operations may be fine and the cause external, such as higher interest rates. If the excess return shrank while the cost of equity held steady, return on equity itself is falling and the business is weakening. Reading both pinpoints which side of the equation moved.

### Return on Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is the profitability side of the excess return. On its own it shows what the company earned on owners' money, but it cannot say whether that was enough. The excess return adds the missing benchmark.

**What the combination tells you.** A high return on equity with a thin or negative excess return means the hurdle is high, often because the stock is risky, so even strong profitability is not clearing the bar. A modest return on equity with a positive excess return means the company is creating value despite unremarkable headline profitability, because its hurdle is low. Reading both stops a high return on equity from being mistaken for value creation on its own.

## Part 8: Limitations

Excess return inherits every weakness of its two inputs. The cost of equity is an estimate built from a model that needs a beta, a risk-free rate, and an equity risk premium, each of which is debatable, so the hurdle is uncertain and a small change in it can flip the sign of the excess return. Return on equity has its own problems: it can be inflated by heavy borrowing, distorted by share buybacks that shrink equity, or thrown off by one-time gains in net income.

A company with very little or negative book equity can show a wild return on equity and therefore a meaningless excess return. The metric is also backward-looking, measuring value created on existing equity, and it says nothing about future opportunities. Because both inputs are sensitive to assumptions and accounting, the excess return is best read as a direction and a rough size rather than a precise figure, and a single year near zero should not be over-interpreted.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the excess return positive across the worst year on the chart, or only in good years?
2. Is a positive excess return built on operating strength, or mostly on financial leverage that adds risk?
3. Did a recent fall in the excess return come from a weaker return on equity, or from a higher cost of equity driven by rates?
4. What beta is feeding the cost of equity, and would a different beta change the sign of the excess return?
5. Is return on equity being distorted by buybacks that have shrunk the equity base or by one-time gains in net income?
6. Does the actual price-to-book agree with the excess return, with a multiple above one when the excess return is positive?
7. For a bank or insurer, is excess return being used as the main value-creation test in place of the cost-of-capital spread?
8. How wide is the excess return, and is it large enough to survive a one or two point rise in the cost of equity?
9. Is the company's book equity large and stable enough for return on equity, and therefore the excess return, to be meaningful?
10. If the excess return is negative, is the business in a cyclical trough that should recover, or in a lasting decline?
