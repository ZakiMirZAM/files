# Selling And Marketing Expenses

## Part 1: Definition

Selling and marketing expense is the cost of winning and keeping customers. The line collects the salesforce and its commissions, advertising, brand campaigns, promotions, distribution to retailers, and the staff and software that support selling. The line sits below gross profit on the income statement, in the block of operating costs that come after the cost of the product itself. The line answers one question: how much does the company spend to turn its products into sales?

A beginner term to define first is operating cost. An operating cost is a cost of running the business that is not the direct cost of making the product. Selling and marketing expense is one such operating cost. In the vertical analysis table the app shows this line as a percent of revenue. A reading of 12 percent means the company spends twelve cents of every sales dollar on selling and marketing.

Common readings of the line as a percent of revenue:

| Selling and marketing share of revenue | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 5 percent | Light selling cost. Common where products sell themselves or where one big customer buys in bulk. |
| 5 to 15 percent | Typical for many established product companies. |
| 15 to 30 percent | Heavy selling cost. Common in consumer brands and companies chasing fast growth. |
| Above 30 percent | Very heavy. The company is buying growth, defending a brand under attack, or selling a product that is hard to move. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

This line is not a ratio. The company reports the dollar figure directly. The app converts the figure to a percent of revenue for the vertical analysis table:

`Selling And Marketing Share = Selling And Marketing Expenses ÷ Revenue`

Selling and marketing expense is the top number. The line adds together the pay and commissions of the sales team, advertising and promotion, the cost of getting the product to the point of sale, and the marketing staff and tools that run all of this. Depreciation on assets used by the sales team is often folded in here too. Depreciation is the yearly charge that spreads the cost of a long-lived asset over its useful life.

Revenue is the bottom number. Revenue is the total sales the company made in the year. Dividing by revenue states the selling cost in cents per sales dollar, which lets a small company and a large company be compared on the same scale. Some companies report selling and marketing combined with administrative cost in one line called SG&A. In that case the separate selling figure may not exist, and this row will be blank.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Selling and marketing spending shows how hard the company has to work to sell what it makes. A company whose product pulls customers in needs less of this spending. A company whose product must be pushed onto customers needs more. The share of revenue spent here captures that difference in one number.

The line also reveals the engine behind revenue growth. Revenue that rises while selling cost stays flat as a percent of sales is growth the company is earning cheaply. Revenue that rises only because selling cost is rising faster is growth the company is buying. The two look identical at the top line and very different here.

The line carries a hidden quality that gross profit does not show. Much of this spending builds brand awareness and customer relationships that pay off in later years. The accounting treats all of the spending as a cost of the current year. A company can lift this year's profit by cutting advertising, while quietly weakening the demand that feeds future years.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The business stage moves the line the most. A company entering new markets or launching products spends heavily to get noticed, so the share of revenue runs high. A mature company with a known brand spends less to hold the same customers, so the share runs low. The same company can show a high share early and a low share once the product is established.

The selling model matters. A company that sells through its own salesforce carries commissions and salaries here. A company that sells through other retailers may carry less, because the retailer does the selling. Pricing and promotion swing the line as well. Heavy discounting and coupon programs often land in this line and lift the share in a weak demand year.

One-off items can distort a single year. A large one-time brand campaign, a product launch, or the marketing cost of entering a new country can spike the line for a year and then fall away. A reader should separate a permanent rise in selling intensity from a one-year burst of spending.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as high or low depends entirely on how the sector reaches its customers.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer brands and household goods | High, often 15 to 30 percent | Brand advertising is the main weapon, and the spending must repeat every year to hold shelf space. |
| Software and subscription companies | High during growth | Customer acquisition is the main cost of growth, and the spending is front-loaded before the revenue arrives. |
| Industrial and capital equipment | Low to moderate | Sales come through a small technical salesforce and long relationships rather than mass advertising. |
| Commodity producers | Very low | Output is sold into a market at a market price, so little selling effort is needed. |
| Wholesale and distribution | Low | Thin margins leave little room for advertising, and volume comes through price and availability. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two companies reach customers in different ways. A consumer brand and a commodity producer cannot be judged by the same selling-cost share, because one must advertise to survive and the other does not.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read this line as a percent of revenue across the multi-year chart, not as a raw dollar figure. The dollar figure rises with the size of the company and tells you little on its own. The share of revenue tells you whether selling is getting cheaper or more expensive relative to the sales it produces.

A share that falls steadily while revenue grows is the strongest pattern. A falling share with rising sales means the company is gaining customers without spending more to do it, which points to a strengthening brand or product. A share that rises steadily means each new sales dollar costs more to win, which can signal fading demand, tougher competition, or a growth push that may not last. A sudden one-year jump is usually a launch or campaign rather than a permanent change. A share that drops sharply in a weak year deserves suspicion, because cutting advertising is an easy way to dress up current profit at the expense of future demand.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Selling General And Administrative Expenses

**Why read the two together.** Selling and marketing expense is one half of SG&A, the combined operating-cost line that adds selling cost to general and administrative cost. Reading the selling line beside the SG&A total shows how much of the company's overhead is the cost of selling rather than the cost of running the corporate office. A company can hold its SG&A total flat while the selling half quietly grows and the administrative half shrinks.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising selling share inside a flat SG&A total means the company is shifting money toward winning customers and away from back-office cost. A falling selling share inside a flat total can mean the opposite, that administrative cost is creeping up while selling effort is cut. Watching the split over time separates a company that is investing in demand from one that is letting overhead expand. The two lines together describe where the operating budget is really going.

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of the product, measured before any selling cost. Selling and marketing expense is the next cost subtracted after gross margin. A high gross margin gives the company room to spend on selling, while a thin gross margin leaves almost nothing for it.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high gross margin and a high selling share is reinvesting its product profit into demand, which can be healthy if revenue responds. A company with a thin gross margin and a high selling share is in a squeeze, because the selling cost eats the little profit the product earns. Reading the two lines together shows whether the selling spend is affordable or whether it is draining a profit the product can barely produce.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is operating income as a percent of revenue, the profit left after all operating costs including selling. Selling and marketing expense is one of the largest costs standing between gross margin and EBIT margin. A change in selling intensity flows straight through to the EBIT margin.

**What the combination tells you.** When EBIT margin falls and the selling share is the line that rose, the profit pressure is coming from the cost of winning customers rather than from the product itself. When EBIT margin holds steady while the selling share rises, some other cost must be falling to offset it. Reading the two together locates which operating cost is driving the change in overall profitability.

## Part 8: Limitations

This line mixes spending that builds long-term value with spending that buys only this year's sales, and the accounting shows them as one cost. Brand advertising that strengthens demand for years and a one-time discount to clear inventory both land here. A reader cannot tell from the single number how much of the spending is investment and how much is just the running cost of staying in business.

Reporting choices reduce comparability. Many companies bury selling cost inside a combined SG&A line, so the separate figure does not exist for them. Companies that sell through retailers carry less selling cost than companies that sell directly, which makes the same product look cheaper to sell for one and dearer for the other. The line also says nothing about whether the spending worked. A high selling share is good if it produced fast growth and bad if revenue stayed flat, and the line alone cannot tell which happened.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is selling and marketing as a percent of revenue this year, and how does that compare with the share three and five years ago? Is the trend up or down?
2. Did revenue grow because selling cost grew, or did revenue grow while the selling share held steady or fell?
3. Is the current level inflated by a one-time campaign or product launch, or is it the company's normal run rate?
4. Does the company report selling separately, or only inside a combined SG&A line that hides how much is selling cost?
5. Does the company sell through its own salesforce or through outside retailers, and how does that change where the selling cost shows up?
6. In the most recent weak year, did the company cut advertising to protect reported profit? What might that do to demand in the following years?
7. How does the selling share compare with direct competitors that reach customers the same way? Is this company spending more or less to win the same sale?
8. How much of this spending builds lasting brand and customer relationships, and how much is short-term discounting that must be repeated every year?
9. Is the gross margin high enough to fund the current selling share, or is selling cost consuming most of the product's profit?
10. If the company stopped growing, how far could the selling share fall, and what would that do to the EBIT margin?
