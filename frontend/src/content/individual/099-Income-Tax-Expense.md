# Income Tax Expense

## Part 1: Definition

Income tax expense is the charge a company records for the income taxes owed on its profit for the year. The figure has two parts. The current part is the tax actually assessed on this year's taxable income. The deferred part is an accounting adjustment that reconciles the timing difference between the profit reported to investors and the profit reported to the tax authority. Taxable income is the profit figure the tax rules produce, which often differs from the profit in the financial statements.

In the common-size table the line's share of revenue shows the tax charge against the size of the business. Measured against income before tax, the line gives the effective tax rate. Effective tax rate is tax expense divided by pretax profit. The effective rate is the share the tax authority takes, and one minus that share is the keep-rate that the Tax Burden factor measures.

Common readings of the effective rate:

| Effective rate level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near the home statutory rate | The company is taxed close to the standard rate of its home country. |
| Well below the statutory rate | Foreign operations, credits, or special structures lower the rate. The durability of those is the key question. |
| Above the statutory rate | Losses in some places, non-deductible items, or one-time charges raised the rate. |
| Swinging year to year | One-time items, audit settlements, or stock-compensation effects are moving the rate. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The provision builds up from a baseline:

`Income Tax Expense = Current tax + Deferred tax`

The current part starts from pretax income taxed at the home statutory rate, then adjusts for the items the tax footnote lists. Those items include foreign rate differences from operating in lower-tax or higher-tax countries, tax credits for research or energy, state and local taxes, the tax effects of stock compensation that move with the share price, changes in allowances against deferred tax assets, and settlements with tax authorities. The deferred part captures the motion in timing differences, such as faster tax depreciation than book depreciation, and the use or creation of loss carryforwards. A loss carryforward is a past loss that can offset tax on future profit.

The tax footnote is the real text of this line. The rate reconciliation names each driver and its contribution to the rate. The deferred schedule shows the stock of timing differences. The unrecognized-benefits table holds the contested positions the company has taken. A reader who wants to judge the rate's durability reads the footnote, because the single line on the face of the statement hides the drivers.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The tax line is a major claim on profit whose drivers are unusually visible. The components of the effective rate are disclosed in the footnote in a way that almost no other expense is. That disclosure makes the durability question tractable. The rate's drift can be split into law changes, country mix, and one-time items. The durability of the rate matters because a low rate that cannot last inflates today's earnings.

The line feeds the valuation models directly. The app's discounted-cash-flow models use the company's own effective rate when projecting future after-tax cash flow. A rate that will not hold makes the intrinsic value estimate too high. The rate is literally an input to value.

The gap between the tax expense and the cash actually paid in tax matters for cash-flow analysis. The deferred part of the expense is an accrual, not a cash payment. The cash taxes appear separately in the cash-flow statement. Comparing the two shows how much of the recorded tax has actually been paid, which feeds the income-quality work that checks reported profit against cash.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Tax law sets the baseline rate. The mix of countries the company operates in blends the rates, with profit in low-tax places pulling the effective rate down. Credits and incentives subtract from the charge. One-time items punctuate the rate in single years. The share price moves the stock-compensation tax effects, which flatter the rate when the stock rises and reverse when it falls. Audit settlements arrive on their own schedule.

The deferred part adds its own drivers. Timing differences move with the capital-spending cycle, because faster tax depreciation during a building phase creates deferred tax liabilities. Loss carryforwards are consumed as past losses shelter current profit. Changes in valuation allowances on deferred tax assets are judgment calls, and releasing an allowance can produce a large one-time gain in the tax line. The recognition of deferred tax assets when a company turns profitable is among the largest one-time swings this line produces.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The sector patterns follow where the rate-lowering opportunities are. Multinational technology and pharmaceutical companies have historically run light effective rates through country structuring. Domestic operators run near the statutory rate. Sensitivity to tax reform concentrates where the gap between the effective rate and the statutory rate is widest.

| Industry type | Typical tax behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Multinational technology and pharma | Historically low effective rates | Profit booked in lower-tax countries pulls the rate down |
| Domestic-only operators | Near the statutory rate | Little foreign profit to lower the rate |
| Capital-intensive operators | Persistent deferred tax liabilities | Faster tax depreciation than book depreciation defers tax |
| Loss-history companies | Noisy rates | Carryforwards and allowance changes swing the rate |
| REITs and pass-through structures | Little or no corporate tax | Tax is paid by the investors, not the company |

A cross-sector comparison of effective rates is meaningless when the companies have very different country footprints, because the country mix drives most of the gap.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the line's own multi-year series first, then read its share-of-pretax series, which is the effective rate's history. Normalize the one-time items before judging the trend, because audit settlements and allowance releases distort single years. Split the drift using the reconciliation's rows, and judge durability against the statutory rate and any reform on the horizon.

The mix of current and deferred tax adds a cash dimension to the trend. Years heavy with deferred tax mean the book rate sits above or below the cash rate, and the persistent gaps pile up on the balance sheet as deferred tax balances. The stock-compensation effects track the share price, flattering the rate during a rising market and reversing during a falling one. A rate that improves only because the stock is rising is not a durable improvement, and the trend should be read with that driver removed.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Tax Burden

**Why read the two together.** Tax burden is the DuPont factor that measures the share of pretax profit kept after tax, figured as net income divided by pretax income. Income tax expense is the charge that determines that keep-rate. The two are the same fact seen from opposite sides, one as the amount taken and one as the share kept. Reading them together connects the tax line to the return-on-equity arithmetic.

**What the combination tells you.** A high tax burden factor means the effective tax rate is low and most of the pretax profit survives. A falling tax burden factor signals a rising effective rate that is eating earnings growth. Watching both across the years shows whether the company's after-tax profit is benefiting from a tax advantage and whether that advantage is holding. A tax advantage that reverses pulls the keep-rate down with it.

### Income Before Tax

**Why read the two together.** Income before tax is the base the tax is charged on. Income tax expense divided by income before tax gives the effective tax rate. The pretax line is the denominator and the tax line is the numerator of that rate. Neither figure means much for tax analysis without the other.

**What the combination tells you.** A tax expense that grew faster than pretax income means the effective rate rose, which points to a less favorable country mix or expiring benefits. A tax expense that grew in step with pretax income means the rate held steady. Across the years the pair shows whether the company keeps a stable share of its profit after tax. A stable rate makes the earnings easier to value.

### Income Quality (CFO / NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income quality compares cash from operations against net income to test whether reported profit turned into cash. The deferred part of the tax expense is an accrual that does not match the cash actually paid in tax. Reading the two together connects the tax accrual to the cash-versus-profit gap that income quality measures. The deferred tax balance is one of the items that can drive a wedge between profit and cash.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with large deferred tax benefits will report lower tax expense than the cash it pays, or the reverse, depending on the direction of the timing differences. A persistent gap between book tax and cash tax shows up in the income-quality read. Watching both shows whether the reported after-tax profit is supported by cash. A reported profit that leans on deferred tax accruals is lower quality than one matched by cash taxes paid.

## Part 8: Limitations

The provision is heavy with estimates. The reserves for contested positions and the allowances on deferred tax assets are both judgment calls. The true rate for a year emerges only as audits close years later. The gap between book tax and cash tax makes the expense an imperfect measure of the actual cash claim. One-time items make single years noisy, and the footnote's complexity buries the drivers that the analysis needs.

The drivers of the line are substantially outside management's control. Tax law and audit outcomes are set by governments, not by the company, so forecasting the rate calls for humility. A low rate today can be raised by legislation tomorrow. The line is the tax authority's claim, disclosed better than most expenses but settled less certainly than most, and it should be read through the footnote with the cash taxes from the cash-flow statement beside it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What does the rate reconciliation name as the drivers of the effective rate, and which drivers are durable versus one-time?
2. How does the cash tax rate from the cash-flow statement compare with the reported rate, and what deferred motion fills the gap?
3. What is the deferred schedule showing as it accrues, and under what conditions would the deferrals convert into cash payments?
4. How large are the unrecognized benefits, the contested positions, and what would losing them do to the rate and the cash?
5. What did changes in valuation allowances contribute, and were any earnings swings purely recognition events rather than performance?
6. How much of the rate's recent path is the share price moving the stock-compensation tax effects?
7. Where do current tax-reform proposals bear on this company's structure, and is a reversion to a higher rate priced in?
8. What loss carryforwards shelter current income, how long do they last, and what is the rate once they run out?
9. What tax rate do the valuation models assume, and does the footnote evidence support it?
10. If the rate moved back to the statutory blend of the company's footprint, what would the earnings, the quality score, and the intrinsic value become?
