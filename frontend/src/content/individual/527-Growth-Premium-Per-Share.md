# Growth Premium per Share

## Part 1: Definition

The growth premium per share is the part of a share's value that depends on future growth rather than on current earnings. The metric compares two values of the same company. The first is the no-growth value, called EPV per share, which is the earnings power value of one share if the business never grows. The second is the DCF intrinsic value per share, which is the value including the full forecast of growth. DCF stands for discounted cash flow, a method that projects future cash and discounts it back to a value today. The growth premium answers one question: how much of the estimated value comes from growth that has not happened yet?

The no-growth value is the floor. A company is worth at least its current earning power, whatever happens to growth. The DCF value adds the estimated worth of future growth on top of that floor. The gap between the two is the growth premium per share. A large premium means most of the estimated value rests on growth still to come, while a small premium means the value is anchored in earnings the company already produces.

Common readings of the growth premium relative to EPV per share:

| Growth premium versus EPV per share | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The value is almost entirely current earning power. The forecast adds little, so the valuation is easy to trust. |
| Modest | Some of the value depends on growth. The buyer should check that the growth is protected from competitors. |
| Large | Most of the value depends on growth that has not arrived. The valuation stands or falls on the forecast. |
| Negative | The forecast values the company below its no-growth floor, which points to expected shrinkage or heavy reinvestment at poor returns. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Growth Premium per Share = DCF Intrinsic Value per Share − EPV per Share`

The DCF intrinsic value per share is the first input. The DCF value is the app's full valuation, built by forecasting future cash flows and discounting them to a present value. The forecast includes revenue growth, changing margins, and the reinvestment needed to fund that growth. The DCF value therefore contains both the current earning power and the added worth of growth.

EPV per share is the second input, and it is subtracted. EPV per share is the no-growth value, sustainable after-tax operating earnings capitalized at the cost of capital, then bridged to a value per share. The cost of capital is the yearly return investors require to finance the company. Because EPV per share deliberately excludes growth, subtracting it from the DCF value leaves only the worth of growth. The remainder is the growth premium per share.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A single valuation number hides how much of it depends on a forecast. The growth premium per share pulls that dependence into the open. A small premium means the value rests on earnings the company already produces, which are observable. A large premium means the value rests on growth that has to be predicted, which is far less certain.

The premium also frames the central judgment a buyer must make. Paying a price above the no-growth floor is paying for growth, and the premium states exactly how much. Greenwald's rule is that growth creates value only when the company earns returns above its cost of capital behind a protected market position. A large premium is justified only if that protection is real and the returns are high.

The premium also focuses the research. When the premium is small, the work is to confirm that current earnings are durable. When the premium is large, the work is to test the growth forecast and the competitive protection behind it. Directing effort to the part of the value that carries the most risk is the practical use of the number.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The growth premium is a difference, so anything that moves either value moves the premium. A higher forecast growth rate raises the DCF value and widens the premium. A higher forecast margin or a longer forecast period does the same. Any input that makes the future look better than the present pushes the premium up.

The cost of capital pulls in two directions at once, because it sits in both values. A lower cost of capital raises the DCF value and EPV per share together, so the effect on the premium depends on which value it lifts more. Falling interest rates tend to widen the premium for fast growers, because their value is weighted toward distant cash flows. A weaker sustainable earnings base lowers EPV per share, which widens the premium even when the forecast has not changed.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal size of the premium depends on how much growth a sector can realistically fund at good returns. A large premium is expected in some sectors and a warning in others.

| Industry type | Typical premium behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer staples and mature industrials | Small premium | Growth is slow, so most of the value is current earning power. |
| High-growth software and internet | Large premium | The forecast prices years of expansion, so most of the value is growth. |
| Cyclical manufacturers and commodity producers | Distorted premium | A revenue peak inflates EPV per share and shrinks the premium, while a trough does the reverse. |
| Research-heavy pharma and technology | Overstated premium | Expensing research lowers EPV per share, so part of the premium is really hidden current earning power. |
| Banks and insurers | Not computed | The app does not build EPV for financial companies, so no premium is shown. |
| Companies with negative sustainable earnings | Not computed | With no no-growth floor to subtract, the premium cannot be measured. |

Comparing the premium across sectors mostly compares how growth-dependent each sector's valuation is. The comparison is meaningless unless the companies share similar margin stability and reinvestment needs.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The growth premium usually moves because the DCF value moves, since EPV per share is the steadier of the two. A widening premium over time means the forecast is leaning harder on growth, either because expectations have risen or because the no-growth floor has slipped. A narrowing premium means the value is settling back onto current earnings.

Watch which side is driving the change. A premium that widens because EPV per share is falling is a warning, because the floor under the value is dropping. A premium that widens because the DCF value is rising on stronger fundamentals is more benign, though it still raises the reliance on the forecast. A premium that swings sharply from year to year usually signals an unstable growth forecast rather than a real change in the business.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DCF Intrinsic Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** The growth premium per share is defined as the DCF intrinsic value per share minus EPV per share. The DCF value is one of the two ingredients, so the premium cannot be understood without it. Reading the DCF value alongside the premium shows how large the growth part is relative to the whole valuation. A premium that is a small slice of the DCF value is a valuation that barely depends on growth.

**What the combination tells you.** When the premium is a large share of the DCF value, most of the estimated worth is growth, and the whole valuation depends on the forecast holding up. When the premium is a small share of the DCF value, the valuation rests mostly on current earnings and is easier to trust. Tracking both numbers over time shows whether a rising valuation is coming from a stronger business or from a more aggressive growth forecast.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC is return on invested capital, the after-tax operating profit earned per dollar of capital in the business. The growth premium states how much of the value depends on growth, and ROIC states whether that growth can create value. Greenwald's rule is that growth adds value only when ROIC exceeds the cost of capital. The two together test whether a large premium is deserved.

**What the combination tells you.** A large growth premium paired with ROIC well above the cost of capital describes growth that can genuinely build value, so the premium may be earned. A large premium paired with ROIC near or below the cost of capital describes growth that consumes capital without creating value, so the premium is likely a mistake. A small premium makes ROIC less decisive, because little of the value is riding on growth in the first place.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** WACC is the weighted average cost of capital, the blended return that lenders and shareholders require. WACC sits inside both the DCF value and EPV per share, so it shapes the growth premium from both sides. A change in WACC can widen or narrow the premium without any change in the business. Reading the premium against WACC shows how much of it is a product of interest rates.

**What the combination tells you.** A large premium built on a low WACC is fragile, because a rise in rates lifts WACC and can shrink the premium sharply. A premium that survives a higher WACC is more dependable, because it does not rely on cheap money. Testing the premium at a WACC one point higher shows how much of the growth value is really a low-rate effect. The more the premium shrinks under that test, the more it depends on interest rates rather than the business.

## Part 8: Limitations

The growth premium is only as reliable as the two values behind it. The DCF value carries every uncertainty of a long forecast, and EPV per share carries the risk that its sustainable earnings estimate is wrong. An error in either value passes straight into the premium. A premium computed from a flattering forecast and a depressed floor can look far larger than the real economics justify.

The premium also cannot say, by itself, whether the growth it measures is good growth or bad growth. Value-creating growth and value-destroying growth can produce the same premium in the DCF value, so the number must be read next to a measure of returns on capital. A premium built on a revenue peak is overstated, because the inflated EPV per share is subtracted from an inflated DCF value in a way that does not cancel cleanly. The metric locates the growth part of the value but does not prove that the growth is worth paying for.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How large is the growth premium compared with EPV per share, and how much of the total value therefore depends on growth?
2. What growth rate and margin does the DCF forecast assume, and are those assumptions consistent with the company's history?
3. Does the company earn returns above its cost of capital, so that the growth in the premium can actually create value?
4. What keeps competitors from copying the company's returns, and how durable is that protection?
5. Is EPV per share depressed by a revenue trough or by expensed research, which would overstate the premium?
6. Is the DCF value inflated by a revenue peak, which would also distort the premium?
7. How much of a recent change in the premium came from the DCF value, and how much from a shift in EPV per share?
8. Would the premium survive a higher cost of capital, or does it mostly reflect low interest rates?
9. How does the premium compare with direct competitors, and does this company depend on growth more than its peers?
10. If the company delivered no growth at all, how much of the current price would be left unsupported?
