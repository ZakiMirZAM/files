# Cash Conversion Cycle

## Part 1: Definition

The cash conversion cycle measures how many days pass between when a company pays cash for its goods and when it collects cash from selling them. The cycle is stated in days. A shorter cycle means cash returns to the company quickly. A longer cycle means cash stays tied up in operations for longer and the company must fund the gap. The cycle answers one question: how long is the company's own cash locked inside the business before it comes back?

The cycle has three parts. Days inventory outstanding is the average time goods sit before they sell. Days sales outstanding is the average time customers take to pay after a sale. Days payables outstanding is the average time the company takes to pay its own suppliers. The first two delay the return of cash. The third delays the outflow of cash, which helps the company. The vault describes the cycle as the time that elapses from when a firm pays for raw materials to when it collects receivables.

Common readings of the cycle:

| Cycle level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | The company collects from customers before it pays suppliers. Suppliers fund the operations. A strong position. |
| 0 to 30 days | Cash is recovered quickly. Common for efficient retailers and fast sellers. |
| 30 to 90 days | A moderate cycle typical of many manufacturers and distributors. |
| Above 90 days | Cash stays locked up for months, so the company needs more financing to operate. |
| Very long | Common for makers of aged or slow-selling goods, where a long cycle is normal rather than a warning. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Cash Conversion Cycle = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Sales Outstanding − Days Payables Outstanding`

The first two inputs add to the cycle. Days inventory outstanding is the average number of days a good sits in stock before it sells. Days sales outstanding is the average number of days customers take to pay after the sale. Together these two form the operating cycle, the full time from buying goods to collecting the cash from selling them. The longer either runs, the longer the company's cash is committed.

The third input subtracts from the cycle. Days payables outstanding is the average number of days the company waits before paying its own suppliers. Every day the company delays paying is a day a supplier finances the operation rather than the company. Subtracting payables days reflects that supplier credit shortens the time the company's own cash is at risk. A company that pays suppliers as slowly as it collects from customers can drive the cycle toward zero or below.

| Component | What it measures | Effect on the cycle |
|---|---|---|
| Days Inventory Outstanding | Time goods sit before sale | Adds days |
| Days Sales Outstanding | Time customers take to pay | Adds days |
| Days Payables Outstanding | Time the company takes to pay suppliers | Subtracts days |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The cycle shows how much cash the day-to-day business swallows. A positive cycle means the company must fund the gap between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, and that funding costs money. The vault notes that a longer cycle means the firm requires more financing, so every firm has an incentive to reduce its cycle. The cycle states that financing need as one figure in days.

The cycle answers a question that the income statement does not. Profit can look healthy while cash sits frozen in inventory and unpaid customer bills. The cycle measures the timing of cash rather than the size of profit. A company can be profitable on paper yet starved of cash if its cycle keeps stretching.

The cycle is also a read on operating discipline. Shortening it means selling stock faster, collecting from customers sooner, or paying suppliers later without harming those relationships. A company that drives its cycle down frees up cash that would otherwise need outside financing, which lowers cost and risk.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Each of the three components moves the cycle. Faster selling shortens days inventory outstanding and pulls the cycle down. Tighter collection shortens days sales outstanding and does the same. Negotiating longer payment terms with suppliers raises days payables outstanding, which also shortens the cycle. A company can attack the cycle from all three sides at once.

Market power and policy choices drive much of the result. A dominant buyer can demand long payment terms from suppliers and push its cycle negative. A company chasing sales growth may loosen its own credit terms, which lengthens collection and stretches the cycle. Seasonality distorts single readings. A company that stocks up before a peak season shows a long cycle in the buildup months that does not reflect the full-year pace. Stretching payables too far can damage supplier relationships even as the reported cycle improves, so a falling cycle is not always healthy.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a good cycle depends on the business model.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Large discount and grocery retail | Often near zero or negative | Goods sell for cash fast and suppliers grant long payment terms. |
| Apparel and general retail | Short to moderate | Inventory turns within a season and most sales settle quickly. |
| Manufacturing and distribution | Moderate to long | Goods move through production and sit in stock, and customers buy on credit. |
| Makers of aged goods such as spirits | Very long | Inventory must age for a long time before it can sell. |
| Subscription and software businesses | Often negative | Customers prepay and there is little or no physical inventory. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | These firms have no inventory cycle, so the metric does not describe them. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two businesses carry different inventory and credit models. Compare a company only against direct peers, and judge the trend in its own cycle over time.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady cycle means the company's working-capital timing is under control. A steady decline is usually a good sign, showing faster selling, quicker collection, or longer supplier terms. A decline driven only by stretching payables deserves a second look, because pushing suppliers too far can backfire even while the number improves.

A steady rise is the pattern that deserves the most attention. A lengthening cycle ties up more cash and can signal slowing sales, weaker collection, or lost bargaining power with suppliers. Read the three components separately to find the cause. Seasonality can fake a trend. A company measured during its pre-season inventory buildup shows a long cycle that reverses once the selling season arrives, so compare the same quarter across years rather than reading a single period.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Inventory Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days Inventory Outstanding is one of the three building blocks of the cash conversion cycle. The cycle cannot move without a change in inventory days, collection days, or payables days. Reading the inventory component on its own shows how much of the total cycle comes from goods sitting in stock. This separates an inventory problem from a collection or payment problem.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising cycle driven mostly by rising inventory days points to slowing sales or overbuying rather than a collection issue. A stable inventory figure inside a rising cycle clears inventory of blame and sends attention to receivables or payables. Reading the component against the whole cycle tells a beginner exactly which lever moved.

### Days Sales Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days Sales Outstanding is the collection component of the cycle. The cycle lengthens whenever customers take longer to pay, even if inventory and supplier terms hold steady. Reading collection days alongside the full cycle isolates how much of the cash delay comes from customers rather than from stock or suppliers.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising cycle paired with rising collection days points to looser credit terms or customers in distress. A steady collection figure inside a moving cycle clears receivables of blame. Reading the two together helps a beginner decide whether the company is funding its customers or its inventory.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free Cash Flow is the cash left after the company funds its operations and its equipment. A lengthening cash conversion cycle drains cash into working capital and pulls free cash flow down. A shortening cycle releases cash and lifts it. The cycle explains part of why free cash flow swings from year to year.

**What the combination tells you.** Weak free cash flow alongside a lengthening cycle shows that cash is getting trapped in inventory and receivables rather than lost to poor profits. Strong free cash flow with a shrinking cycle confirms that working-capital discipline is feeding the cash pile. Reading the pair links the timing measure to the actual cash the company keeps.

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The Current Ratio compares current assets against current liabilities to gauge short-term solvency. The cash conversion cycle measures how fast those current assets turn back into cash. A company can show a comfortable current ratio while its cycle reveals that the assets behind it are slow to convert. The two together test both the size and the speed of the company's liquidity.

**What the combination tells you.** A healthy current ratio with a long cycle means the assets are there but slow to free up, so the liquidity is less reliable than it looks. A modest current ratio with a short or negative cycle can still be safe because cash returns quickly. Reading both keeps a beginner from trusting a static balance-sheet ratio without checking how fast the assets actually convert.

## Part 8: Limitations

The cycle uses balance-sheet figures from a single date, so a seasonal high or low can distort all three components at once. A company measured during a stock buildup looks far worse than its full-year average. The cycle also relies on the inventory-valuation method, which differs between companies and makes cross-company comparison less clean than the single number suggests.

A shorter cycle is not always better. A company can shrink its cycle by starving inventory and risking stockouts, by squeezing customers into terms that cost sales, or by stretching suppliers until relationships fray. The cycle says nothing about the quality of those trade-offs. The metric also has no meaning for banks, insurers, and other firms with no inventory cycle, where the figure should be ignored entirely.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Why is the current cycle at its level: how much comes from inventory days, collection days, and payables days?
2. Which of the three components has moved the most over the last several years, and what does that component reveal about the business?
3. Is a falling cycle driven by genuine efficiency, or mainly by stretching payments to suppliers that could damage those relationships?
4. Is the cycle measured at a seasonal high or low? What does the same quarter look like across several years?
5. Has a rising cycle locked up cash that the company then had to fund with debt or by drawing down its cash balance?
6. Does the company hold enough bargaining power to demand long supplier terms, or is it the weaker party that must pay quickly?
7. Has the company loosened its own credit terms to chase sales, and is that showing up as longer collection days inside the cycle?
8. Does the cycle line up with the swings in free cash flow, or do other forces explain the cash movement?
9. How does the cycle compare with direct competitors using a similar business model? Which peer recovers its cash fastest?
10. Could a short cycle be hiding thin inventory that risks stockouts, or customer terms so tight they are costing the company sales?
