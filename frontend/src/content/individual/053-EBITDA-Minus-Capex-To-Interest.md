# (EBITDA − Capex) / Interest

## Part 1: Definition

The (EBITDA − Capex) / Interest ratio measures how many times a company could pay one year of interest after first paying for the equipment and facilities the business needs to keep running. Interest is the fee a company pays on borrowed money such as bank loans and bonds. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA is a rough measure of operating cash earnings. Capex means capital expenditures. Capex is the money a company spends on equipment, buildings, and similar long-lived assets. The ratio answers one question: after the company spends what it must on equipment, does enough remain to carry the cost of the debt?

This ratio is the strictest member of the interest coverage family. The plain interest coverage ratio is covered in its own definition under Interest Coverage and is not redefined here. The (EBITDA − Capex) / Interest ratio charges the company for routine equipment spending before measuring the cushion, so the ratio reads lower than plain coverage. The app shows this strict variant beside the headline coverage tile.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | Cash earnings after required equipment spending cannot pay the year's interest. The company must draw on cash, sell assets, or borrow more. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | Interest is covered but with thin room. A weak year could push the ratio below 1.0. |
| 2.0 to 4.0 | A workable cushion for a company that spends heavily on equipment. |
| 4.0 and above | Comfortable. The business funds its equipment and still covers interest several times over. |
| Very high | Debt is small relative to cash earnings, and the ratio starts to lose meaning. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`(EBITDA − Capex) / Interest = (EBITDA − Capital Expenditures) ÷ Interest Expense`

The top number is EBITDA minus capex. EBITDA adds depreciation and amortization back to operating income. Depreciation and amortization are non-cash charges that spread the recorded cost of assets over several years. Subtracting capex then removes the real cash the company spends each year to buy and replace those assets. The result is a cash figure that already reflects the cost of staying in business.

The bottom number is interest expense. Interest expense is the year's total cost of carrying all borrowed money, including bonds, bank loans, and lease interest. Principal is not included. Principal is the borrowed amount itself rather than the fee for borrowing. Principal is left out because an old loan can usually be replaced with a new loan when repayment comes due, while interest must be paid from earnings every year.

| Coverage variant | Formula | What the variant changes |
|---|---|---|
| Plain interest coverage | Operating Income ÷ Interest Expense | Credits the company with full operating profit before any equipment spending. Reads the highest. |
| EBITDA coverage | EBITDA ÷ Interest Expense | Adds back depreciation and amortization but still ignores equipment spending. |
| (EBITDA − Capex) / Interest | (EBITDA − Capex) ÷ Interest Expense | Charges the company for required equipment spending first. Reads the lowest and is the strictest test. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Most businesses must keep spending on equipment and facilities just to maintain current earnings. Plain interest coverage ignores that spending and can flatter a capital-heavy company. The (EBITDA − Capex) / Interest ratio subtracts the equipment cost first, so the ratio shows the cushion that actually remains for lenders.

The ratio answers a question that plain coverage does not. Plain coverage asks whether profit covers interest. This stricter ratio asks whether cash covers interest after the company has paid to keep its assets working. A company can look safe on plain coverage while heavy equipment spending quietly consumes the real cushion.

Lenders watch the strict ratio closely for capital-intensive borrowers. A wide gap between plain coverage and this ratio is a warning that the apparent safety depends on cutting equipment spending, which a company cannot do for long without the business shrinking.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three forces move the ratio. The first is EBITDA. EBITDA rises and falls with sales, prices, and operating costs. The second is capex, which a company can raise to grow or hold low to harvest cash. A company that delays maintenance can flatter the ratio for a year or two before the underspending shows up as broken or outdated assets. The third is interest expense, which climbs when the company borrows more or when floating-rate debt reprices to higher market rates. Floating rate means the interest rate moves with market rates rather than staying fixed.

Accounting and timing choices can distort single readings. Capex is lumpy because a large factory or fleet purchase can fall entirely in one year. A single heavy capex year can sink the ratio even when the underlying business is healthy. One-time gains or losses inside EBITDA move the top number for reasons unrelated to ongoing operations. Reading several years together smooths these swings.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The acceptable level depends on how much equipment the business needs and how steady its earnings are.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and other capital-light companies | Ratio close to plain coverage | Capex is small, so subtracting capex barely changes the reading. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Often thin or negative in build years | Heavy capex on networks and plants can exceed EBITDA during expansion. Regulators allow this because returns are near certain. |
| Telecom and cable | Persistently lower than plain coverage | Constant network spending consumes a large share of cash earnings every year. |
| Industrial and manufacturing | Moderately below plain coverage | Factories and machinery require steady reinvestment, so the gap is real but manageable. |
| Cyclical producers such as miners and auto makers | Judge at the worst year | Both EBITDA and capex swing widely. The ratio in a strong year proves little. |
| Banks | Not applicable | Interest is the core cost of banking rather than a financing cost, and banks carry little physical capex. |

Compare a company only against others in the same sector, and weight the worst year on the chart more heavily than the latest year.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady high line means the business funds its own equipment and still covers interest with room to spare. A steady low line means the company operates close to the limit that required spending and debt allow. The pattern that deserves the most attention is steady decline. A ratio that falls year after year means debt or equipment costs are growing faster than cash earnings.

A single-year dip is often a lumpy capex year rather than a real deterioration, because one large equipment purchase can fall in a single period. The dip still matters because the dip shows how thin the cushion becomes when spending and interest land together. Compare this strict line with the plain coverage line. A plain coverage ratio that holds steady while this ratio sinks means equipment spending is consuming the cushion that plain coverage still reports as healthy.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest Coverage credits the company with full operating profit before any equipment spending. The (EBITDA − Capex) / Interest ratio charges the company for that spending first. Reading the two side by side shows how much of the apparent cushion survives the cost of keeping the business running. The gap between them is the clearest single signal of how capital-heavy the company is.

**What the combination tells you.** A narrow gap means the business is capital light, so plain coverage can be trusted on its own. A wide gap means heavy equipment spending consumes most of the operating cushion, so the strict ratio is the honest one. Watching both lines over years shows whether rising equipment costs are eroding a cushion that plain coverage still reports as comfortable.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Capex / Depreciation compares the cash a company spends on assets against the depreciation charge that estimates how fast assets wear out. A ratio near 1.0 suggests the company is roughly replacing what it uses. This context explains why the capex line in the strict coverage ratio is high or low in a given year.

**What the combination tells you.** A low Capex / Depreciation ratio paired with strong strict coverage may mean the company is underspending on its assets and flattering the cushion. A high Capex / Depreciation ratio that drags strict coverage down may reflect genuine growth investment rather than weakness. Reading the pair separates a thin cushion caused by real reinvestment from one caused by rising debt.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free Cash Flow is operating cash flow minus capex, the cash left after the business funds its operations and its equipment. The strict coverage ratio also subtracts capex, so the two share the idea of cash after required spending. Free Cash Flow measures the dollars available, while the strict coverage ratio measures how many times those dollars cover interest.

**What the combination tells you.** Positive free cash flow with strong strict coverage means the company funds its assets, pays its interest, and still has cash left for dividends or debt reduction. Negative free cash flow with weak strict coverage means the company cannot cover its own spending and its interest from operations. Reading the pair links the size of the cash surplus to the safety of the interest payment.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** The strict coverage ratio measures whether cash after equipment spending can afford the yearly interest. Net Debt / EBITDA measures the total size of the debt in years of earnings. Affordability and size are different questions, and a full view of debt risk needs both answers.

**What the combination tells you.** Low total debt with strong strict coverage signals conservative finances. High total debt with strong strict coverage signals a large load carried at cheap rates, which is exposed to refinancing risk when rates rise. Low total debt with weak strict coverage points to heavy capex or weak earnings rather than too much borrowing. Reading the pair separates the cost of the debt from the amount of the debt.

## Part 8: Limitations

EBITDA is not cash. EBITDA ignores changes in working capital and the cash drain of taxes, so a company can show healthy EBITDA while collecting cash slowly. The ratio also treats one year of capex as if it were the normal annual amount. Capex is lumpy, so a quiet year flatters the ratio and a heavy year punishes it, neither of which reflects the steady level of spending the business truly needs.

The ratio shares the blind spots of all coverage measures. The ratio ignores principal repayments entirely, which is why the debt service view stays a required companion. The ratio reflects the rates on existing loans rather than the rates the company will face at the next refinancing. For a company with almost no debt the ratio is enormous and uninformative. For a company with negative cash earnings after capex the ratio cannot be read sensibly, and that failure happens exactly when the answer matters most.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Why is the current ratio at its level: strong cash earnings, low equipment spending, cheap old loans, or some mix? What does the ratio become when each returns to normal?
2. How large is the gap between plain Interest Coverage and this strict ratio? Does the gap reflect a genuinely capital-heavy business?
3. Was the latest capex figure a normal year or a lumpy one? What is the ratio on a multi-year average of capex instead?
4. Could the company be underspending on equipment to flatter the ratio? Does the Capex / Depreciation ratio support that suspicion?
5. What was the ratio in the company's worst earnings year on the chart? Could that year's interest have been paid from cash after equipment spending?
6. How much of the debt has a floating rate or comes due within three years? What would the ratio be if all the debt repriced to current market rates?
7. Does the strict ratio move with free cash flow, or do working-capital swings make the two tell different stories?
8. If the ratio is very high, why does the company borrow so little? Is the caution a strategy or a sign of shrinking investment opportunities?
9. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle? Which competitor would be forced to cut equipment spending first in a downturn?
10. Have recent buybacks, dividends, or acquisitions been funded with new debt that raised interest while equipment spending stayed high? Is management thinning the cushion from both sides at once?
