# EV To EBIT

## Part 1: Definition

EV to EBIT measures how expensive the whole business is compared with the operating profit the business earns in a year. EV stands for enterprise value, which is the price of buying the entire company. Enterprise value adds the market value of the shares to the net debt, which is total debt minus cash on hand. EBIT stands for earnings before interest and taxes, which is the profit the business earns from normal operations before any interest or tax is paid. The multiple answers one question: how many years of operating profit does a buyer pay to own the whole business?

A multiple of 12 means a buyer pays twelve years of current operating profit to own the company outright. A multiple of 6 means the buyer pays six years of operating profit. A lower multiple means the buyer pays less for each dollar of operating profit. The multiple looks at the price an acquirer of the whole company would face, so the multiple counts the debt as part of the price. The plain price-to-earnings ratio ignores the debt, and that is the gap this multiple closes.

Common readings of the multiple:

| Multiple level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 8 | The business is priced cheaply against its operating profit, or the market expects profit to fall. |
| 8 to 14 | A normal range for a steady, established company. |
| 14 to 20 | The market expects profit to grow, or the business is high quality. |
| Above 20 | The market prices in strong future growth. A small profit miss can cut the price sharply. |
| Negative | EBIT is negative, so the multiple cannot be read. The business is losing money at the operating level. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`EV To EBIT = Enterprise Value ÷ EBIT`

Enterprise value is the top number. Enterprise value is the market value of all the shares plus net debt. Net debt is total borrowings minus cash and short-term investments. The cash is subtracted because a buyer who takes over the company also takes over the cash, and that cash can be used to pay down part of the debt. Enterprise value is the true cost of owning the operating business free of its financing.

EBIT is the bottom number. EBIT is operating profit before interest and taxes are deducted. Interest is left out because interest depends on how much the company has borrowed rather than on how well the business runs. Tax is left out because tax rates differ across countries and across companies. Stripping out both lets the multiple compare the raw operating performance of two companies that carry different debt loads and face different tax rates.

| Related multiple | What it uses | How it differs |
|---|---|---|
| EV to EBITDA | Enterprise value over EBITDA | EBITDA adds depreciation and amortization back to EBIT. Depreciation spreads the cost of equipment over its life. The EBITDA version ignores that cost, so the multiple reads lower. |
| Earnings yield (Greenblatt) | EBIT over enterprise value | The same ratio turned upside down. A higher yield means a cheaper business. The vault's Magic Formula ranks companies on this measure. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The plain price-to-earnings ratio is distorted by debt and tax. Two companies can run identical operations and earn identical operating profit, yet show very different price-to-earnings ratios because one carries debt and the other does not. EV to EBIT removes that distortion. The multiple puts companies with different debt loads and different tax rates on an equal footing.

The multiple thinks like a buyer of the whole company rather than a buyer of a few shares. A buyer of the entire business must pay off the shareholders and take on the debt. Enterprise value captures both costs. The acquirer view is why merger advisers, private equity firms, and screens that compare many companies at once rely on enterprise multiples.

The multiple also works when a company has heavy debt that would make the price-to-earnings ratio misleading. EBIT sits above interest on the income statement, so the multiple measures the operating business before the debt is served. The multiple shows what the operations are worth on their own.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The share price moves the top number every day. A rising share price raises enterprise value and lifts the multiple even when profit has not changed. The amount of debt also moves the top number. Taking on debt to fund a buyout or a buyback raises enterprise value. Holding a large cash pile lowers net debt and shrinks enterprise value.

Operating profit moves the bottom number. EBIT rises and falls with sales, prices, and costs. A weak year shrinks EBIT and inflates the multiple even when the share price holds steady. One-off items inside EBIT distort the reading. A one-time gain lifts EBIT and lowers the multiple for a reason unrelated to the ongoing business. A restructuring charge does the reverse. Depreciation policy matters too, because aggressive depreciation lowers EBIT and raises the multiple compared with a company that depreciates slowly.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

A normal multiple in one sector can be high in another. Faster-growing and higher-quality sectors carry higher multiples because buyers pay ahead for future profit.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and other capital-light businesses | High, often above 18 | Profit grows fast and needs little equipment. Buyers pay ahead for the growth. |
| Established industrials and consumer goods | Middle, roughly 10 to 15 | Profit is steady but grows slowly, so the multiple sits in a normal band. |
| Commodity producers such as miners and oil firms | Low and swinging | EBIT rises and falls with commodity prices. A low multiple at the peak can be a trap. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Interest is the core business, so EBIT does not describe their operations. Use price-to-book instead. |
| Heavily indebted companies | Lower price-to-earnings, fairer EV multiple | The multiple captures the debt that the price-to-earnings ratio hides. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two sectors grow at different speeds or use equipment in very different amounts. Compare a company against direct peers in the same sector and at the same point in the business cycle.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows the multiple over several years next to the live number. A multiple that drifts steadily higher means the share price is climbing faster than operating profit. The business is getting more expensive relative to what it earns. A multiple that drifts steadily lower means profit is growing faster than the price, or the market is losing faith in future profit. The reason behind the direction matters more than the direction itself.

A sudden step down often means a profit collapse rather than a price collapse, because a shrinking EBIT can inflate or distort the multiple in a single year. Check whether EBIT fell or the share price fell. Watch for base effects in cyclical companies. A commodity producer can show a very low multiple at the top of the cycle, when EBIT is briefly huge, and that low multiple is a warning rather than a bargain. Read the multiple across a full cycle, not at a single peak or trough.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### EV/EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** EV to EBIT charges the business for depreciation, the yearly cost of wearing out equipment. EV to EBITDA adds that cost back and ignores it. The gap between the two multiples shows how equipment-heavy the business is. A capital-light software firm shows almost no gap, while a factory-heavy producer shows a wide gap.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide gap between the two multiples means depreciation is large, so the business must keep spending to replace equipment. The stricter EV to EBIT multiple is the more honest one for such a company. A narrow gap means the business owns little equipment, and either multiple can be trusted. Watching both over time shows whether rising depreciation is quietly making the business look cheaper than it really is.

### P/E

**Why read the two together.** The price-to-earnings ratio prices the shares alone and ignores the debt. EV to EBIT prices the whole business including the debt. Reading the pair separates the value of the shares from the value of the operating business. The price-to-earnings ratio also uses net profit after interest and tax, while EV to EBIT uses operating profit before both.

**What the combination tells you.** A low price-to-earnings ratio next to a high EV to EBIT multiple usually means heavy debt is flattering the share-level number. The shares look cheap only because the debt is hidden. When both measures agree the business is cheap, the value is more trustworthy. When they disagree, the debt is the reason, and the EV multiple is the safer guide.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is operating profit divided by sales, so the margin shows how much of each sales dollar becomes operating profit. EBIT is the bottom number of the EV to EBIT multiple. A company with a thin EBIT margin produces a small bottom number, which inflates the multiple. The margin explains why the profit is the size it is.

**What the combination tells you.** A high multiple paired with a rising EBIT margin can be justified, because the growing margin lifts future profit. A high multiple paired with a falling margin is a warning, because the profit base is shrinking under the price. Reading the two together shows whether the price is supported by improving operations or floating above weakening ones.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net Debt to EBITDA measures how heavy the debt load is in years of earnings. That same debt sits inside the enterprise value at the top of the EV to EBIT multiple. A company with a heavy debt load carries a larger enterprise value for the same share price. Reading the pair shows how much of the price tag is debt rather than equity.

**What the combination tells you.** A high EV to EBIT multiple driven by a high Net Debt to EBITDA means the buyer is paying mostly to take on debt. Such a company is exposed to rising interest rates and refinancing risk. A high multiple with low debt means the price reflects the equity and the growth, not the borrowings. The pair shows whether the enterprise value is built on profit expectations or on leverage.

## Part 8: Limitations

EBIT is an accounting profit, not cash, so the multiple can flatter a company that books profit faster than it collects cash. EBIT also depends on depreciation policy, and two companies with the same assets can report different EBIT by depreciating at different speeds. One-off gains and charges inside EBIT distort a single year's reading. The multiple says nothing about how fast profit will grow, so a low multiple on a shrinking business is not a bargain.

The multiple breaks down for banks and insurers, where interest is the core business rather than a financing cost. The multiple cannot be computed when EBIT is negative, and that failure happens exactly when a struggling company most needs judging. Enterprise value also leaves out some real obligations such as pension shortfalls and lease commitments unless they are added back by hand. The multiple is a strong first valuation question and a weak last one.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current multiple high because the share price has risen or because operating profit has fallen? What does the multiple become once profit returns to a normal year?
2. How does the multiple compare with direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle? Is the company cheaper or dearer than its peers, and why?
3. How much of the enterprise value is debt rather than equity? Would the multiple still look reasonable if interest rates rose and the debt had to be refinanced?
4. Does EBIT contain any one-time gains or charges this year? What is the multiple using a clean, recurring EBIT?
5. How does the company depreciate its assets compared with peers? Is a slow depreciation policy lifting EBIT and lowering the multiple?
6. For a cyclical company, what was the multiple at the worst year on the chart? Is today's low multiple a genuine bargain or a peak-earnings trap?
7. How wide is the gap between EV to EBIT and EV to EBITDA? Does heavy equipment spending make the stricter multiple the one that matters?
8. Is the company growing operating profit fast enough to justify the multiple, or is the price floating above a flat profit base?
9. Does the enterprise value capture pension shortfalls and lease commitments? Or are real obligations missing from the top number?
10. If the multiple is unusually low, is the market right that profit will fall, or is the business genuinely mispriced?
