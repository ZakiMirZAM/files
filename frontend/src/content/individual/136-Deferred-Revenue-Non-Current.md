# Deferred Revenue Non Current

## Part 1: Definition

Deferred revenue non current is cash a company has already collected from customers for goods or services it will deliver more than one year from now. Deferred revenue is money received before the work is done. The word non current means the obligation is settled beyond the next twelve months. The line answers one question: how much paid-for work does the company still owe its customers over the long term?

Deferred revenue is a liability, not income. A liability is something the company owes. The company owes delivery of the product or service, not a cash refund in normal cases. The company keeps the cash and works off the obligation over time by providing what was promised. Deferred revenue is also called unearned revenue, because the company has the cash but has not yet earned it under accounting rules.

The table below reads common situations for this line.

| What you see | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| A large and growing balance | Customers are prepaying for long contracts. Demand is being booked in advance. |
| A balance that shrinks every year | The company is delivering old prepaid contracts faster than it signs new ones. |
| Almost no balance | The business collects payment as it delivers, or contracts are short. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Deferred revenue non current is not a calculated ratio. The line is a balance reported on the balance sheet. The balance sheet is the statement that lists what a company owns and owes on a single date. The company splits its total deferred revenue into two parts by timing.

`Deferred Revenue Non Current = Total Deferred Revenue − Deferred Revenue Current`

Total deferred revenue is all cash collected in advance for work not yet done. Deferred revenue current is the portion the company expects to deliver within twelve months. The non current portion is what remains. The non current portion is the work owed in year two and beyond. The split matters because a near-term obligation and a multi-year obligation carry different timing.

The balance rises when the company collects new advance payments. The balance falls when the company delivers the promised work and moves the earned amount into revenue on the income statement. The income statement is the report of sales and profit over a period.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Deferred revenue non current shows demand the company has already secured but not yet reported as sales. Customers have paid in advance and committed to a long relationship. A rising balance often points to future revenue that is close to guaranteed, because the cash is already in hand.

The line also shows the quality of the funding. The company is using customer cash to run the business. Customer prepayments carry no interest and no fixed repayment date in cash. The company settles the obligation by doing the work it already planned to do.

A subscription or long-term service business will carry a meaningful deferred revenue balance. The presence of the balance signals that customers pay first and receive the service later. That payment pattern favors the seller.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Contract length is the first driver. Longer prepaid contracts push more of the balance into the non current bucket. A shift from annual billing to multi-year billing raises this line even when the customer count is flat.

Billing terms are the second driver. A company that bills upfront for the full term builds a large balance. A company that bills monthly builds almost none. The balance reflects when cash is collected, not when the customer signs.

Accounting rules are the third driver. The revenue recognition standard sets exactly when a company may move deferred revenue into earned revenue. A change in how the company defines delivery can shift the balance without any change in the underlying contracts. New contract terms or bundled products can also move the split between current and non current.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The size of this line depends on whether the business collects cash before or after it delivers. The table shows the pattern by sector.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and subscription services | Large and often growing | Customers prepay for multi-year licenses and support. |
| Insurance | Large in a related form | Premiums are collected before coverage is provided. |
| Media and publishing | Moderate | Multi-year subscriptions are prepaid. |
| Retail and manufacturing | Small or none | Customers pay at or after delivery. |
| Construction and long projects | Varies widely | Milestone prepayments can be large and lumpy. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is meaningless. A retailer with no deferred revenue is not weaker than a software firm with a large balance. The line only carries meaning against companies with the same billing model.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the deferred revenue non current line as a multi-year series and as a percent of total assets. Total assets is everything the company owns. The vertical analysis on this page shows each balance-sheet line as a share of total assets, so a rising raw number can still be a flat share if the company is growing.

A steady rise in the balance that also lifts the percent of total assets means the company is signing longer prepaid contracts or winning more advance commitments. A steady decline means the company is working off old prepaid contracts faster than it adds new ones, which can warn of slowing bookings. A sudden step up usually follows a large multi-year deal or a shift to upfront billing. High volatility points to lumpy project prepayments rather than steady subscriptions. One base effect can fake a trend. A jump caused by a billing-policy change, not by new demand, looks like growth but is only a timing shift.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Revenue is the sales the company reports as earned in a period. Deferred revenue non current is paid-for work the company has not yet earned. The two lines describe the same customer relationships at different stages. Reading both shows how much future revenue is already funded by cash in hand.

**What the combination tells you.** Deferred revenue non current that grows faster than revenue means the company is booking advance commitments faster than it delivers, which points to strong future sales. Deferred revenue that shrinks while revenue holds steady means the company is consuming its backlog of prepaid work without replacing it. A balance that tracks revenue closely points to a stable subscription base. The pair separates today's reported sales from tomorrow's funded sales.

### Operating Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Operating cash flow is the cash the business generates from normal operations. Customer prepayments land in operating cash flow when collected, then sit on the balance sheet as deferred revenue until earned. Reading both shows how much of the cash inflow came from advance payments rather than from delivered sales.

**What the combination tells you.** Strong operating cash flow paired with a rising deferred revenue balance means customers are funding the business in advance, which is a favorable position. Operating cash flow that holds up only because deferred revenue is rising warns that the cash is borrowed against future work. A falling deferred revenue balance can drain operating cash flow as old prepayments run out. The pair shows whether cash strength is durable or front-loaded.

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio compares current assets to current liabilities to test short-term liquidity. Deferred revenue is a liability, but the non current portion sits outside the twelve-month window. Reading both clarifies which prepaid obligations actually press on near-term resources.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a large non current deferred revenue balance owes work rather than cash over the long term. The obligation is therefore gentler than debt. The current ratio shows whether the near-term portion strains liquidity. A weak current ratio next to a large deferred revenue balance is less alarming than the same ratio next to large short-term debt. The pair separates obligations settled in cash from obligations settled by delivering a service.

## Part 8: Limitations

Deferred revenue non current shows committed work, but the commitment is not always firm. Some contracts allow cancellation and a partial refund, which means the cash is not fully secured. The line also says nothing about the profit in the contract. A large prepaid balance on unprofitable contracts is a future cost, not a future gain.

The line is also shaped by billing choices that have nothing to do with demand. A company that switches to upfront billing inflates this line without selling anything new. Comparisons across companies break down when billing models differ. The split between the current and non current portions rests on management estimates of delivery timing, which can be optimistic. The line is a useful read on funded future revenue and a weak read on contract quality.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current balance produced by new advance bookings, by a shift to upfront or multi-year billing, or by both? What does the balance become if billing returns to the prior pattern?
2. How fast is deferred revenue non current growing compared with reported revenue over the last several years?
3. What share of these prepaid contracts can the customer cancel, and how much cash would be refunded on cancellation?
4. Are the prepaid contracts profitable, or does the balance represent future work the company will deliver at a loss?
5. Did any accounting change in how delivery is recognized move the balance without a change in customer demand?
6. How does deferred revenue non current as a percent of total assets compare with direct competitors using the same billing model?
7. Is operating cash flow being supported by rising deferred revenue, and what happens to cash flow if advance bookings slow?
8. What is the typical contract length, and is it lengthening or shortening over time?
9. Does the split between current and non current deferred revenue rest on realistic delivery timing, or on optimistic management estimates?
10. If deferred revenue non current is shrinking, is the company failing to replace expiring prepaid contracts with new ones?
