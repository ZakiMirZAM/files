# FCF To Total Debt

## Part 1: Definition

FCF to total debt measures how much of the company's whole debt load it could repay in one year using the cash left over after running and maintaining the business. FCF stands for free cash flow, which is the cash from operations after the company pays for the equipment and buildings it needs. Total debt is every borrowing the company owes. The total includes bank loans, bonds, and the debt portion of leases. The ratio answers one question: how quickly could the company pay off its debt from its own cash?

A ratio of 50 percent means one year of free cash flow could retire half the debt, so full repayment would take about two years. A ratio of 10 percent means one year of free cash flow covers only a tenth of the debt, so full repayment from internal cash would take about a decade. The vault describes the ratio as showing hypothetically how long it would take the company to pay back its debt with cash generated from the business. The vault calls it one of the most telling measures of financial strength.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 10 percent | Repaying the debt from internal cash would take more than ten years. The company leans on refinancing. |
| 10 to 20 percent | Modest. The debt is serviceable but slow to clear. |
| 20 to 40 percent | Healthy for most companies. The debt can be cleared in a few years if needed. |
| Above 40 percent | Strong. Free cash flow could retire the debt quickly. |
| Negative | Free cash flow is negative. The business is consuming cash, not generating it to repay debt. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`FCF To Total Debt = Free Cash Flow ÷ Total Debt`

Free cash flow is the top number. Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Operating cash flow is the cash the business collects from its operations. Capital expenditures, often shortened to capex, are the amounts spent on equipment and buildings needed to keep the business running. Free cash flow is the strictest measure of cash truly available, because the company is charged for the spending it cannot avoid. The strictness is why the vault treats free cash flow as the most conservative cash figure in credit analysis.

Total debt is the bottom number. Total debt is the sum of all interest-bearing borrowings. The sum includes long-term debt, its current portion, and lease obligations. The vault builds the figure from those balance-sheet lines. Some analysts use net debt instead, which subtracts cash on hand. This app's ratio uses gross total debt, so it does not credit the company for its cash balance. The gross figure gives the more cautious reading.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Debt size alone does not show whether a company can handle its borrowings. A large debt is safe for a strong cash generator and dangerous for a weak one. The ratio links the debt to the cash that could repay it. The ratio states financial strength as a single number.

The ratio is more honest than profit-based measures because it uses cash after equipment spending. A company can report profit yet have no cash left once it pays to maintain its assets. The vault notes that capital expenditures, though counted as investment, are often an ongoing operating cost. Charging for that spending before measuring repayment power gives a truer picture.

The ratio also shows how fast a company could reduce its debt if it chose to. A high ratio means the company could deleverage quickly and handle upcoming maturities, which are the dates when debts come due. The company may not choose to repay, but the ratio shows that the option exists.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Free cash flow moves the top number, and several forces move free cash flow. Operating cash flow rises and falls with sales, prices, and costs. Capital expenditures pull the other way, so a year of heavy equipment spending shrinks free cash flow and lowers the ratio. Working capital swings matter too, since a build-up of inventory or unpaid customer bills drains cash in a single year. A company that subtracts dividends from free cash flow will show a lower ratio than one that does not.

Total debt moves the bottom number. Borrowing to fund an acquisition, a buyback, or a dividend raises total debt and lowers the ratio. Repaying debt does the reverse. One-off items distort a single reading. A large one-time capital project depresses free cash flow for one year, and the vault advises separating regular maintenance spending from special projects. An acquisition financed with cash lowers free cash flow that year for a reason unrelated to the ongoing business. A persistently low ratio is the real warning, not a single weak year.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a safe ratio depends on how much equipment a sector needs and how steady its cash is.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Capital-light software and services | High, often above 40 percent | Little equipment spending, so most operating cash becomes free cash flow. |
| Capital-heavy industrials and telecoms | Lower, often 10 to 20 percent | Heavy ongoing equipment spending eats operating cash, so the ratio sits lower even when healthy. |
| Utilities | Low by design | Large, steady capital programs hold free cash flow down, but stable revenue makes a low ratio tolerable. |
| Cyclical producers such as miners | Swings widely | Free cash flow rises and falls with commodity prices, so judge the ratio at the worst year. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Free cash flow is not defined in the usual way for lenders. The metric does not describe them. |

The vault makes the rule plain. A business that needs heavy capital spending should carry a lower debt ratio than a business that needs little. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two sectors spend very differently on equipment. Compare a company against direct peers and weight the worst year on the chart.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows the ratio over several years next to the live number. A steadily rising ratio means free cash flow is growing faster than debt, so the company is getting stronger and could clear its debt sooner. A steadily falling ratio is the pattern that deserves the most attention. A falling ratio means debt is growing faster than cash, or cash generation is weakening. A multi-year slide rarely reverses without a deliberate move to cut spending or pay down debt.

A single weak year is often a capital-spending spike rather than a debt problem, because one large project can sink free cash flow for one year. The weak year still matters because it shows the worst-case cash level the debt must survive. The vault suggests looking at what the company has done with its cash over several years, which reveals management's approach. Watch for base effects in cyclical companies, where a peak-earnings year can flatter the ratio. Read the ratio across a full cycle, not at a single high point.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** FCF to total debt measures repayment power using the strictest cash figure. Net Debt to EBITDA measures the size of the debt against earnings before equipment spending. One ratio uses cash after capex, the other uses a profit measure before it. Reading the pair shows the debt load through both a strict cash lens and a broader earnings lens.

**What the combination tells you.** A healthy FCF to total debt ratio next to a high Net Debt to EBITDA means the earnings measure is too generous, because real cash after equipment spending is thinner than profit suggests. When both measures look strong, the debt is safe on cash and on earnings. When they disagree, heavy capital spending is usually the reason, and the free cash flow ratio is the more honest guide. The pair separates earnings-based leverage from cash-based leverage.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** FCF to total debt measures whether the company could repay the whole debt over time. Interest coverage measures whether the company can afford the yearly interest fee on that debt. Repaying the debt and affording the interest are different questions. A company can comfortably pay interest while being slow to repay the principal.

**What the combination tells you.** High interest coverage with a low FCF to total debt ratio means the company can carry the debt but would struggle to retire it, so the company depends on refinancing. Refinancing can become costly or unavailable in a credit squeeze. When both are high, the debt is safe on the fee and on repayment. When both are low, the company has a genuine solvency problem. The pair shows whether the risk is the interest cost or the repayment of the debt itself.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the top number of the ratio, so the ratio cannot be read without understanding the cash behind it. Free cash flow shows the absolute cash available, while the ratio scales that cash against the debt. Reading the pair shows whether a change in the ratio came from the cash side or the debt side.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising ratio driven by rising free cash flow is healthy, because the company is generating more cash to retire its debt. A rising ratio driven only by debt repayment, while free cash flow is flat, is weaker. A falling free cash flow with steady debt warns that repayment power is eroding. Reading the cash figure alongside the ratio shows which side is moving the number.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation compares what the company spends on equipment with the accounting cost of wearing that equipment out. Capital spending is the charge that turns operating cash flow into free cash flow, the top number of the debt ratio. A company spending far more than its depreciation is investing heavily, which lowers free cash flow now. Reading the pair shows why free cash flow is high or low.

**What the combination tells you.** A low FCF to total debt ratio paired with a high capex to depreciation reading means heavy investment is depressing free cash flow today, possibly for future growth. A low ratio with capex near depreciation means the weak free cash flow is not an investment story, so the cash generation itself is poor. The pair shows whether thin free cash flow reflects growth spending or a weak business.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio uses one year of free cash flow, which can be lumpy. A single heavy capital project or a working-capital swing can sink the ratio for a year without signaling real weakness. The vault warns that free cash flow figures depend on judgment about which items to include. Dividends, acquisitions, and one-time charges are all treated differently by different analysts. Two analysts can compute different free cash flow figures for the same company, so the ratio is only as reliable as the cash figure behind it.

The ratio assumes all free cash flow could go to debt repayment. Companies also owe dividends, face working-capital needs, and pursue growth. The ratio does not show when the debt comes due, so a company with a strong ratio can still face a maturity wall, meaning a large block of debt due in a single year. The ratio cannot be read when free cash flow is negative, and that happens during the heavy-investment or downturn years when debt risk is highest. The ratio is a strong quick gauge of financial strength and a poor substitute for reading the full maturity schedule.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio low because free cash flow fell this year or because debt rose? What does the ratio become in a normal capital-spending year?
2. Was free cash flow depressed by a one-time capital project? What is the ratio using regular maintenance spending only?
3. How is free cash flow defined here? Are dividends, acquisitions, and one-time charges added back or netted out?
4. What was the ratio in the company's worst year on the chart? Could the debt have been serviced from that year's free cash flow?
5. When does the debt come due? Does a strong ratio hide a large block of debt maturing in a single year?
6. Is a low ratio caused by heavy growth investment or by weak cash generation? Does capex run far above depreciation, or near it?
7. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle? Which peer could deleverage fastest in a downturn?
8. Has recent debt funded buybacks, dividends, or acquisitions rather than the operating business?
9. Does interest coverage tell the same story as this ratio, or can the company afford the interest while being slow to repay the principal?
10. Is the ratio improving because free cash flow is rising or only because debt is being repaid while cash stays flat?
