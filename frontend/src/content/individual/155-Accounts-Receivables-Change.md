# Accounts Receivables Change

## Part 1: Definition

Accounts receivables change is the line that captures how much cash the company's unpaid customer bills tied up or released during the year. Accounts receivable is money owed by customers for sales already made but not yet collected. When receivables grow, the company has made sales without yet collecting the cash, so cash is tied up. When receivables shrink, the company has collected faster than it has made new credit sales, so cash is freed. On the cash flow statement this move is one of the operating adjustments. The line answers one question: did rising or falling customer balances consume cash or release it this year?

The operating section of the cash flow statement starts with net income and adjusts it toward the cash the business produced. Accounts receivables change is one of those adjustments and is usually the largest single piece of the working capital move. The line is negative when receivables grew, because a sale was booked but not yet collected. The line is positive when receivables shrank, because collections ran ahead of new credit sales.

| Direction of the line | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | Receivables grew. Sales were booked faster than cash was collected. |
| Near zero | Customer balances held roughly steady. Collections matched new credit sales. |
| Positive | Receivables shrank. Collections outran new credit sales, releasing cash. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The line is reported directly on the cash flow statement. The build is:

`Accounts Receivables Change = the change in receivables, with the sign flipped`

The figure equals the year-over-year change in the receivables balance on the balance sheet, flipped in sign for the cash flow statement. A rise in the balance becomes a negative figure here, because the rise tied up cash. A fall in the balance becomes a positive figure, because the fall released cash.

A few events move the line apart from ordinary collection. Selling receivables to a third party for early cash, called factoring, releases cash through this line in one period. Buying another company can import a receivables balance that lands outside this line. A steady run of small negative figures, a few percent of revenue each year, usually reflects the ordinary collection lag of a growing company. Rising sales carry an unfunded tail of unpaid bills.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Accounts receivables change shows whether reported sales are turning into collected cash. Revenue recognized minus this consumption is roughly the revenue actually collected. The line is the largest single piece of the gap between profit and operating cash flow, so it sits at the heart of judging cash quality.

The line also serves as a check on sales quality. When receivables rise much faster than sales, the company may be booking revenue ahead of collecting it, or loosening its credit terms to push sales. A consumption that stays in proportion to revenue growth is the ordinary collection lag. A consumption that outpaces revenue growth is a warning worth investigating.

The question the line answers is how much cash each year of sales growth left uncollected. Reading the line prices the funding cost of extending credit to customers.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Customer payment terms and the mix of customers drive the line. Longer terms and slower-paying customers raise receivables and deepen the consumption. Tighter collection and faster-paying customers shrink receivables and can release cash. Customer financial stress can stretch collection without any change in policy.

The pace of revenue recognition also moves the line. Booking sales quickly while cash arrives slowly widens the gap. Selling receivables for early cash releases cash in the period of the sale, and that release can mask an underlying slowdown in collection. An acquisition that brings in a receivables balance can distort the line for the year it closes. Ordinary growth drives the steady consumption, with the collection lag scaling up as sales expand.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal behavior depends on how a sector sells. Comparing the line across very different selling models can mislead.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Business-to-business sellers | Steady consumption | Customers buy on credit, so receivables grow with sales. |
| Cash and card retailers | Near zero | Customers pay at the point of sale, so few balances build. |
| Project and contract businesses | Lurches year to year | Billing is staged, so receivables swing with project timing. |
| Subscription services billed in advance | Small or releasing | Customers often pay before the service period. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one company sells on credit and another collects cash at the till. Calibrate the consumption against the company's own revenue growth and against direct peers.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the line as a percent of revenue across the years the app shows. Revenue is the common-size base for the cash flow statement. The key reading is the line against revenue growth. A consumption that stays in proportion to revenue growth is the ordinary rhythm of a credit-selling business. A consumption that runs well ahead of revenue growth is the classic signature of revenue being recognized faster than it is collected.

Releases should be read by cause. A positive line from genuinely faster collection is healthy. A positive line that appears only because sales are slowing is a downturn effect, not an improvement. A one-time release from selling receivables should be bracketed as an event rather than read as a trend. Compare the cumulative consumption across the table to see the total funding the receivables book has absorbed over the period.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Sales Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days Sales Outstanding measures how many days, on average, customers take to pay. Accounts receivables change measures the same collection behavior as cash moved during the year. One states collection in days, the other in dollars of cash tied up.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising Days Sales Outstanding alongside a deepening consuming line confirms that customers are paying more slowly and tying up more cash. A falling figure alongside a releasing line confirms that collection is speeding up. The pair shows the same collection trend from the time side and the cash side.

### Receivables Turnover

**Why read the two together.** Receivables Turnover measures how many times a year the company collects its average receivables balance. A slowing turnover means receivables are building relative to sales. Accounts receivables change shows the cash effect of that same buildup.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling turnover alongside a deepening consuming line points to receivables outgrowing sales, a possible collection or recognition problem. A stable turnover with a consumption in line with growth points to ordinary credit lag. The pair separates a healthy growing book from one that is stretching.

### Change In Working Capital

**Why read the two together.** Change In Working Capital is the summary line that nets all the operating accounts, and receivables is usually its largest component. Reading the receivables leg next to the total shows how much of the overall working capital move comes from collecting from customers.

**What the combination tells you.** A consuming working capital total driven mostly by this receivables line points to collection or sales-quality issues rather than inventory or supplier effects. A receivables line near zero with a consuming total points elsewhere in the loop. The pair shows which leg is moving the operating cash.

## Part 8: Limitations

Events can distort the line apart from ordinary collection. Selling receivables for early cash releases cash that hides a real slowdown in collection. An acquisition can import a balance that lands outside the line. A single year carries timing noise, since a large payment landing just before or after the year-end can swing the figure.

The line shows the cash effect but not the reason on its own. The same negative figure can be healthy growth or a collection problem, and the two look identical until read against the receivables balance and the collection-day measures. The line should be read by proportion to revenue growth and bracketed for one-time events.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the consumption in proportion to revenue growth, or is it running ahead of sales?
2. What does the cumulative consumption across the table say about the cash the receivables book has absorbed?
3. Did any year move because of selling receivables for early cash or an acquired balance?
4. When the line released cash, was it from faster collection or from slowing sales?
5. How does the cash effect reconcile with the receivables balance and the collection-day measures?
6. How much cash did each dollar of revenue growth leave uncollected this year?
7. Do the collection-day and turnover measures flag a buildup that the cash line confirms?
8. What would the line swing to if payment terms returned to a normal level?
9. How did the line behave in the company's worst sales year on the chart?
10. What funding cost does the collection lag imply at the growth rate the valuation assumes?
