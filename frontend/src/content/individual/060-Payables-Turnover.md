# Payables Turnover

## Part 1: Definition

Payables turnover measures how many times in a year a company pays off the money it owes its suppliers. When a company buys goods or services on credit, it receives the goods now and agrees to pay later. The unpaid bills owed to suppliers sit on the balance sheet as accounts payable. The metric answers one question: how quickly does the company settle its supplier bills?

A reading of 12 means the company pays off its full supplier balance about twelve times a year, or once a month on average. A reading of 4 means the company pays only four times a year, so it takes about three months to settle each bill. A higher number means the company pays suppliers fast. A lower number means the company pays slowly and keeps the cash longer. The metric is the mirror image of days payables outstanding, which states the same idea as a number of days.

| Reading | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 4 | Very slow payment. The company holds supplier money for months, which signals strong bargaining power or a cash shortage. |
| 6 to 10 | A common range. The company pays on roughly standard supplier terms. |
| 10 to 15 | Fast payment. The company settles bills quickly, which may capture discounts but gives up free financing. |
| Very high | The company pays almost on delivery, which suggests suppliers demand quick payment or the company chooses not to use trade credit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Payables Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Accounts Payable`

Cost of goods sold is the top number. Cost of goods sold is the direct cost of the products the company sold during the year, mostly the cost of materials and goods bought from suppliers. Purchases are the truest input, because only purchases create payables. Purchases are rarely reported, so cost of goods sold stands in. A larger cost figure relative to the payables balance means the company is paying off and replacing its supplier bills many times during the year.

Accounts payable is the bottom number. Accounts payable is the total unpaid bills the company owed its suppliers on the measurement date. The figure comes from the balance sheet. Some analysts use the average of the beginning and ending balance to smooth out swings. A larger payables balance relative to purchases means more bills are sitting unpaid, which lowers the turnover.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Suppliers who let a company pay later are lending it money at no interest. Payables turnover shows how fast the company gives that money back. A lower turnover means the company holds supplier cash longer and finances more of its operations for free. A higher turnover means the company returns the cash quickly and relies less on supplier credit.

The metric also reveals bargaining power. A company that dominates its suppliers can pay slowly and show a low turnover, because suppliers accept the wait to keep the business. A weak buyer must pay fast and shows a high turnover. Reading the metric next to competitors shows who controls the supply chain.

The metric also flags cash strain. A turnover that suddenly drops, meaning the company is paying much slower, can mean the company is short of cash and is stretching payment to conserve it. The investor then checks whether the slowdown reflects strength or distress.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move the number. Supplier terms set the baseline. A company offered 30-day terms pays faster, and shows a higher turnover, than one offered 60-day terms. Bargaining power lets a large buyer pay slowly and lowers the turnover. A deliberate policy to hold cash and pay late lowers it further. A choice to pay early for a discount raises it.

Accounting timing can distort a single reading. The payables balance is a single-date snapshot, so a large purchase made just before year end inflates the balance and depresses the turnover even if payment habits never changed. Using cost of goods sold instead of true purchases understates buying for a company that builds inventory, which distorts the turnover. A shift in the supplier mix toward vendors with different terms moves the number without any real change in financial health.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Normal turnover depends on the trade customs of the sector and on how much the company buys from suppliers.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Large retailers | Low turnover | Powerful buyers pay slowly, which funds a large part of store inventory with supplier credit. |
| Grocery and fast-moving goods | Low relative to sales speed | Goods sell fast while bills are paid slowly, which builds a strong cash position. |
| Manufacturers | Moderate | Steady purchasing settled on conventional supplier terms. |
| Services and software | High or hard to read | Few physical goods are bought, so cost of goods sold is small and the metric carries little meaning. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | These firms do not buy inventory from trade suppliers, so the metric does not describe them. |

Compare turnover only against companies in the same sector that buy similar inputs. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one company carries heavy physical purchasing and the other buys almost nothing from suppliers.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady, stable line means the company pays suppliers on consistent terms year after year. A steady fall in turnover means the company is paying slower, which can reflect growing bargaining power that frees up cash, or a cash shortage that forces delay. The two causes look identical on the chart, so the fall must be checked against the company's cash position and profit. A fall paired with healthy cash usually signals strength, while a fall paired with weak cash usually signals strain.

A steady rise in turnover means the company is paying faster, which uses more cash but may capture early-payment discounts or repair strained supplier ties. A sharp one-year swing often reflects a timing quirk, such as a large purchase near the reporting date, rather than a real change in habit. Read this line together with the receivables turnover line, because paying suppliers slowly while collecting from customers quickly is the strongest cash position a company can hold.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Payables Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days payables outstanding restates payables turnover as a number of days rather than a number of times. Dividing 365 by payables turnover gives the average days the company takes to pay suppliers. The two metrics carry the same information in different forms, so reading them together turns an abstract turnover count into a concrete waiting period. The days figure is often easier for a beginner to compare against standard supplier terms.

**What the combination tells you.** A turnover of 12 equals about 30 days to pay, while a turnover of 6 equals about 60 days. Comparing the days figure with the terms suppliers offer shows whether the company pays on time or stretches the bill. When the days figure runs well beyond standard terms, the company is leaning hard on supplier financing. The pair converts the turnover into a direct read of payment behavior.

### Receivables Turnover

**Why read the two together.** Receivables turnover measures how fast the company collects cash from customers. Payables turnover measures how fast the company pays cash to suppliers. The relationship between the two decides whether cash comes in before or after it must go out. A company that collects fast while paying slowly holds the strongest cash position.

**What the combination tells you.** High receivables turnover paired with low payables turnover means the company collects from customers before it pays suppliers, which funds operations cheaply. Low receivables turnover paired with high payables turnover means the company pays suppliers before customers pay it, which drains cash. When both move together, the cash position stays balanced. Reading the pair shows which side of the supply chain funds the business.

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** The cash conversion cycle measures the days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. The payment speed that payables turnover describes is one of the three pieces of that cycle. Paying suppliers slower, meaning a lower turnover, shortens the cycle and reduces the funding the company needs. Reading the turnover inside the full cycle shows how much it eases the company's cash needs.

**What the combination tells you.** Low payables turnover that shortens the cash conversion cycle means suppliers fund much of the working capital, which is a strong cash position. High payables turnover that lengthens the cycle means the company funds itself and needs more outside cash. When the turnover falls only because the company is straining to conserve cash, the lower funding need may mask distress. The pair separates healthy supplier financing from a forced delay in paying bills.

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio compares short-term assets to short-term bills due within a year, and accounts payable is one of those bills. Slow payment builds up the payables balance, which raises short-term bills and lowers the current ratio. The payment speed therefore helps explain where the current ratio sits.

**What the combination tells you.** Low payables turnover paired with a low current ratio can look risky, yet it may be safe if the company collects cash quickly and uses supplier credit on purpose. High payables turnover paired with a high current ratio means the company funds itself and keeps a thick cushion. Reading both shows whether a low current ratio reflects real weakness or a deliberate use of supplier financing. The combination guards against a false alarm from the current ratio alone.

## Part 8: Limitations

The metric uses cost of goods sold in place of purchases, because purchases are rarely reported. A company that buys inventory but holds it rather than selling it will show a cost of goods sold that understates true buying, which distorts the turnover. A company with low cost of goods sold relative to what it buys can show a turnover that misreads its true payment speed. The payables balance is also a single-date snapshot, so a large purchase near year end can distort the reading for reasons unrelated to normal practice.

The metric cannot tell strength from distress on its own. A low turnover may mean the company wields power over suppliers, or it may mean the company cannot pay on time. The two causes produce the same number and must be separated by checking cash and profit. The metric also ignores early-payment discounts, so a company that pays fast to capture discounts shows a high turnover while making a smart financial choice. The metric is a useful clue about how the company uses supplier credit, not a verdict on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What standard terms do the company's main suppliers offer, and does the implied payment speed match those terms or run well beyond them?
2. Has the turnover fallen because the company gained bargaining power, or because it is short of cash and stretching payment? What does the cash balance say?
3. How does the turnover compare with direct competitors that buy similar inputs? Does the company pay slower or faster than its peers?
4. Is the latest reading distorted by a large purchase made near the reporting date? What does the turnover look like averaged over the year?
5. How does the company's payment speed compare with its collection speed from customers? Does cash come in before or after it goes out?
6. Is the company passing up early-payment discounts by paying slowly, and does that cost more than the value of holding the cash?
7. Have suppliers reacted to slow payment by tightening terms, demanding deposits, or threatening to cut the company off?
8. Does the company depend on a few large suppliers who could force faster payment, or is the supplier base spread widely enough to support slow payment?
9. Has any recent improvement in cash flow come from paying suppliers slower rather than from the operating business?
10. If the turnover rose back to standard terms and the company had to pay faster, how much cash would it need to find, and could it raise that cash easily?
