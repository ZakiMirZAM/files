# Quality Score

## Part 1: Definition

The Quality Score is a number from 0 to 100 that reports how many basic quality tests a company passes right now. The score is a screening shortcut over accounting ratios. Accounting ratios are simple fractions built from the financial statements, such as profit divided by sales. The score is not a valuation. The score does not tell you what the business is worth or what the share is worth paying.

The score answers one question. Across profitability, efficiency, liquidity, solvency, and growth, how many of the standard markers of a financially healthy company does this business currently show? Profitability means how much profit the company earns on the money put into it. Efficiency means how much sales the company produces from its assets. Liquidity means whether the company can pay bills due within a year. Solvency means whether the company can carry its debt through a bad year. Growth means whether sales and profit are rising rather than shrinking.

A high score means the company clears most of the common hurdles a careful investor checks first. A low score means several hurdles are missed. The score is a first filter. The score sorts companies into those that deserve deeper work and those that need a strong reason to keep studying.

Common readings of the score:

| Score band | What that band usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 30 | Most measured tests fail. The company shows several signs of financial weakness at once. |
| 30 to 44 | A large share of tests fail. The fundamentals are below the common bar. |
| 45 to 59 | A mixed picture. Roughly half the tested hurdles are cleared. |
| 60 to 74 | Most tests pass. The fundamentals look solid on the measured checks. |
| 75 and above | Nearly every measured test passes. The fundamentals are strong across the board. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The app checks up to 15 ratios against fixed thresholds and reports the percent passed. A threshold is the fixed cutoff a ratio must clear to count as a pass. The score is computed in four plain steps.

First, the app reads each of the 15 ratios for the company. Second, the app compares each ratio to its fixed threshold and marks the ratio as a pass or a fail. Third, the app skips any ratio whose data is missing, so a company with only 12 usable ratios is graded out of 12 and is not penalized for the 3 it lacks. Fourth, the app divides the number of passed checks by the number of scored checks and multiplies by 100.

The formula is:

`Quality Score = (checks passed ÷ checks scored) × 100`

The passed checks are listed as strengths on the card. The failed checks are listed as weaknesses. You can always see exactly which tests produced the number.

The 15 checks and their thresholds:

| Check | Passes when | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Return On Equity | at least 0.15 | higher is better |
| Return On Invested Capital | at least 0.12 | higher is better |
| Return On Assets | at least 0.07 | higher is better |
| Return On Sales | at least 0.10 | higher is better |
| Gross Profit Ratio | at least 0.35 | higher is better |
| Asset Turnover | at least 0.50 | higher is better |
| Current Ratio | at least 1.50 | higher is better |
| Quick Ratio | at least 1.00 | higher is better |
| Debt Service Coverage | at least 1.50 | higher is better |
| Interest Coverage | at least 5.00 | higher is better |
| Debt To Equity | at most 1.00 | lower is better |
| Debt To Assets | at most 0.50 | lower is better |
| Margin Of Safety | at least 0.20 | higher is better |
| Free Cash Flow Growth | at least 0.05 | higher is better |
| Net Income Growth | at least 0.05 | higher is better |

The score maps to a verdict band. The bands are a label for the number, not a trading instruction.

| Score | Verdict band |
|---|---|
| 75 and above | STRONG BUY |
| 60 to 74 | BUY |
| 45 to 59 | HOLD |
| 30 to 44 | WEAK HOLD |
| Below 30 | AVOID |

## Part 2b: Every input, defined

The 15 checks fall into five families. Each family catches a different way a business can be weak. Each input below has its own full definition elsewhere in this same dropdown section.

The profitability family tests whether the company earns enough on the money entrusted to it. Return On Equity is net income divided by shareholders' equity. Net income is the final profit after all costs and taxes. Shareholders' equity is the owners' stake in the company. Return On Invested Capital is after-tax operating profit divided by all the capital invested in operations. Return On Assets is net income divided by total assets. Return On Sales is net income divided by revenue, where revenue is total sales. The Gross Profit Ratio is gross profit divided by revenue, where gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of making the goods. Durable high returns on capital are the most reliable accounting sign of a real competitive advantage. Low returns mean the business destroys value as it grows.

The efficiency family has one check. Asset Turnover is revenue divided by total assets. The check tests whether the asset base actually produces sales rather than sitting idle.

The liquidity family checks whether the company can pay bills over the next year without raising fresh money. The Current Ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities. Current assets are cash and items that turn into cash within a year. Current liabilities are bills due within a year. The Quick Ratio is the same test with inventory removed, because inventory can be slow to sell.

The solvency family checks whether the balance sheet survives a bad year. Debt Service Coverage compares operating earnings to the year's total debt payments. Interest Coverage compares operating earnings to the year's interest cost. Debt To Equity and Debt To Assets size the debt load against the capital base. A company that fails the solvency family can be killed by a downturn no matter how profitable it looks in good times.

The growth family confirms the business is moving forward rather than shrinking. A cheap but melting business is a classic value trap. Free Cash Flow Growth is measured as the average annual growth across the available history, so the check rewards consistency. Free cash flow is the cash left after running the business and paying for equipment. Net Income Growth is measured on the latest year only, so the check reacts quickly to one good or bad year.

Margin Of Safety sits outside those five families and is the only price-linked check. Margin Of Safety compares the app's discounted cash flow intrinsic value per share to the current share price. A discounted cash flow value is an estimate of worth built from the company's future cash flows. The check is included so the score reflects not just business quality but whether the price leaves room for error.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

No single ratio is trustworthy on its own. A spectacular Return On Equity can be manufactured with debt. A fat margin can hide a collapsing balance sheet. Cheapness alone says nothing about survival. By requiring a company to pass many independent tests at once, the score makes it hard for one flattering number to carry a weak business.

The score also standardizes the first hour of analysis. Every company in the app is asked the same questions, in the same order. The fixed routine removes the temptation to skip the uncomfortable checks for a stock you already like.

The score is a screening shortcut, not a verdict. The score points your attention. The score does not replace the deeper reading that follows.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The score moves for three kinds of reasons. Telling them apart is most of the work.

The first reason is real operating change. Rising margins, faster asset turns, debt paydown, or accelerating cash flow flip individual checks from fail to pass. The score then describes a genuinely better business.

The second reason is an accounting or structural effect that changes a ratio without changing the business. A large buyback shrinks equity. The buyback can push Return On Equity over its threshold while also raising Debt To Equity. An acquisition loads the balance sheet with goodwill and depresses Return On Assets. Goodwill is the premium paid above the fair value of an acquired company's net assets. A new lease rule or a capitalization choice moves the debt ratios.

The third reason is the price-linked check. Margin Of Safety depends on the current share price and on the discounted cash flow model's assumptions. The score can change when nothing about the company changed, simply because the stock moved.

One more effect is worth naming. Skipped checks shrink the base. A company with sparse data is graded on fewer tests. Each individual pass or fail then swings the score by more.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The thresholds are fixed across all sectors. Fixed thresholds penalize structurally low-ratio sectors and flatter high-ratio ones. The score is most honest when comparing companies in the same industry. The score is least honest across different industries.

| Industry type | Typical behavior of the score | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and branded consumer goods | High by default | Capital-light businesses clear the return and margin hurdles easily, so a high score is table stakes and the growth and valuation checks carry the real signal. |
| Utilities, telecoms, autos, airlines | Mid even when well run | Capital-intensive businesses structurally fail several checks such as gross margin, asset turnover, and the debt ratios, so a mid score can describe a sound operator. |
| Retailers and distributors | Mixed | Thin margins fail the margin checks while high turnover passes Asset Turnover, the opposite mix from luxury or pharma. |
| Banks and insurers | Unreliable | Their balance sheets make current ratio, the debt ratios, and coverage nearly meaningless, which is why the app values them with residual income and dividend models instead. |

The right habit is to compare a company's score against close peers and against its own history. Comparing a single company's score against the whole market is misleading.

## Part 5b: When this model applies and when it fails

The score works best for non-financial operating companies with several years of clean reported data. In those companies the accounting ratios map onto economic reality, so a pass or a fail means what it appears to mean.

The score misleads in four cases. The score fails for financial firms, because debt is their raw material rather than a risk signal. The score fails for early-stage or heavily investing companies, because reported earnings understate their economics while they spend to grow. The score fails for cyclical companies at the top of a cycle, because peak earnings make every return check pass exactly when the risk is highest. The score fails for companies in the middle of a restructuring, because the trailing numbers describe a business that no longer exists. In those four cases, read the score as a description of the past accounts, then move directly to the valuation models and the cash-quality checks.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The score is a snapshot, but its ingredients are charted throughout the app, so read the trend through them. A set of checks that passes more often each year usually marks either genuine operating improvement or mounting debt. The Debt To Equity trend tells you which.

A deteriorating set of checks is an early warning. The cash flow and coverage checks in particular tend to weaken before any single bad quarter shows up in the headlines. Watch those checks first.

Be suspicious of a score that flips between bands often. Frequent flipping usually means several ratios sit right at their thresholds. At a threshold the difference between a pass and a fail is noise rather than signal.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Income Quality (CFO/NI)

**Why read the two together.** The Quality Score trusts the reported earnings that feed the profitability checks. Income Quality compares operating cash flow to net income and tests whether those earnings are backed by real cash. A company can pass the return checks on paper while collecting little actual cash. Reading the pair shows whether the score rests on cash profit or on accounting profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A high score paired with weak Income Quality is a warning, not a comfort. The passing returns may come from earnings the company has not yet turned into cash. A high score paired with strong Income Quality means the profitability checks are backed by cash the company actually collected. When Income Quality is weak, treat every profitability pass in the score as unproven until the cash arrives.

### ROIC − WACC Spread

**Why read the two together.** The Quality Score asks whether returns clear fixed hurdles such as a Return On Invested Capital of 0.12. The ROIC − WACC Spread asks the sharper question of whether returns clear the company's own cost of capital. WACC is the weighted average cost of capital, the blended cost of the company's debt and equity. A company can pass the fixed hurdle while still earning less than its true cost of capital.

**What the combination tells you.** A passing return check with a positive spread means the company creates value above its cost of capital. A passing return check with a negative spread means the company clears the app's flat bar but still destroys value, because its capital is unusually expensive. Reading the two together separates a return that merely looks high from a return that actually beats the cost of the money behind it.

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** Margin Of Safety is already one of the 15 checks, and reading it on its own deepens the score. The score answers whether the business is sound. Margin Of Safety answers whether the price leaves room for error against the app's discounted cash flow value. A wonderful business at a hostile price still fails as an investment.

**What the combination tells you.** A high score with a passing Margin Of Safety means a sound business is also reasonably priced, the most attractive combination. A high score with a failing Margin Of Safety means the quality is real but the market has already paid for it. A low score with a wide Margin Of Safety means the price is cheap for a reason the failing checks may explain. Reading the pair keeps business quality and price quality as two separate judgments.

### DuPont ROE Decomposition

**Why read the two together.** The score contains a single Return On Equity check that passes or fails as one number. The DuPont ROE Decomposition splits that same Return On Equity into margin, asset turnover, and financial leverage. Financial leverage measures how much debt is amplifying the return. The split shows whether a passing Return On Equity was earned through the business or borrowed through debt.

**What the combination tells you.** A Return On Equity pass driven by margin and turnover describes a return earned by operations. A Return On Equity pass driven mostly by financial leverage describes a return propped up by debt, which the Debt To Equity check may already be failing. Reading the decomposition next to the score assigns the credit for a passing grade and shows whether the pass is durable or fragile.

## Part 8: Limitations

The score inherits every weakness of the ratios inside it. The score is built on reported accounting, so anything that distorts earnings or book values distorts the score. The score contains no judgment about moat durability, management honesty, or industry direction. The thresholds are universal, so cross-sector comparisons favor structurally high-margin industries.

The equal weighting is arbitrary. Failing Interest Coverage is far more dangerous than failing Asset Turnover, yet both cost the same single point. The Margin Of Safety check imports all the assumptions of the discounted cash flow model, including its growth and discount-rate choices. Because missing data shrinks the base, two companies with the same score may have been graded out of a different number of checks. Use the score to direct your attention. Do not use the score to replace it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which specific checks did this company fail? For each failure, is the cause a structural feature of the industry, a deliberate management choice, or a deterioration that started recently?
2. For each check that passed, would the pass still hold using average figures from the last five years instead of the latest year? Or is the pass the product of one unusually good year?
3. Is the Return On Equity pass earned through margin and asset turnover, or manufactured through debt? Open the DuPont ROE Decomposition and assign the credit.
4. How close is each ratio to its threshold? Which passes would flip to failures if the inputs moved by ten percent, and which failures would flip to passes?
5. Does Income Quality confirm that the earnings feeding the profitability checks are backed by operating cash flow? Or are accounting entries doing the work?
6. If the Margin Of Safety check is failing, is the cause a worse business or a higher price? Which discounted cash flow assumption would have to be wrong for the check to flip?
7. What would this score have looked like three years ago with the data available then? Did the change come from operations, from accounting, or from the share price?
8. Which single failing check, if fixed, would say the most about management's priorities? Has management acknowledged that problem in its own communications?
9. Are the gross margin and return passes consistent with the company's pricing power and supplier relationships? Or do the ratios flatter a competitive position that is weaker than it looks?
10. For this specific sector, which of the 15 checks are genuinely informative and which are structurally rigged to pass or fail? Re-grade the company in your head using only the informative checks.
