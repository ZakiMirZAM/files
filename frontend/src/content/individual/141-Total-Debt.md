# Total Debt

## Part 1: Definition

Total debt is the sum of all the money a company has borrowed and must pay back. Total debt adds together the short-term borrowings due within one year and the long-term borrowings due after one year. Total debt also includes the capitalized portion of leases, where a long rental commitment is recorded on the balance sheet as if the company had borrowed to buy the asset. Total debt answers one question: how large is the borrowed money that the company is on the hook to repay?

Total debt sits inside total liabilities on the balance sheet. Total liabilities also hold items the company owes that are not borrowings, such as money owed to suppliers and taxes owed. Total debt isolates the interest-bearing part, meaning the part that charges a fee for the use of the money. In the app's vertical analysis, total debt is shown as a percent of total assets, so a reader sees how much of everything the company owns is funded by borrowed money.

Common readings of total debt as a share of total assets:

| Total debt level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The company funds itself with equity and cash. Debt poses little risk. |
| Low to moderate | A normal mix of borrowing and owner capital for most healthy companies. |
| High share of assets | The company leans heavily on borrowed money. Earnings and refinancing matter much more. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The build is a simple sum:

`Total Debt = Short-Term Borrowings + Long-Term Borrowings + Capitalized Leases`

Short-term borrowings are the loans and bonds due within the next twelve months, plus the current portion of any long-term loan that comes due soon. Long-term borrowings are the loans and bonds due after twelve months. Both belong because each charges interest and each must be repaid or replaced on a set schedule.

Capitalized leases are long rental contracts recorded as debt under current accounting rules. The treatment of leases changed over time, so older statements may exclude leases that newer statements include. Some stricter analyses also add unfunded pension promises or redeemable preferred stock to total debt. The app reports total debt in its gross form, meaning before subtracting any cash. The net form that subtracts cash is covered in the Net Debt definition.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The plain size of borrowed money tells an investor how much fixed repayment the company has signed up for. Borrowed money must be serviced and repaid no matter how business is going. A large total debt narrows the room for error in a weak year.

Total debt is the base figure that many risk ratios use. Debt To Equity compares total debt against owner capital. Net Debt / EBITDA compares debt against yearly earnings. FCF / Total Debt compares the cash the company generates against the borrowed amount it must one day clear.

The gross figure is the conservative starting point. Cash can be spent, trapped abroad, or already promised elsewhere. The obligation to repay total debt stands whether or not the cash is truly available.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Total debt rises when the company issues bonds or draws bank loans to fund acquisitions, buybacks, dividends, or new projects. Total debt falls when the company repays loans or buys back its own bonds. Signing or ending large leases also moves the number under current accounting rules.

The share-of-assets reading also moves when total assets change, even if the borrowed amount is steady. A company that grows its asset base while holding debt flat will show a falling debt share. Accounting choices shift the boundary too. The lease accounting change added debt to many balance sheets without any new borrowing, so a jump at that point can be a rule change rather than a real increase.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as heavy depends on how steady the business is and how many hard assets it owns. Steady cash flows and pledgeable assets support more debt safely.

| Industry type | Typical total debt behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and infrastructure | High and stable | Predictable regulated cash flows support large, lasting debt. |
| Capital-light software | Very low | Few hard assets to borrow against and strong cash generation. |
| Real estate | Very high | Property is financed mostly with mortgages secured on the buildings. |
| Cyclical manufacturers | Moderate, judged at the trough | Earnings swing, so debt safe in a good year can be heavy in a bad one. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Borrowing is the raw material of the business, not a financing choice. |

A cross-sector comparison of total debt is meaningless when one sector borrows as its core activity, as banks do. Compare a company only against peers with the same asset base and the same cash-flow steadiness.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows total debt as a percent of total assets over several years. A steady rise in that share means the company is funding more of itself with borrowed money each year. A rising share calls for a check of what the new debt paid for, whether productive investment or buybacks and dividends. A steady decline means deliberate deleveraging, meaning paying debt down faster than the asset base shrinks.

A sudden step up in one year is usually a single large event, such as an acquisition funded with new bonds. A step can also be an accounting change, so the lease rule shift should be ruled out before reading a jump as real borrowing. High volatility in the debt share often points to a company that borrows to fund deals and then pays down between them. Read the internal mix too. A shift from long-term toward short-term borrowing raises refinancing risk, because short debt must be replaced sooner.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Total debt states the gross size of borrowing. Net Debt / EBITDA states the size of borrowing after cash, measured in years of earnings. Total debt alone does not say whether the load is large relative to what the business earns. Pairing the two turns a raw dollar figure into a sense of how heavy the burden really is.

**What the combination tells you.** A large total debt with a low Net Debt / EBITDA means the company holds a lot of cash against its borrowings and earns enough to cover them. A large total debt with a high Net Debt / EBITDA means a genuine burden that strains the earnings base. Watching both over time shows whether rising debt is being matched by rising earnings or is outrunning them.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Total debt is the borrowed side of the funding mix. Debt To Equity sets that borrowed side against the owner capital that absorbs losses first. The same total debt is far safer behind a thick equity cushion than behind a thin one. Reading both shows the structure of the funding, not just its size.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising total debt with a rising Debt To Equity means borrowing is growing faster than the equity base, so leverage is climbing. A rising total debt with a steady Debt To Equity means equity is keeping pace, which is far calmer. The pair separates more borrowing from more risk per dollar of owner capital.

### FCF / Total Debt

**Why read the two together.** Total debt is the amount that must eventually be cleared. FCF / Total Debt measures the free cash flow the company produces against that amount, meaning the cash left after running and maintaining the business. The ratio shows how many years of free cash flow it would take to repay all the borrowing. Total debt gives the size, and this ratio gives the speed of possible repayment.

**What the combination tells you.** A high total debt with a healthy FCF / Total Debt means the company can work the debt down from its own cash if it chooses. A high total debt with a weak FCF / Total Debt means repayment depends on refinancing rather than on internal cash, which is fragile when credit tightens. Reading the pair shows whether the borrowing is self-clearing or rollover-dependent.

## Part 8: Limitations

Total debt depends on accounting conventions that move the boundary. The lease rule change pulled commitments onto the balance sheet that were previously off it, so figures across different years or different countries may not be built the same way. Carrying values can also differ from what the company would actually pay to retire the debt today.

The gross figure ignores the cash a company holds, so total debt on its own can overstate the real burden for a cash-rich company. Total debt also says nothing about when the debts come due, what interest rates they carry, or what loan conditions attach to them. A company with modest total debt that all matures next year can be in more danger than a company with larger debt spread over twenty years. Total debt is the right first measure of borrowing and a poor last one.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How does total debt split between short-term and long-term borrowings, and how much comes due within the next three years?
2. Has the share of total assets funded by debt risen or fallen over the years on the chart, and what drove the change?
3. Did any jump in total debt come from real borrowing, or from the lease accounting rule change?
4. What did the most recent increase in debt pay for: productive investment, an acquisition, or buybacks and dividends?
5. How much of the debt carries a floating interest rate that rises with the market, and how much is fixed?
6. How does total debt as a share of assets compare with direct peers that own the same kind of asset base?
7. How large is cash held against total debt, and how much of that cash is truly available rather than trapped abroad?
8. Could the company repay a meaningful part of its debt from its own free cash flow, or does repayment depend on issuing new debt?
9. What loan conditions reference the debt level, and how much of an earnings decline would breach them?
10. Is the debt trajectory a stated policy, or has it drifted upward without a clear plan to bring it back down?
