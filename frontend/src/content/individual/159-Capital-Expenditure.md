# Capital Expenditure

## Part 1: Definition

Capital expenditure is the cash a company spends to buy, build, or upgrade long-lived assets. Long-lived assets include factories, machinery, and buildings. A long-lived asset is one the business expects to use for many years rather than sell within the year. Capital expenditure is shortened to capex. The line answers one question: how much cash did the company pour into its physical base this year?

Capital expenditure sits inside the investing section of the cash flow statement. The investing section records cash spent on or received from long-term assets. Capex is almost always a cash outflow, so the line is normally a negative number that subtracts from cash. The spending does not appear as a cost on the income statement all at once. Instead the cost is spread over the asset's useful life through depreciation, meaning the yearly charge that writes down the asset's value as it wears out.

Common readings of the line:

| Line reading | What that reading usually means |
|---|---|
| Large outflow versus depreciation | The company is investing to grow, replacing worn assets and adding new capacity. |
| Outflow close to depreciation | The company is spending about enough to maintain its existing asset base. |
| Outflow well below depreciation | The company is underinvesting, which lifts cash now but can erode the business later. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Capital expenditure is taken directly from the investing section of the cash flow statement, where it is reported as cash paid for property, plant, and equipment:

`Capital Expenditure = Cash paid to buy or build long-lived assets`

The single input is the cash the company actually spent on long-lived physical assets during the year. Property, plant, and equipment (often shortened to PP&E) is the accounting name for those assets. The figure is real cash leaving the business, not an accounting estimate, which is what makes it so useful.

Analysts often split capex into two kinds, though companies rarely report the split. Maintenance capex is the spending needed just to keep the current business running at its present level. Growth capex is the extra spending that adds new capacity or new lines of business. The total reported on the line combines both, and separating them usually requires judgment about how much spending merely replaces worn assets.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Capital expenditure decides how much of a company's cash is free for owners. Free Cash Flow is the cash left after capex, calculated as operating cash flow minus capital expenditure. Operating cash flow is the cash the core business generates before this spending. Capex is subtracted to reach Free Cash Flow. A company with heavy capex needs converts far less of its profit into the cash that funds dividends, buybacks, or debt repayment.

Capex also reveals management's plans. Rising capex signals a company building for growth, while flat or falling capex signals a company harvesting its existing base. The line shows where management is placing its bets in real cash, which is harder to dress up than words in a report.

The line is a window on the true cost of staying in business. Many companies must keep spending heavily on equipment just to maintain current earnings. Reading capex against depreciation shows whether reported profit reflects a business that funds its own upkeep or one that is quietly running its assets down.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The nature of the business is the biggest driver. A steel mill or a telecom network must spend enormous sums on physical assets, while a software firm spends almost nothing. Capex as a share of revenue is set mostly by what the company makes and how it makes it.

The growth phase moves capex next. A company expanding capacity, opening sites, or entering new markets spends far above depreciation. A mature company that is no longer growing spends closer to depreciation, just enough to replace what wears out. A shrinking or cash-strapped company may cut capex below depreciation, which lifts cash now at the cost of the future.

Capex can be lumpy and can be managed. A single large project, such as a new plant, can spike the line in one year and leave it low for several years afterward. Management can also defer capex to flatter Free Cash Flow in a weak year. Deferral boosts cash today but builds up a backlog of spending that must eventually be made, so a low-capex year is not always good news.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as heavy or light capex depends entirely on the industry, so the line is best judged as a share of revenue and against depreciation within the same sector.

| Industry type | Typical capex behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities, telecom, railroads | Very high, steady | Networks and infrastructure demand constant heavy investment to run and maintain. |
| Heavy industry and energy producers | High and cyclical | Plants and equipment are costly, and spending rises and falls with the commodity cycle. |
| Retailers | Moderate, tied to store growth | Spending tracks the pace of opening, refitting, and closing stores. |
| Software and other capital-light firms | Very low | The business runs on people and code rather than physical assets, so capex is minimal. |
| Banks and insurers | Low and not central | Their main investments are financial assets, so physical capex says little about them. |

A cross-sector comparison of raw capex is meaningless because a utility and a software firm live in different worlds. Compare capex as a share of revenue against direct competitors, and always read it next to depreciation in the same sector.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows capital expenditure as a multi-year series and as a percent of revenue. Reading capex as a percent of revenue removes the effect of the company simply getting bigger, so a rise or fall reflects a real change in investment intensity rather than scale. A line that holds a steady share of revenue points to a stable, predictable investment pattern.

A steady rise in capex as a percent of revenue means the company is investing more heavily, which can build future growth or can signal a business that needs ever more spending just to compete. A steady fall can mean improving efficiency or, less happily, a company starving its asset base to prop up Free Cash Flow. Read the trend next to revenue growth to tell investment from neglect.

Capex is lumpy, so a single-year spike is often one large project rather than a change in strategy. Watch for base effects. A year that defers spending will look strong on Free Cash Flow even though the backlog is growing, and the deferred spending will hit a later year. Compare the capex line with the depreciation line over time, because capex running below depreciation for years is a warning that the asset base is wearing out faster than it is being renewed.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free Cash Flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure, so capex is the exact amount subtracted to reach it. Operating cash flow is the cash the core business produces before investment in long-lived assets. Reading capex against Free Cash Flow shows how much of the company's cash generation is consumed by the need to invest in physical assets. The pairing is the single clearest view of how much cash is truly left for owners.

**What the combination tells you.** High operating cash flow with low capex produces strong Free Cash Flow, the mark of a capital-light, cash-generative business. High operating cash flow swallowed by heavy capex produces thin Free Cash Flow, meaning the business must keep reinvesting just to stand still. Watching both lines over time shows whether rising capex is eroding the cash that reaches shareholders or whether investment is genuinely producing more cash later.

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Capex / Depreciation compares the cash spent on assets with the yearly depreciation charge that writes down those assets. Depreciation roughly stands in for the spending needed just to replace assets as they wear out. Reading capex against this ratio shows whether the company is investing above, at, or below the rate at which its existing assets are aging. The ratio turns the raw capex figure into a judgment about growth versus maintenance.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio well above one means capex exceeds depreciation, so the company is expanding its asset base, which usually signals growth investment. A ratio near one means the company is spending about enough to maintain its assets, the steady state of a mature business. A ratio below one for several years means assets are wearing out faster than they are replaced, which flatters Free Cash Flow today and threatens the business tomorrow.

### Capex Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Capex Per Share divides total capital expenditure by the number of shares, putting investment on a per-share basis. Capex Per Share lets an owner see how much investment sits behind each share alongside per-share measures of cash and earnings. Reading the two together connects the company-wide capex line to the value of a single share.

**What the combination tells you.** Rising capex per share alongside rising cash and earnings per share suggests investment is producing returns for owners. Rising capex per share while per-share cash flow stalls suggests heavy spending that is not yet paying off, or may never. Reading capex per share next to the total line shows whether share issuance is diluting the investment each owner ultimately benefits from.

## Part 8: Limitations

Capital expenditure on the cash flow statement is a single combined figure, so it does not separate the maintenance spending needed to stand still from the growth spending that adds capacity. The two have very different meanings for value, yet the line lumps them together, and most companies never disclose the split. Without that breakdown, an investor cannot be sure how much of the spending is truly discretionary.

The line is also lumpy and easy to manage. A single large project distorts one year, and deferring routine spending can flatter Free Cash Flow in a weak year while a backlog quietly grows. Capex also excludes investment that does not pass through the property line. Research and expensed software development are left out, along with assets gained through acquisitions. A low capex figure can therefore understate how much a company is really investing. Read capex next to depreciation and across several years, never as a single number.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How does capex compare with depreciation this year and over the past several years? Is the company investing above or below the rate its assets age?
2. How much of this year's capex is maintenance spending to stand still, and how much is growth spending that adds capacity?
3. Did a single large project spike the line this year, or is the level representative of normal spending?
4. Has the company deferred capex in a weak year to flatter Free Cash Flow? If so, how large is the backlog building up?
5. What share of operating cash flow does capex consume, and how much Free Cash Flow is left for owners after it?
6. Is capex as a percent of revenue rising or falling, and does the trend match the company's revenue growth?
7. How does the company's capex intensity compare with direct competitors in the same sector?
8. Does the company invest heavily through channels that bypass the capex line, such as research or acquisitions?
9. When capex has run below depreciation, what evidence is there that the asset base is or is not wearing out?
10. Does management explain the purpose of major capital projects, and have past projects earned a return on the cash they consumed?
