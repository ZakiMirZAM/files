# Price / EPV

## Part 1: Definition

Price divided by EPV compares what the market charges for one share against what that share is worth on current earnings alone. EPV stands for earnings power value, the value of a share if the company never grows again. The ratio answers one question: how much of the share price is paid for current earnings, and how much is paid for growth that has not happened yet?

A ratio near 1 means the price and the no-growth value are about equal. The buyer pays only for current earnings, and any growth comes free. A ratio above 1 means the market pays a premium over the no-growth value, so part of the price rests on expected growth or improvement. A ratio below 1 means the market pays less than the no-growth value, which is a sign that investors doubt the company can even hold today's earnings.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Price ÷ EPV | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Well below 1 | The market pays less than the no-growth value. Investors expect earnings to shrink, or the share offers a discount on current earning power. |
| Near 1 | The price contains almost no growth. Any growth the company delivers comes free to the buyer. |
| 1 to 2 | A meaningful part of the price rests on future growth. The buyer must judge whether the company can protect that growth. |
| Well above 2 | Most of the price is growth that has not arrived. Current earnings alone would not come close to justifying the price. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Price / EPV = Current Price ÷ EPV per Share`

The current price is the top number. Current price is the market price of one share right now, the amount a buyer actually pays. The price reflects everything the market believes about the company, including its future growth. The price is the figure the ratio tests against a growth-free benchmark.

EPV per share is the bottom number. EPV per share is the value of one share built only from the earnings the business can sustain today, with growth valued at zero. The app builds it by capitalizing sustainable after-tax operating earnings at the cost of capital. The result is then adjusted for cash, debt, and minority interest. The cost of capital is the yearly return investors require to finance the company. Setting the price over this no-growth value isolates how much of the price is growth.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A share price on its own says nothing about how much growth a buyer is paying for. Price over EPV states that split as one number. A ratio near 1 tells the investor that current earnings alone support the price. A ratio well above 1 tells the investor that the case for the share rests on growth.

The ratio reframes the usual price multiples. A price-to-earnings ratio compares price against one year of profit. Price over EPV compares price against a full-cycle, growth-free value of the whole operating business. The second view is harder to fool with a single strong year, because sustainable earnings smooth the cycle.

The ratio also directs where the research should go. When the ratio sits near 1, the investor can focus on whether today's earnings are durable. When the ratio sits well above 1, the important question becomes whether the company can grow at returns above its cost of capital. Greenwald warns that only growth inside a protected market position adds value, so a high ratio demands proof of that protection.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Both halves of the ratio move it. The price moves every day with sentiment, rates, and news. A shift in any of them can move the ratio while the business is unchanged. A jump in the share price lifts the ratio on its own. EPV per share moves much more slowly, because its inputs are a full-cycle margin and a cost of capital that shift only gradually.

The cost of capital deserves special attention, because it sits inside the denominator. A lower cost of capital raises EPV per share and pushes the ratio down, even though nothing about the business or the price has changed. Falling interest rates therefore tend to lower the ratio across the market, and rising rates lift it. A change in sustainable earnings, from a better or worse full-cycle margin, moves EPV per share and the ratio in the same way.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a high ratio depends on how much genuine growth a sector can convert into value. A high ratio is normal where protected growth is real, and a warning where it is not.

| Industry type | Typical ratio behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer staples and established brands | Modestly above 1 | Steady margins make EPV per share reliable, and prices carry only limited growth. |
| High-growth software and internet | Well above 2 | The market prices years of expansion, so a high ratio is expected rather than a warning by itself. |
| Cyclical manufacturers and commodity producers | Distorted at cycle turns | A revenue peak inflates EPV per share and lowers the ratio, while a trough does the reverse. |
| Mature, slow-growth industrials | Near 1 | Little growth is expected, so the price stays close to the no-growth value. |
| Banks and insurers | Not computed | The app does not build EPV for financial companies, so the ratio is unavailable. |
| Companies with negative sustainable earnings | Not computed | A no-growth value of a loss-making operation has no meaning, so no ratio is shown. |

Comparing the ratio across sectors mostly compares how much growth each sector's price carries. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless unless the companies share similar margin stability and growth prospects.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Because EPV per share moves slowly, most of the swing in the ratio over time comes from the price. A ratio that climbs year after year usually means the market is paying more and more for growth, not that the no-growth value is falling. A ratio that drifts down toward 1 means the market is pricing out growth and settling on current earnings.

A ratio that falls below 1 and stays there deserves attention. A lasting reading under 1 means the market expects the sustainable earnings base to shrink, which is a signal to check whether the earning power is truly eroding. Watch the price and EPV per share separately as well. A ratio that holds steady only because a falling cost of capital is lifting EPV per share is resting on interest rates, not on a cheaper price.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DCF Intrinsic Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Price over EPV measures the price against a growth-free value. The DCF intrinsic value per share measures value with the full forecast of growth included. Reading the two together shows both what the market pays for growth and what the model thinks that growth is worth. The gap between EPV per share and the DCF value is the model's own estimate of the growth premium.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio above 1 paired with a DCF value well above EPV per share means the market and the model both credit growth, and the question is who is closer to right. A ratio above 1 paired with a DCF value near EPV per share means the market pays for growth the model does not see, which is a warning. When the price sits below both EPV per share and the DCF value, the share looks cheap on current earnings and on the forecast alike.

### P/E

**Why read the two together.** P/E is the price-to-earnings ratio, the share price divided by one year of earnings per share. P/E and price over EPV both measure how expensive a share is, but against different benchmarks. P/E uses a single recent year of profit, while price over EPV uses a full-cycle, growth-free value. Reading them together separates a one-year distortion from a lasting premium.

**What the combination tells you.** A high P/E next to a price over EPV near 1 suggests the latest year understated normal earnings, so the share is less expensive than the P/E alone implies. A low P/E next to a price over EPV well above 1 suggests the latest year overstated normal earnings, so the share is more expensive than it looks. When both readings are high, the premium for growth is real and consistent across both benchmarks.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC is return on invested capital, the after-tax operating profit earned per dollar of capital put into the business. Greenwald's framework makes ROIC the test of whether growth deserves any price at all. Price over EPV states how much growth the market is paying for, and ROIC against the cost of capital states whether that growth can create value. The two answer the premium question from opposite sides.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio above 1 paired with ROIC well above the cost of capital describes a company whose growth can justify a premium. A ratio above 1 paired with ROIC near or below the cost of capital describes a premium paid for growth that adds nothing, which favors the seller. A ratio below 1 paired with a high ROIC is the rare case where profitable current earnings are on sale and any growth comes free.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio is only as good as the EPV per share underneath it. A sustainable earnings estimate built on too short a history, or on a revenue peak, produces a distorted denominator and a misleading ratio. The single division by the cost of capital means a small change in that rate moves EPV per share and the ratio noticeably, so the ratio partly tracks interest rates rather than the business.

The ratio also cannot tell a fair growth premium from an excessive one on its own. A reading of 2 is reasonable for a protected, fast-growing business and far too high for an ordinary competitor. A franchise is a market position that keeps competitors from copying the returns, and only franchise growth earns a large premium. The ratio is silent for financial companies and for loss-making companies, and it says nothing about how durable today's earnings are. The ratio locates the growth premium but does not judge whether the premium is deserved.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the ratio above or below 1, and how much of the current price is therefore being paid for growth rather than current earnings?
2. What sustainable earnings sit in the EPV per share denominator, and do the years behind them cover a full cycle?
3. Is current revenue near a cyclical peak or trough, and would a normalized revenue base change the ratio much?
4. If the ratio is above 1, what specific growth must the company deliver to justify the premium the market is paying?
5. Does the company earn returns above its cost of capital, so that the growth in the premium can actually create value?
6. How much of a recent move in the ratio came from the share price, and how much from a change in EPV per share?
7. Is a low cost of capital flattering the ratio, and what would the ratio be if the cost of capital rose by one point?
8. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors at the same point in the cycle, and which one carries the largest growth premium?
9. If the ratio is below 1, does the market have a real reason to expect the earnings base to shrink?
10. Would you accept this ratio if the company never grew again, and what does that answer say about your thesis?
