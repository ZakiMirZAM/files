# Revenue Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Revenue growth measures how fast a company's sales are rising or falling. Revenue, also called the top line, is the total money a company brings in from selling its goods and services before any costs are taken out. Revenue growth compares this year's revenue with last year's and states the change as a percentage. A positive figure means sales grew, a negative figure means sales shrank, and a number near zero means sales held about flat.

It is the most basic measure of whether a business is expanding. Profit can be managed with cost cuts and accounting choices, but revenue is harder to dress up: either customers are paying the company more or they are not. Revenue growth is usually the first thing analysts look at, because a company that cannot grow its sales will eventually struggle to grow its profit. The figure is the starting point for almost every other growth question.

| Revenue growth level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | Sales are shrinking. The business is losing customers, pricing, or market share. |
| 0 to 5 percent | Slow growth, roughly in line with or below the overall economy. |
| 5 to 15 percent | Healthy growth, ahead of the economy and likely gaining ground. |
| Above 15 percent | Fast growth, typical of younger companies or those in expanding markets. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Revenue Growth = (Revenue this year − Revenue last year) ÷ Revenue last year`

Subtract last year's revenue from this year's to get the change in dollars, then divide by last year's revenue to turn that change into a percentage. This is a year-over-year figure, comparing one full period with the same period a year before. If revenue was 100 last year and 112 this year, the growth is (112 − 100) ÷ 100, which is 12 percent.

Year-over-year comparison matters because it lines up the same stretch of the calendar, which removes the effect of seasonal swings. A retailer earns far more in the holiday quarter than in the spring, so comparing one quarter with the previous quarter would be misleading. Comparing this holiday quarter with last holiday quarter is a fair test. The calculation is plain, but it pays to know what is inside the revenue: organic growth from selling more, price increases, acquisitions that add new sales, and currency swings can all move the figure, and they are not equally valuable.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Revenue growth shows whether the market for a company's products is expanding and whether the company is winning its share of that market. A business growing its sales has room to grow profit, to invest, and to reward shareholders. A business with flat or falling sales is fighting a harder battle, because it must squeeze more profit from a shrinking base, which has limits. Growth is the fuel for almost everything else a company wants to do.

The figure is also a useful reality check on the rest of the financial statements. Profit that rises while revenue falls can only come from cost cuts, which cannot continue forever. Profit that rises alongside revenue is more durable, because it rests on a growing business rather than a shrinking cost base. Revenue growth gives the context that makes profit growth meaningful.

A natural benchmark is nominal growth in the overall economy, which combines real growth with inflation and tends to run somewhere around 5 to 7 percent. A company growing faster than that is taking share or operating in an expanding market. A company growing slower is losing ground in real terms, even if its sales technically rose.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces drive revenue growth. Volume is the cleanest: selling more units to more customers. Price is the next: raising prices lifts revenue even if volumes hold steady, and a company with pricing power can grow this way for years. Inflation flatters the figure, because rising prices across the economy push nominal revenue up without any real expansion, which is why the economy-wide benchmark already includes inflation.

Acquisitions can add a large jump in revenue in a single year, but that growth is bought rather than earned and does not repeat unless the company keeps acquiring. Currency movements swing reported revenue for companies that sell abroad, since foreign sales are translated back into the home currency at changing exchange rates. The base year also matters: a weak prior year makes this year's growth look large, and a strong prior year makes it look small. Because all these forces mix together in one number, the quality of revenue growth depends on what is driving it, not just on its size.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Normal revenue growth varies widely by industry, because some markets are mature and steady while others are young and expanding. A growth rate that is impressive in one sector is ordinary in another.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and technology | Fast | Expanding markets and scalable products support double-digit growth. |
| Consumer staples | Slow and steady | Mature demand grows roughly with population and prices. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Slow | Regulated, saturated markets allow only modest growth. |
| Industrial and cyclical | Variable | Sales rise and fall sharply with the economic cycle. |
| Commodity producers | Volatile | Revenue swings with commodity prices regardless of volume. |

Compare revenue growth within an industry and across a full cycle. A cyclical company can post very fast growth coming out of a downturn and then negative growth heading into one, so a single year tells you little. Judge it against peers facing the same market conditions.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady, multi-year record of solid revenue growth is the mark of a durable business in a healthy market. It shows the company keeps finding new customers or charging more for what it sells. A decelerating trend, where growth slows year after year, can signal a maturing market, rising competition, or a product losing its edge, and it often arrives before any decline in profit.

Look behind the numbers for the source of the growth. A trend lifted by a string of acquisitions is different from one driven by organic sales, because the acquired growth stops when the deals stop and often brings extra costs and debt. A single-year spike may come from a one-time contract, a price increase, or a favorable currency move rather than lasting expansion. Read revenue growth next to the broader economy too, because growth that merely keeps pace with inflation is standing still in real terms. The most valuable pattern is organic growth that runs steadily ahead of the economy across both good years and bad.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Income Growth

**Why read the two together.** Revenue growth is the top line and net income growth is the bottom line. Comparing the two shows what happens to a sale on its way down to profit. Revenue growth tells you the business is expanding, and net income growth tells you whether that expansion is reaching the owners.

**What the combination tells you.** When net income grows faster than revenue, the company is becoming more profitable as it grows, a sign of operating leverage or improving margins. When net income grows slower than revenue, costs are rising faster than sales, and the growth is getting more expensive. When revenue grows but net income falls, the expansion is not paying off. Reading both shows the quality of the growth, not just its existence.

### Operating Leverage

**Why read the two together.** Operating leverage measures how much profit changes for a given change in revenue. Revenue growth is the input, and operating leverage describes how that input is amplified or dampened on the way to operating profit. The two together explain why a given level of sales growth produces the profit growth it does.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with high operating leverage and rising revenue can show profit growing much faster than sales, because fixed costs are spread over more revenue. The same high leverage works in reverse when revenue falls, magnifying the drop in profit. Reading revenue growth alongside operating leverage shows how sensitive the bottom line is to the top line, which matters most for cyclical businesses where revenue swings hard.

## Part 8: Limitations

Revenue growth says nothing about profitability. A company can grow sales rapidly while losing money on every sale, so fast growth is not automatically good. The figure also hides what is driving it. Growth bought through acquisitions, lifted by a one-time price increase, or flattered by a weak prior year looks the same in the headline number as steady organic expansion, yet it is far less valuable and may not repeat.

Inflation and currency movements distort the figure too, inflating nominal growth without any real increase in the business, or swinging reported revenue for companies that sell abroad. Aggressive revenue recognition can pull future sales into the current period, making growth look stronger than the underlying demand. And because the calculation depends on the base year, an unusual prior year can make a normal year look like a boom or a bust. Revenue growth is a starting point, not a conclusion, and it must be read with margins, cash flow, and the source of the growth to mean anything.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the revenue growth organic, or is it coming from acquisitions that will not repeat unless the company keeps buying?
2. How does the growth compare with nominal growth in the overall economy of roughly 5 to 7 percent?
3. Is the growth driven by selling more, by raising prices, or merely by inflation lifting the whole market?
4. Did a weak or strong prior year flatter or depress this year's growth figure?
5. Is net income growing as fast as revenue, or are costs rising faster than sales?
6. How much of the reported growth is a currency effect for a company that sells abroad?
7. Is the multi-year trend accelerating, steady, or decelerating, and what does that say about the market and the competition?
8. Does a single-year spike trace back to a one-time contract or price increase rather than lasting demand?
9. Is revenue recognition aggressive enough to be pulling future sales into the current period?
10. Across a full economic cycle, is the average growth ahead of the economy, or only in the good years?
