# Sloan Accruals Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The Sloan accruals ratio measures how much of a company's reported profit is made of accounting estimates rather than collected cash, scaled against the size of the company. Accruals are the parts of profit that are recorded under accounting rules before any cash has changed hands. Accrual accounting records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when the cash moves. The ratio compares those non-cash accruals with total assets, so companies of different sizes can be compared. The ratio answers one question: how much of this profit exists only on paper?

A ratio of +8 percent means accruals equal to eight percent of total assets sit inside the period's profit, so that profit has not yet arrived as cash. A negative ratio means cash exceeded reported profit, which is the conservative and safer configuration. A high positive ratio is the warning sign. The vault, drawing on the accounting-quality literature, treats a wide gap between accrual profit and cash as a red flag for earnings manipulation. The ratio is the formal, scaled version of the question income quality asks.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0 (negative) | Cash exceeded reported profit. The most conservative configuration. |
| 0 to 5 percent | Low accruals. Profit is well supported by cash. |
| 5 to 10 percent | Moderate accruals. Worth watching, especially if rising. |
| Above 10 percent | High accruals. A large share of profit has not turned into cash. A warning sign. |
| Above 25 percent | Very high. Profit is heavily built on accounting estimates. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Sloan Accruals Ratio = (Net Income − Operating Cash Flow) ÷ Total Assets`

The top number is accruals, found by subtracting operating cash flow from net income. Net income is the reported profit from the income statement. Operating cash flow is the cash the business actually collected from operations. The difference is the part of profit that did not arrive as cash, which is the accrual portion. A large positive difference means reported profit ran well ahead of cash. The vault stresses that investors test earnings quality by comparing net income with cash flow from operations, and this top number is that comparison.

Total assets is the bottom number. Total assets is everything the company owns, taken from the balance sheet. Dividing accruals by total assets scales the figure to the size of the company, so a small firm and a large firm can be compared on the same footing. Many versions use average total assets, the average of the start and end of the year, to smooth out a balance sheet that changed during the year. Some versions of the Sloan ratio measure accruals from balance-sheet changes instead of from the cash flow statement. The two approaches usually point the same way.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Profit built on accruals tends to be less durable than profit backed by cash. Estimates can be reversed, and aggressive estimates eventually correct. The vault warns that companies have inflated profit by recording revenue too soon or hiding expenses. A high accruals ratio flags that risk. The ratio measures earnings quality with a single scaled number.

The ratio improves on a simple cash-versus-profit comparison by scaling to company size. A raw gap between profit and cash is hard to compare across firms. Dividing by total assets makes the figure comparable across companies and across years. The ratio turns earnings quality into a number that can be screened in bulk.

The ratio also has a track record in the accounting-quality research the vault draws on. Companies with high accruals have tended to report weaker profit in following years, because the accrued profit does not repeat. The ratio gives an early read on whether today's profit is likely to last.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Working capital changes move the accruals at the top. When unpaid customer bills rise, profit is booked but cash lags, which raises accruals. When inventory builds up, cash is tied up and accruals rise. A company that recognizes revenue aggressively, meaning it books sales before the cash is secure, pushes accruals higher. Collecting cash faster than booking profit pushes accruals down toward negative.

Non-cash charges also shape the figure. Depreciation is the accounting cost of wearing out equipment. Depreciation is subtracted from net income but uses no cash, which tends to lower accruals on the cash-flow definition. Total assets at the bottom moves the ratio too. A company that grows its asset base quickly will show a smaller ratio for the same dollar of accruals. One-off items distort a single reading. A large one-time non-cash gain inflates profit without cash and raises accruals for a reason unrelated to the ongoing business. A ratio that climbs year after year is the durable warning, not a single high reading.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a normal accruals ratio depends on how a sector grows and how much it invests in working capital.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Fast-growing companies | Higher accruals | Growth ties up cash in inventory and receivables, so profit runs ahead of cash. |
| Mature, stable companies | Low accruals near zero | Cash and profit track each other closely once growth slows. |
| Capital-heavy industrials | Often lower or negative | Large depreciation lowers cash-based accruals on the cash flow definition. |
| Seasonal businesses | Swings within the year | Working capital builds and unwinds across seasons, so a single quarter can mislead. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Operating cash flow is distorted by lending and investing flows, so the accrual measure does not describe them. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one sector grows fast and ties up cash while another is mature and cash-stable. Compare a company against direct peers and watch its own ratio across several years.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows the ratio over several years next to the live number. A ratio that stays low and steady means profit is consistently backed by cash, which is the healthy pattern. A ratio that climbs steadily is the pattern that deserves the most attention. A rising accruals ratio means each year's profit leans more on estimates and less on cash, which often precedes a profit disappointment. A multi-year climb rarely reverses without a real change in collection or accounting.

A single high year can be a growth effect, because a surge in inventory or receivables lifts accruals for one year. The high year still matters because it can mark the start of a climb. Watch for base effects from rapid asset growth, which can shrink the ratio and hide rising accruals. Compare the trend against net income growth. A company whose profit rises while accruals climb is producing profit of falling quality. Read several years together, since one year of working-capital swing can mislead.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Income Quality (CFO/NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income quality divides operating cash flow by net income as a simple ratio. The Sloan accruals ratio measures the same gap between profit and cash, but scales it against total assets. Both metrics ask how much of reported profit is real cash. Reading the pair confirms whether a weak income quality reading is matched by a high accruals reading.

**What the combination tells you.** A low income quality ratio paired with a high accruals ratio is a strong warning, because both say profit is built on estimates. When income quality is healthy and accruals are low, the profit is well supported by cash. When the two disagree, the difference usually comes from depreciation or fast asset growth, which the scaling treats differently. The pair gives a fuller earnings-quality picture than either metric alone.

### Net Income

**Why read the two together.** Net income is part of the top number of the accruals ratio, so the accruals reading only makes sense alongside the profit it is testing. The ratio judges how much of that net income is cash and how much is accrual. Reading the pair shows whether rising profit is backed by cash or is drifting into accruals.

**What the combination tells you.** Rising net income paired with a rising accruals ratio is a classic warning, because the profit is growing while its cash backing weakens. Rising net income with a low, steady accruals ratio means the growth is cash-backed and durable. The research the vault draws on suggests the high-accruals version of that profit is the one most likely to fade. The pair separates the size of the profit from the quality of it.

### Operating Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Operating cash flow is the other part of the top number, subtracted from net income to find accruals. The accruals ratio is large precisely when operating cash flow falls short of profit. Reading the pair shows how far cash is trailing the reported profit. Operating cash flow is the real-cash benchmark the accruals figure is measured against.

**What the combination tells you.** A high accruals ratio next to weak operating cash flow confirms that profit is not converting to cash. When operating cash flow keeps pace with or exceeds profit, accruals stay low and the profit is trustworthy. The vault cautions that operating cash flow can itself be inflated by reclassifying financing inflows, so a suspiciously strong cash flow deserves a second look. Reading the two together shows whether the cash backing the profit is genuine.

## Part 8: Limitations

A high accruals ratio is not proof of manipulation. Fast-growing companies show high accruals for the innocent reason that growth ties up cash in inventory and receivables. The ratio also depends on which definition is used, since the cash-flow version and the balance-sheet version can differ. Depreciation policy shifts the cash-flow version, so two companies with the same assets can show different accruals by depreciating at different speeds.

The ratio breaks down for banks and insurers, where operating cash flow is distorted by lending. The ratio uses one period, which can be lumpy because of seasonal working-capital swings. A single reading can mislead, so the trend matters more than any one year. The vault notes that operating cash flow can be inflated by accounting tricks, which means a low accruals ratio built on a doctored cash flow statement is a false comfort. The ratio is a strong screen for earnings quality and a poor final verdict on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current accruals ratio high because of a one-time non-cash gain or because of a steady climb over several years? Which inputs drove the change?
2. Are unpaid customer bills growing faster than sales? Could the company be booking revenue it has not yet collected?
3. Is the company building inventory ahead of demand, and is that the reason profit ran ahead of cash this year?
4. Is the high ratio a growth effect from tying up cash in working capital, or a sign of aggressive accounting?
5. Does the income quality ratio tell the same story? Are both metrics pointing to profit built on estimates?
6. Is operating cash flow genuine, or could it be inflated by financing inflows reclassified as operating cash?
7. Does net income contain one-time non-cash gains? What is the accruals ratio using recurring profit only?
8. Has the ratio climbed while reported profit kept rising? Is the quality of that profit falling each year?
9. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors over the same years? Is the company's earnings quality better or worse than its peers?
10. Has rapid asset growth shrunk the ratio and hidden a rising level of accruals in the profit?
