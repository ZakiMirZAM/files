# Chance The Story Ends

## Part 1: Definition

The chance the story ends is the probability that the business stops operating within the next ten years. The story ends when an all-or-nothing event stops the company outright. Debt that cannot be repaid or refinanced can end the story, and so can cash that runs out. So can the loss of a court case, a license or a product approval. The measure also records what the owners keep if that happens. The measure answers one question: how likely is it that the company never delivers the cash flows the valuation counts, and what would shareholders be left with?

A discounted cash flow valuation values a going concern, which is a business expected to keep operating for a long time. The going-concern assumption overstates the value when there is a real chance the business will not survive. The chance the story ends corrects the value for that risk directly. Ordinary ups and downs that only lower profit belong in the cash flows and the cost of capital instead.

Common readings of the chance:

| Chance level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | A large, profitable company with little debt and open access to banks, bond buyers and new shareholders. A figure near zero is a valid answer. |
| Low | Some debt or some losses, with a cushion of cash and a steady record of raising money when needed. |
| Material | Losses that consume cash, large debt falling due within a few years, or dependence on new money arriving on time. |
| High | Debt that operations cannot repay, a weak credit grade, or one event such as a trial or an approval that decides survival. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Value per share = Value if the story works × (1 − Chance it ends) + Proceeds per share if it ends × Chance it ends`

Value if the story works is the discounted cash flow value per share with the story's inputs, assuming the business survives. Chance it ends is the probability that the business stops within ten years, stated as a fraction. Proceeds per share if it ends is what shareholders would receive from a forced sale of the assets after lenders are repaid, divided by the number of shares. Lenders are paid before shareholders, so the proceeds for shareholders are often zero.

A round example shows the arithmetic. A value of 40 if the story works, a chance of 0.2 that the story ends and proceeds of 10 per share give a value of 40 × 0.8 + 10 × 0.2, which is 34 per share. The loss of value comes from the gap between the working value and the forced-sale value. A forced sale fetches less than the business is worth while operating.

The conversion anchors the chance on the share of companies with the same synthetic rating that defaulted within ten years. A synthetic rating is the credit grade the site estimates from the company's interest coverage, which is operating income divided by interest expense. The conversion then adjusts the anchor for what the story says about cash, debt falling due and access to banks, bond buyers and new shareholders. The proceeds default to zero unless the story gives a reason to expect more.

The chance is handled apart from the discount rate on purpose. The discount rate, or cost of capital, prices the ordinary risk that cash flows come in higher or lower than expected. The end of the story is a different kind of risk: the business either survives or does not. Raising the discount rate to cover that risk would cut the value of every future year by a compounding amount and would hide the size of the survival risk inside another number.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A valuation that assumes survival is too high for any company with a real chance of failing. The risk of failure is highest for young companies that must pass several tests before becoming established businesses. The risk is also high for older, declining companies that carry heavy debt.

Stating the chance separately makes the ending of the story visible. The reader can see how much of the value survives if the worst outcome happens and how likely the story says that outcome is. Two investors can then argue about a probability and a forced-sale value rather than about a vague extra premium in the discount rate.

For most large and profitable companies the chance is close to zero, and adding an extra discount for failure would count the same risk twice. The measure matters most where it should matter: for companies whose survival genuinely depends on cash, debt or a single event.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Cash and losses come first. For a company that loses money, dividing the cash on hand by the yearly loss before depreciation shows how many months the cash lasts. When the cash lasts only a short time, the chance rises that the story ends before the business turns profitable.

Debt falling due comes next. The debt maturities note in the annual report shows how much must be repaid or refinanced in each of the next five years. Heavy repayments in a year when markets may be closed raise the chance, even for a company with steady profits. Interest coverage and the synthetic rating summarize how comfortably operating income carries the debt.

Access to capital changes the chance with market conditions. In strong markets even companies with little cash can raise new money. In weak markets even companies with large cash balances can find themselves under threat. Size, a steady record of cash generation and long relationships with lenders all widen that access.

Single events can dominate everything else. A company that needs one approval, one license renewal or one court ruling to survive carries a chance that no ratio captures. Insurance and hedging can remove some of these risks, and the story should say which ones.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | Typical chance | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Young technology and biotechnology companies | Often material or high | Losses consume cash, and survival can hinge on one product or one approval. |
| Heavily indebted mature companies | Depends on refinancing | Profits may be steady, but large repayments falling due can end the story if lenders refuse to refinance the debt. |
| Utilities and other regulated businesses | Usually low | Regulated prices make revenue steady, which keeps lenders willing to refinance. |
| Cyclical and commodity producers | Rises in downturns | Profits can collapse in a bad year while the debt stays the same. |
| Retailers and restaurants with many leases | Higher than the balance sheet suggests | Long lease commitments behave like debt that must be paid in every year. |
| Banks, insurers and property trusts | Not used | The story valuation does not run for these companies, because the site's firm-level cash flow valuation does not apply to them. |

Comparing the chance across sectors is meaningless without the company's own cash, debt and access to capital. Two companies in the same industry can face very different chances, and two companies in different industries can face the same one.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app does not chart the chance itself, so read the series that drive it. A steady decline in interest coverage means debt is growing faster than earnings, and the synthetic rating moves down with it. A rising Net Debt / EBITDA line means the debt is taking more years of earnings to repay. Each of those trends pushes the chance up.

For a company that loses money, read the cash balance against the yearly loss over several years. Cash that falls while losses persist shortens the time the cash lasts. A sudden rise in cash usually means new money was raised, which buys time but does not change the business.

A single bad year deserves a careful reading rather than alarm. An earnings collapse in a downturn often recovers with the cycle. The collapse still shows how close the company came to failing and how large the cushion must be in the next downturn.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest coverage measures how many times operating income covers the year's interest. The synthetic rating that anchors the chance is built from interest coverage. A change in coverage therefore moves the anchor of the chance directly.

**What the combination tells you.** High coverage with a chance near zero is the normal profile of a sound company. Low coverage with a chance near zero is a contradiction that the story must explain, for example with cash or assets that could repay the debt. Falling coverage over several years warns that the chance is rising even if the story has not yet said so.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net Debt / EBITDA measures how many years of earnings before depreciation it would take to repay the debt after subtracting cash. The chance the story ends depends on whether the debt can be repaid or refinanced. The ratio gives the size of that debt in years of earnings.

**What the combination tells you.** A low ratio supports a chance near zero, because the company could repay its debt quickly. A high ratio with large repayments due soon makes refinancing the deciding event of the story. A high ratio at a company with volatile earnings is the profile most likely to break in a downturn.

### DCF intrinsic value per share

**Why read the two together.** The intrinsic value per share in story mode is the value after the chance has been applied. The value if the story works is the figure before that adjustment. The gap between the two shows how much value the chance of failure removes.

**What the combination tells you.** A small gap means survival is not in doubt and the value rests on the operating story. A large gap means the survival question carries as much weight as the growth or the margin. When the proceeds for shareholders are zero, the value falls in direct proportion to the chance, so a small change in the chance moves the value noticeably.

## Part 8: Limitations

The chance is difficult to estimate. The rating-based anchor comes from the past record of rated bonds, and the standards behind credit grades can change over time. The anchor covers a fixed ten-year window, while the danger for many companies is concentrated in the next one or two years.

The forced-sale value is often a guess, since assets sold in a hurry rarely fetch their fair value and some assets cannot be sold apart from the business. The formula also treats failure as a single event, while real distress often unfolds in stages such as a restructuring that leaves shareholders with a small part of the company. A story whose cash flows already include bad outcomes can count the same risk twice if a large chance is added on top.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which specific events in the risk factors and legal proceedings notes could stop the company outright rather than only lower its profit?
2. How much debt falls due in each of the next five years, and could the company repay it from cash and operations if lenders refused to refinance?
3. If the company loses money, how many months does the cash on hand last at the current yearly loss?
4. What synthetic rating does the Credit Analysis section show, and has the rating moved down over recent years?
5. Could the company raise new money in a market crisis, or does its survival depend on new money arriving on time?
6. Which of the listed risks could be insured or hedged, and has the company done so?
7. What would the assets fetch in a forced sale, and would anything be left for shareholders after lenders are repaid?
8. Do the story's cash flows already include the bad outcomes, so that a large chance would count the same risk twice?
9. How much does the value per share fall between the value if the story works and the value after the chance is applied?
