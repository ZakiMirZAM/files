# Sales To Invested Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Sales to invested capital measures how much revenue a company produces from each dollar invested in its operations. Invested capital is the money supplied by lenders and shareholders and committed to running the business. The ratio divides one year of sales by that capital. The result shows how hard the committed money is working.

The ratio answers one question: for each dollar put into the business, how much revenue does the business generate? A ratio of 1.5 means every invested dollar produces a dollar and fifty cents of annual sales. A ratio of 0.5 means three dollars of capital must sit in the business to produce one dollar of revenue. A higher ratio means the company needs less capital to support its sales.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.5 | The business is capital heavy. A large amount of money must sit in the company to support its sales. |
| 0.5 to 1.0 | A moderate capital load. Common for manufacturers and asset-owning businesses. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | Capital is working hard. Each invested dollar supports more than its own value in sales. |
| Above 2.0 | The business is capital light. The company conducts heavy sales on a thin base of committed money. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Sales To Invested Capital = Revenue ÷ Invested Capital`

Revenue is the top number. Revenue is total sales for the year, the broadest measure of the commerce the business conducts. Sales sit on top because the ratio is testing how much business activity the committed capital supports.

Invested capital is the bottom number. Invested capital is total debt plus shareholders' equity, less cash and other assets not used in operations. Debt and equity are the two sources of the money put into the business. Cash and non-operating assets are removed because the ratio is asking what the operating capital produces, not what idle money does. Invested capital is used rather than total assets because invested capital is the money committed by choice, and measuring sales against committed money makes the ratio a test of how well that money is deployed.

This ratio is one of the two parts that produce the return on invested capital. The return on invested capital equals the operating profit margin multiplied by this ratio. A company can reach the same return through a high margin on slow capital or a thin margin on fast capital, and this ratio is what tells the two apart.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Capital efficiency is half of what creates value, and the half that gets less attention. Margins draw most of the focus, but a company that doubles the sales squeezed from each invested dollar doubles its return at the same margin. The lever is just as powerful and often cheaper to pull. Sales to invested capital isolates that lever.

The ratio also shows the capital cost of growth. The inverse of the ratio is how much capital each new dollar of sales requires. A company at 2.0 needs fifty cents of fresh capital for each new sales dollar. A company at 0.5 needs two dollars. That difference decides how much of a growth plan survives as cash rather than being swallowed by new investment.

For acquisitions and expansion plans the ratio is a first sanity check. The plan promises a certain amount of revenue from a certain amount of committed capital. The company's own history of this ratio is the base rate that the promise should be judged against.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The top number moves with the revenue drivers: volume, price, and product mix. Pricing deserves a flag, because inflation lifts sales against a capital base recorded at its older, lower cost. The ratio can climb without any real improvement. The bottom number moves with the capital cycle. New equipment spending builds the capital base ahead of the revenue it will eventually support, which depresses the ratio during an investment phase. Acquisitions load the base with goodwill, the premium paid above the value of the assets bought, so serial acquirers tend to run low ratios.

Working capital creep also moves the ratio. When receivables and inventory grow faster than sales, the capital base swells and the ratio falls. Impairments work the other way. An impairment writes down the capital base and lifts the ratio, but only by admitting that past spending lost value. Business model shifts move the ratio at its root. Outsourcing production pushes capital onto suppliers and raises the ratio, while vertical integration pulls capital back in and lowers it.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The level of the ratio is mostly a matter of business model. The amount of capital a model needs to conduct a dollar of sales varies widely by industry.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Retailers and distributors | Often above 2.0 | Heavy sales flow through a thin base of committed capital. |
| Manufacturers | Near 1.0 | Plants and equipment require a sizable but not extreme capital base. |
| Utilities and telecom | 0.3 to 0.6 | Enormous fixed networks support sales, so each capital dollar conducts little revenue. |
| Software with large acquisitions | Often modest | Goodwill from deals dominates invested capital, holding the measured ratio down. |
| Banks and insurers | Not applicable | Capital is the raw material of the business rather than support for sales. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when two business models need very different amounts of capital. Compare within the same model. When comparing two companies, split any difference in return on invested capital into a margin difference and a capital-efficiency difference, because the split shows where the real edge lives.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A rising trend means each committed dollar conducts more commerce. At a steady margin, the rise feeds straight through into a rising return on invested capital. A falling trend has two common stories. The first is an investment phase, where new spending has built the capital base but the revenue has not yet arrived. The second is efficiency decay, where capital is piling up in working capital and stalled projects. The capex and acquisition lines on the chart date the investment story and set the deadline by which the new spending must produce revenue.

A step drop with a slow recovery is the signature of an acquisition. The base jumps on the deal closing and the ratio recovers only as the bought revenue arrives, so the speed of recovery grades the deal. Watch the ratio next to the margin line. Rising capital efficiency with falling margins can be a deliberate move down-market, while the reverse can be a move up-market, and both can be sound. The quiet warning is a ratio that erodes with no investment story behind it, which means the balance sheet is silting up.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** Sales to invested capital is one of the two parts that produce the return on invested capital. The other part is the operating profit margin. The return equals the margin multiplied by this ratio, so the two metrics decompose the return into its sources.

**What the combination tells you.** A given return on invested capital can come from a high margin on slow-turning capital or a thin margin on fast-turning capital. Reading this ratio next to the return shows which engine the company runs. A return that holds steady while this ratio falls means the margin is rising to compensate, and watching both reveals whether an advantage is in pricing or in capital efficiency.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** The EBIT margin is the other part of the return on invested capital. EBIT margin measures profit per dollar of sales, and this ratio measures sales per dollar of capital. One is profitability and the other is capital efficiency.

**What the combination tells you.** A company strong on this ratio but weak on margin competes through volume and capital efficiency. A company strong on margin but weak on this ratio competes through pricing. Reading the pair shows whether a change in returns came from selling more cheaply or from charging more, which are very different strategies.

### EVA Margin

**Why read the two together.** The EVA margin measures the economic surplus per dollar of sales after charging the business for its capital. Sales to invested capital measures how much sales each capital dollar supports. Together they cover capital efficiency and value creation.

**What the combination tells you.** A high sales-to-invested-capital ratio with a positive EVA margin means the company turns capital into sales efficiently and earns a surplus on those sales. A low ratio with a thin EVA margin means the business is capital heavy and earns little above the cost of that capital. Reading both shows whether value comes from working capital hard or from pricing it well.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio rests on book figures that age poorly. Invested capital is recorded at historical cost, so an old asset base is carried at low values and makes the ratio look high. A company with new plants can show a low ratio simply because its assets are recorded at full, recent cost. Inflation widens this distortion, lifting sales against a capital base frozen at older prices. The ratio also treats all revenue as equally valuable, even though a dollar of low-margin sales counts the same as a dollar of high-margin sales.

The ratio says nothing about profitability on its own. A high ratio paired with a thin margin can still be a poor business. Goodwill from acquisitions inflates invested capital and depresses the ratio, so a serial acquirer can look capital heavy for accounting reasons rather than operating ones. During a transition such as a large build or a model shift, a single year misleads, and only the multi-year path carries a reliable signal. The ratio cannot be applied to banks and insurers, where capital is the product itself.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio produced by genuine capital efficiency, or by an old asset base carried at low book value?
2. How much of invested capital is goodwill from acquisitions? What is the ratio on tangible operating capital alone?
3. Is the ratio rising because of inflation lifting sales against an older capital base, or because of real operating gains?
4. Does a falling ratio reflect an investment phase that will produce revenue later, or capital silting up in working capital?
5. When did recent capex and acquisitions land on the chart, and has the promised revenue arrived yet?
6. How does this ratio compare with direct competitors in the same business model?
7. Is the company strong on this ratio but weak on margin, or the reverse? Where does its competitive edge actually sit?
8. Are receivables and inventory growing faster than sales, quietly eroding the ratio from inside?
9. Has a business model shift, such as outsourcing or integration, changed the ratio's normal level?
10. What revenue does a current expansion plan promise from its committed capital, and does that match the company's own history of this ratio?
