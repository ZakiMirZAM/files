# Fee-Based Financials

## What it is

Some companies carry the Financial Services label without running a lending business. Payment networks move money between banks and earn a fee on each transaction. Rating and data firms sell credit ratings, indexes, and financial data. Exchanges earn fees for running markets and clearing trades. These companies are fee-based financials. Their revenue comes from fees, not from the gap between the interest they earn and the interest they pay.

The app values fee-based financials the way it values any operating company, with a discounted cash flow to the firm. A discounted cash flow to the firm values the whole business as today's worth of the cash its operations will produce, before any payment to lenders or shareholders. Banks, card lenders, insurers, and other lenders keep the equity models: the dividend discount model, the cash flow to equity model, and residual income.

## The rule the app applies

| Test | Requirement for the firm model |
|---|---|
| Sector | Financial Services |
| Industry | Credit Services, or Data & Stock Exchanges |
| Funding cost | Interest expense under 10 percent of revenue. Exchanges and data firms may report none; a Credit Services company must report some |
| Loan book (Credit Services only) | Receivables under 30 percent of total assets |

A company must pass every test. The data books a lender's loan interest inside revenue, so the app reads the cost of the money a lender borrows and the loans it holds instead. A Credit Services company that pays 10 percent or more of its revenue in interest, or whose receivables are 30 percent or more of its assets, is a lender and stays on the equity models. Card issuers, consumer lenders and lessors fall in this group, and so does a payments company that holds large customer balances and loans. Every other Financial Services or Real Estate company, and any industry naming banks, insurance, mortgages, capital markets, or REITs, also stays on the equity models.

## Why the line sits here

For a bank, borrowed money is the raw material of the business. A bank borrows from depositors and lends at a higher rate, so its debt is an operating input rather than a source of financing. Separating the firm's cash flow from the cash paid to lenders makes no sense when lending is the product. Regulators also set how much capital a bank must hold, so the bank's reinvestment follows a capital rule rather than an investment plan. The equity models value what shareholders receive and avoid both problems.

A payment network or a ratings firm works the other way. Its debt is ordinary financing. Its reinvestment is spending on technology, people, and acquisitions. Classification systems moved several of these companies into the Financial sector in 2023, but the businesses themselves did not change. Valuing them on dividends alone ignored the buybacks that return most of their cash.

The interest test separates the two kinds of company inside the same industry. A network that earns almost all of its revenue from fees passes. A card company that earns much of its revenue from loan interest is a lender in practice and fails.

## How this section works

When a company passes the test, the valuation section shows the full firm model: the cost of capital, the growth path, the terminal value, and the market-implied growth. Every input follows the same rules as any other operating company. Growth comes from the organic revenue trend (definition 820). The return on capital is the valuation return, equity plus debt minus cash (definition 824).

When a company fails the test, the section shows the equity models, and the firm model is marked not applicable.

## Common traps

The first trap is cash that is not the company's own. Payment firms can hold customer funds waiting to be paid out, and exchanges hold margin deposits posted by traders. Those balances can sit inside cash and inside liabilities. Subtracting them as if they were free cash overstates the return on capital and the value of the shares. Read the balance sheet notes before trusting the cash figure.

The second trap is growth bought through acquisitions. Exchanges and data firms grow heavily by buying other companies. The organic trend removes revenue bought with cash, but deals paid in shares can stay inside the trend.

The third trap is the screen itself. A company with a small lending arm can pass both tests while still carrying credit risk, and a Credit Services company that reports no interest expense at all is kept on the equity models because the figure cannot be checked. Check how the company actually earns its revenue before relying on either model.
