# Current Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The current ratio measures whether a company can pay the bills coming due within the next year using the assets it can turn into cash within that same year. Current assets are the resources expected to become cash within a year. Examples include cash, money owed by customers, and inventory. Current liabilities are the obligations due within a year, such as money owed to suppliers and the part of a loan due this year. Liquidity is the ability to meet near-term bills on time. The ratio answers one question: can the company cover its short-term bills with its short-term resources?

A ratio of 2 means the company holds two dollars of current assets for every dollar of current liabilities. A ratio of 1 means current assets and current liabilities are equal. A ratio below 1 means the next year's bills are larger than the resources on hand to pay them. The company below 1 must then raise new financing, sell assets, or rely on incoming cash flow to close the gap.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | Current bills exceed current resources. The company depends on incoming cash flow or new financing to pay on time. |
| 1.0 to 1.5 | The company can cover current bills but with little spare room. Workable when cash flow is steady. |
| 1.5 to 2.0 | A traditional comfort zone for companies that hold inventory. This app's quality score awards a pass at 1.50 or higher. |
| 2.0 to 3.0 | A wide cushion. Safe, though very high levels can mean cash and inventory are sitting idle. |
| Above 3.0 | Often more current assets than the business needs. The useful question becomes why the company holds so much. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities`

Current assets are the top number. Current assets are the resources a company expects to convert to cash within one year. The main items are cash and short-term investments, money owed by customers (accounts receivable), and inventory. Prepaid items also count. Current assets belong on top because they are the pool of resources available to settle near-term bills. The ratio counts only the one-year resources and ignores long-term assets such as factories and equipment.

Current liabilities are the bottom number. Current liabilities are the obligations a company must settle within one year. The main items are money owed to suppliers (accounts payable), short-term borrowings, and the portion of long-term debt due this year. Unpaid wages, unpaid taxes, and deferred revenue also count. Deferred revenue is cash already collected for goods or services not yet delivered. Current liabilities belong on the bottom because they are the near-term claims that must be paid first. The ratio matches one-year resources against one-year claims and sets aside everything longer-dated on both sides.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Liquidity failures, not profitability failures, are what kill companies quickly. A business can survive years of weak earnings, but a missed payroll or a defaulted payment can end the business in weeks. The current ratio is the fastest first test of how much room sits between the company and that kind of event.

The ratio also prices a hidden risk for shareholders. A company with thin liquidity may be forced to raise money at the worst possible time. Raising money that way can mean issuing new shares cheaply or pledging assets, both of which hurt existing shareholders. A healthy ratio lowers that risk.

The check costs only a moment and rules out a whole category of trouble. This app's quality score awards Current Ratio a pass at 1.50 or higher, a traditional comfort level for companies that carry inventory. The threshold is a screening shortcut, not a precise line, and it suits some sectors better than others.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The ratio moves with working capital decisions and a few financing decisions. Building inventory or letting customer payments stretch out raises current assets, which lifts the ratio. A higher ratio from those two sources can hide weakening discipline, because the goods are not selling and the customers are not paying. Drawing down cash to repay long-term debt lowers current assets and can pull the ratio down while genuinely strengthening the balance sheet. The direction of the change matters less than the reason behind it.

On the bottom side, a long-term loan moving into the current portion as it nears its due date can swing the ratio with no new borrowing at all. Deferred revenue swells current liabilities in subscription businesses, even though that obligation is paid off with future service rather than cash. Seasonality moves the ratio too, since a retailer measured before the holidays looks very different from the same retailer measured after. Always identify which line item moved before judging whether a change is good or bad.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Comfortable levels depend on how predictable a sector's cash cycle is. Steadier and faster cash collection makes a thinner cushion safe. A ratio far above the sector norm deserves as much scrutiny as one below it, since idle current assets earn little.

| Industry type | Typical range or behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturers and distributors | 1.5 to 2.0 or higher | These companies hold inventory and wait on customer payments, so a larger cushion is expected. |
| Retailers and grocers | Often below 1.0 | These companies collect cash from customers today and pay suppliers weeks later, so suppliers fund the business. |
| Utilities | Around 1.0 or below | Revenue is steady and near certain, so a thin cushion is safe on purpose. |
| Software and subscription businesses | Modest, near 1.0 can be healthy | Deferred revenue inflates current liabilities, yet that obligation costs little cash to settle. |
| Banks | Not applicable | The concept does not map to a bank's balance sheet, where short-term items are the core business. |

Always read the ratio against the sector's normal cash rhythm and the company's own history. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two companies collect and pay on different cash cycles, such as a grocer measured against a heavy manufacturer.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A stable current ratio across the chart, whatever its level, points to a settled and managed cash cycle. A steadily rising ratio is ambiguous and needs a closer look. Rising cash signals strength, while rising inventory or unpaid customer balances signal the opposite. The same upward line can mean health or quiet decay, so the trend must be split into its parts.

A steady decline toward and below 1.0 is the classic pattern before distress, especially when the decline speeds up. Suppliers and lenders watch this number, and they tighten terms as the ratio falls, which pushes it down further. A single-year drop is often just a maturing loan rolling into current liabilities. The single-year drop matters only when refinancing is in doubt. Read the trend next to the balance sheet common-size analysis. The common-size view shows which current asset and liability lines are doing the moving as a share of total assets. Base effects can fake a trend when one unusually high or low starting year distorts every later comparison.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Quick Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio counts all current assets, including inventory. The quick ratio runs the same test with inventory stripped out. The quick ratio keeps cash, short-term investments, and money owed by customers. Inventory is the slowest current asset to turn into cash and the one most likely to be sold below its recorded value. Comparing the two ratios isolates how much of the liquidity depends on selling goods.

**What the combination tells you.** A small gap between the two ratios means liquidity rests on cash and customer payments, which are more reliable. A wide gap means liquidity leans on inventory, the least dependable current asset. When the gap widens over time, the company is building stock that has not yet sold. Reading the pair shows whether a healthy current ratio is genuine or propped up by goods on the shelf.

### Operating Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio is a snapshot of one balance sheet date. Operating cash flow is the cash the business actually generates over a full period. Operating cash flow is the force that refills current assets as bills are paid. A snapshot and a flow answer different questions, and both are needed for a full liquidity view.

**What the combination tells you.** Strong operating cash flow can excuse a thin current ratio, because fresh cash keeps arriving to cover bills. Weak operating cash flow makes even a comfortable current ratio temporary, because the cushion drains and nothing replaces it. A high ratio paired with cash burn is a warning that the snapshot is flattering. Reading the pair separates a durable liquidity position from a fragile one.

### Cash Conversion Cycle

**Why read the two together.** The cash conversion cycle measures how many days the company waits between paying for inventory and collecting cash from customers. The current ratio measures the size of the near-term cushion at one moment. The speed of the cash loop explains why a sector's normal ratio is high or low. A fast loop lets a company run a thin ratio safely.

**What the combination tells you.** A short cash conversion cycle paired with a low current ratio is usually efficiency, not danger, because cash returns quickly. A long cash conversion cycle paired with the same low ratio is riskier, because cash is tied up for longer. Watching both over time shows whether the working capital loop is tightening or loosening. The pair explains whether a given ratio is comfortable for this specific business.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio tests only near-term bills due within a year. Net Debt / EBITDA measures the total debt load in years of earnings. Near-term liquidity and total debt size are separate questions. A company can look liquid this year while carrying a heavy long-term debt load.

**What the combination tells you.** A healthy current ratio with a low debt load points to genuinely conservative finances. A healthy current ratio with a heavy debt load means the near-term picture is calm while a larger problem sits further out. A weak current ratio with a low debt load points to a timing squeeze rather than deep trouble. Reading the pair separates the near-term cash question from the size of the overall debt.

## Part 8: Limitations

The current ratio is a snapshot of a single balance sheet date. The snapshot can be shaped by timing payments and collections around the reporting date, a practice known as window dressing, and the picture can be stale a month later. The ratio treats all current assets as equally good. In reality inventory may not sell and customer balances may not be collected, while only cash is certain. The quick ratio exists to strip out the weakest layer.

The ratio ignores the timing inside the year. A bill due next week and an asset converting in eleven months are netted against each other as if they happen at the same moment. The ratio also says nothing about cash flow, the force that actually refills current assets, so a high ratio can sit beside heavy cash burn. Because healthy levels differ by business model, the single 1.50 threshold flags efficient supplier-financed companies as risky and treats bloated companies as safe. The current ratio is a screening test, and only that.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which parts produced the current ratio's present level? How much of the top number is cash versus inventory and customer balances, and how much of the bottom number is debt versus supplier bills and deferred revenue?
2. If the ratio moved over the last few years, exactly which line item moved it? Does the balance sheet common-size analysis show that line growing as a share of total assets?
3. How much long-term debt rolls into the current portion over the next two years? Is the refinancing path, meaning the due dates and available credit lines, actually secure?
4. Is the inventory in the top number genuinely sellable at its recorded value? Are the customer balances concentrated in a few customers whose own finances are sound?
5. What does the ratio look like at other points in the company's seasonal cycle? Is the reporting date the flattering moment or the unflattering one?
6. Does the company hold undrawn credit lines that make the snapshot understate true liquidity? What conditions must be met to draw on those lines?
7. If revenue fell sharply for two quarters, would operating cash flow plus current assets cover current liabilities without emergency financing?
8. Is a low ratio here the supplier-financed efficiency of a strong operator? What payment terms do its suppliers grant compared with peers?
9. Is a high ratio here idle capital? What specific reason does management give for holding this much working capital rather than returning or reinvesting it?
10. Have suppliers or lenders changed this company's terms recently? Would the first sign show up as supplier bills shrinking while the ratio appears to improve?
