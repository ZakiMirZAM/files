# Depreciation And Amortization

## Part 1: Definition

Depreciation and amortization is the income statement's charge for assets wearing out over time. Depreciation spreads the cost of a physical asset across the years the asset is used. A physical asset is something the company can touch, such as a machine, a vehicle, or a building. Amortization does the same for an intangible asset. An intangible asset has no physical form, such as a patent, acquired technology, or a customer list. The line answers one question: how much of past investment is being charged against this year's profit?

The cash for these assets usually left the company years ago, when the assets were bought. The depreciation and amortization line does not move cash this year. The line takes the old purchase cost and assigns a slice of that cost to each year the asset helps the business. In the vertical analysis table the line shows as a percent of revenue. That percent reads as the weight of past spending on equipment and intangibles arriving in the present.

| Level as a percent of revenue | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 3 percent | The business owns few long-lived assets. The model is light on equipment. |
| 3 to 8 percent | A normal range for many manufacturers and retailers. |
| 8 to 15 percent | A heavy asset base. The business spends a lot to build and keep its plant. |
| Above 15 percent | Very capital intensive, common in telecom, utilities, and heavy industry. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The line is the sum of two charges:

`Depreciation And Amortization = Depreciation + Amortization`

Depreciation is the first part. Depreciation takes the cost of a physical asset and divides that cost across the asset's useful life. The useful life is the number of years the company expects to use the asset. A machine that cost a known amount and lasts ten years gives one tenth of its cost as depreciation each year under the simplest method. The charge belongs on the income statement because the asset helps earn revenue in every one of those years.

Amortization is the second part. Amortization spreads the cost of an intangible asset across its useful life in the same way. Acquired technology, capitalized software, and purchased customer lists are common examples. The charge belongs on the income statement for the same reason as depreciation. The asset was paid for once but serves the business across many years, so the cost is matched to those years.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The line shows how much of the company's profit is consumed by the slow using-up of its assets. A business that needs heavy plant to operate will carry a large charge here every year. A business that needs little equipment will carry almost nothing. The size of the line tells the investor how asset-heavy the model is.

The line also separates accounting profit from cash. Depreciation and amortization reduce reported profit but do not move cash in the year they are recorded. An investor who wants to judge cash generation must add the line back to profit. The line is one of the most watched non-cash items on the statement for that reason.

The charge sits above operating income, so a high charge pulls operating income down. A reader who compares two companies must know how much of the gap between them comes from this one line rather than from the real strength of the operations.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The size of the asset base drives the line. A company that has spent heavily on plant and equipment will report a large charge for years afterward. A recent acquisition raises the line, because purchased intangibles such as customer lists and technology begin to amortize. A wave of capital spending today becomes a wave of depreciation tomorrow.

Accounting choices move the line as well. The company chooses the useful life and the method used to spread the cost. A longer useful life spreads the same cost across more years, which lowers the yearly charge and raises reported profit. A shorter life does the opposite. Asset write-downs, where the company cuts the recorded value of an asset, can also change the charge in later years. A reader should check whether a falling charge reflects a real drop in spending or only a stretched useful life.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a high charge depends entirely on how much plant the business needs to run.

| Industry type | Typical behavior of the line | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and telecom | Large and steady | The business runs on networks and plant that must be built and replaced. |
| Heavy industry and miners | Large | Factories, machines, and mines carry large costs spread over many years. |
| Software and asset-light services | Small | Few physical assets, though acquired software can lift amortization. |
| Acquisitive companies | Lifted by amortization | Purchased intangibles add a large amortization charge after each deal. |
| Banks | Small and not central | Earnings come from lending, not from using physical plant. |

A cross-sector comparison of this line is meaningless when the two businesses need different amounts of plant to operate. Compare the line only against companies with a similar asset base.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the line as a share of revenue across the multi-year chart, not only as a raw number. A steady percent means the asset base is growing in step with the business. A steady rise in the percent means the company is becoming more capital intensive, often after a building program. A steady fall in the percent can mean two very different things. The fall can mean the company is growing revenue faster than its asset base, which is healthy. The fall can also mean the company has stretched useful lives or stopped reinvesting, which is a warning.

A step change deserves a direct check. A sudden jump usually follows an acquisition that added intangibles, or a large block of new plant coming into service. A sudden drop can follow an asset write-down or the end of an amortization schedule, when a purchased intangible finishes being charged off. Compare the line with capital spending over the same years. When depreciation runs far above capital spending year after year, the company is using up its plant faster than it is replacing the plant, and reported profit is flattered today at the cost of future spending.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Capex / Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Depreciation measures how fast the existing asset base is being used up. Capex, short for capital expenditures, measures how much the company spends on new equipment and buildings. Dividing one by the other shows whether the company is replacing its assets as fast as the assets wear out. The depreciation line is the bottom of that ratio.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio near one means the company spends about enough to replace what wears out. A ratio well above one means the company is expanding its asset base. A ratio below one for several years means the company is under-investing, and the asset base is shrinking even though reported profit may look fine. Reading the two together shows whether today's profit rests on real maintenance of the plant or on letting the plant run down.

### EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** EBITDA is operating income with depreciation and amortization added back. The depreciation and amortization line is the exact amount that gets added back. A large charge creates a wide gap between operating income and EBITDA. A small charge leaves the two figures close together.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide gap means the business is asset-heavy, and EBITDA hides a real cost that the company must keep funding. A narrow gap means the business is asset-light, and EBITDA is close to true operating profit. Watching the two lines over time shows whether a rising EBITDA is real growth or just a larger add-back from a growing asset base.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the cash left after the company pays for the equipment and buildings it needs. Depreciation is a non-cash charge, so it is added back when cash flow is built from profit. The same assets that create the depreciation charge also create the capital spending that free cash flow subtracts.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with high depreciation and low capital spending will show strong free cash flow today, because the old assets are being charged off while little new cash goes out. The gap can flatter cash flow for a while. When capital spending catches up to depreciation, free cash flow falls. Reading the two together shows whether strong cash flow is durable or borrowed from under-investment.

## Part 8: Limitations

The line is an estimate, not a measured cost. The company chooses the useful life and the method, so two firms with identical assets can report different charges. A reader cannot tell from the line alone whether the assets are genuinely wearing out at the rate shown. Stretched useful lives raise reported profit while leaving the real wear unrecorded.

The line also mixes two different things. Depreciation reflects physical assets that the company usually must replace. Amortization often reflects intangibles from past acquisitions that the company will not buy again. Adding them together can overstate the spending the business truly needs to repeat. The line tells the reader how much past cost is being charged this year. The line does not tell the reader how much the company must spend next year to stay in business.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current charge driven by physical depreciation, by amortization of acquired intangibles, or by both? How much of the line will disappear once the acquisition intangibles finish amortizing?
2. How does the charge compare with capital spending over the last five years? Is the company replacing its assets as fast as the assets wear out?
3. Have the useful lives the company uses changed recently? Did a longer life lift reported profit without any real change in the business?
4. Has the company taken any asset write-downs on the chart? What did each write-down do to the charge in the years after?
5. Did a recent acquisition add a large block of amortization? What does operating profit look like before that purchased-intangible charge?
6. Is the line steady as a share of revenue, or is the share falling because reinvestment has slowed?
7. How does the charge compare with direct competitors that run a similar asset base? Is this company spreading costs over a longer life than its peers?
8. What does free cash flow look like once capital spending catches up to the depreciation charge?
9. Are any costs being capitalized into assets now that will become depreciation later? How large is that future charge likely to be?
10. Does the trend in this line match the company's stated investment plans, or is the asset base quietly shrinking?
