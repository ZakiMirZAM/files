# Total Non Current Assets

## Part 1: Definition

Total non-current assets is the sum of every long-held asset on the balance sheet. Non-current means an asset the company does not expect to turn into cash within one year. The total adds together the physical base such as property, plant, and equipment, the soft layer of goodwill and intangible assets, the patient portfolio of long-term investments, the deferred tax assets, and the catch-all of other non-current assets. The total answers one question: how much of the company's asset base is committed to the long term rather than being held as working, short-term assets?

In the app's vertical analysis the row is shown as a percent of total assets. That percent is the exact complement of the current-asset share, because the two sides always add to one hundred percent. Current assets are the short-term assets expected to turn into cash within a year. A high non-current share describes a company whose value is locked into long-lived assets, while a low share describes a company that runs mostly on fast-cycling working assets. The same share can describe very different companies, so the composition behind the total is where the meaning lives.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The row sums the long-held asset lines:

`Total Non Current Assets = Property Plant And Equipment + Goodwill And Intangible Assets + Long Term Investments + Tax Assets + Other Non Current Assets`

Property, plant, and equipment is the physical base of factories, equipment, and buildings. Goodwill and intangible assets is the soft layer created mostly by acquisitions. Long term investments is the portfolio of stakes and securities the company holds as an investor. Tax assets is the stock of future tax savings. Other non-current assets is the catch-all of long-held items that fit no named line.

The share of total assets shifts as the company builds or lightens its long-lived base. A rising share means the company is committing more capital to long-lived assets, often by building plant or by paying premiums for acquisitions. A falling share means the company is lightening, by selling assets or by letting working assets grow faster. Each component carries its own analysis, and this row carries the total of the committed structure.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The total gives a structural reading of the company. The non-current share, read against its current-asset complement, tells you how capital-intensive the business is. A capital-intensive business locks most of its assets into long-lived form, while a capital-light business keeps most assets working and short-term.

The composition then splits the commitment into its kinds. A high non-current share built on physical plant describes a company whose value rests on a hard-built franchise. The same share built on goodwill describes a company whose value rests on acquisitions. The same share built on long-term investments describes a holding structure. Each kind is graded by the returns it earns, so the total alone is only the starting point.

The trend reads the direction of strategy. A rising share marks an era of building or buying, and a falling share marks an era of lightening, so the path shows where management is steering the asset base.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The components drive the total, and a move in any one moves the aggregate. Capital spending and depreciation move the physical base. Acquisitions and impairments move the soft layer. Purchases and sales of holdings move the investment portfolio. Each change belongs to whichever component shifted.

The share form also reflects the current side. Because the non-current share and the current share always sum to one hundred percent, a surge in current assets such as cash or inventory lowers the non-current share even when no long-lived asset changed. Reading the share therefore requires checking whether the long-lived side moved or the short-term side did.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The non-current share maps how capital-intensive an industry is.

| Industry type | Typical non-current share | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities, telecom, heavy infrastructure | Often above 70 percent | Networks and plant lock up most of the asset base. |
| Manufacturers and industrials | Moderate to high | Factories and equipment are large long-lived assets. |
| Serial acquirers | Elevated | Goodwill and intangibles swell the long-lived side. |
| Holding companies | Elevated | Long-term investments dominate the asset base. |
| Retail and distribution | Lower | Inventory and receivables keep the working side large. |
| Capital-light software and services | Low | Few long-lived assets, so the current side leads. |

A cross-sector comparison of the share is meaningless when the businesses differ in capital intensity, and even within one share the composition can differ. Compare a company against peers with the same business model, and read the mix of components before judging the share.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows this row as a percent of total assets across several years. A rising share means the company is committing more capital to long-lived assets. The component lines tell you whether the commitment is physical plant, acquired goodwill, or investment stakes. A falling share means the company is lightening, through asset sales, lease conversions, or faster growth in working capital.

Always trace a move back to its component. A jump in the share that comes from new factories has a very different meaning from a jump that comes from acquisition premiums. The first builds a hard franchise, while the second depends on the deals performing. Read the trend against the returns the company earns on its assets too, because a rising commitment is only justified when the returns hold up. A long-lived base that keeps growing while returns fall is a sign that capital is being committed without earning its keep.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Total Current Assets

**Why read the two together.** Total current assets is the short-term side of the balance sheet, and total non-current assets is the long-term side. The two shares always sum to one hundred percent, so they are exact complements. Reading them together shows the full split of the asset base between committed and working capital.

**What the combination tells you.** A high non-current share with a thin current share describes a capital-intensive business whose value is locked into long-lived assets. A low non-current share with a large current share describes a working-capital-driven business that turns its assets over quickly. Watching the split over time shows whether the company is becoming more asset-heavy or more asset-light.

### Asset Turnover

**Why read the two together.** Asset turnover measures how much revenue the company generates for each dollar of assets. Total non-current assets is a large part of the asset base that turnover is measured against. The pairing tests how productively the committed structure is being used.

**What the combination tells you.** A large non-current base with low asset turnover means the company has committed heavy capital that generates relatively little revenue, which pressures returns. A leaner base with high turnover means the assets work hard. Watching the pair shows whether a growing long-lived base is being matched by enough revenue to justify the commitment.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC measures the return earned on the total capital invested in the business, and total non-current assets is where most of that capital is committed. The pairing tests whether the long-lived base is earning an adequate return.

**What the combination tells you.** A growing non-current base combined with a healthy ROIC means the commitment is paying off and capital is well placed. A growing base combined with a falling ROIC means the company is committing capital that does not earn its cost. Comparing the two over time shows whether each wave of building or buying is creating value or destroying it.

## Part 8: Limitations

The total conceals the very mix that gives it meaning, so the components must be read before the aggregate can be judged. A factory, a pile of acquisition goodwill, and a portfolio of stakes can all produce the same non-current share with completely different risk and return.

The share also imports the motion of the current side, because the two complements always sum to one hundred. A change in the non-current share can come entirely from the short-term side moving. The row finally carries assets at a mix of historical cost and acquisition premiums, so the gaps between carrying value and real worth accumulate here in full. The total is the committed side of the balance sheet, and it is read properly only through its composition and graded by the returns the components earn.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is the path of the non-current share, and which components drove each move?
2. How does the commitment split between physical assets, soft assets, and financial holdings? How does that split compare with the mix the sector usually shows?
3. What did each building or buying era construct or acquire, and what do those components earn now?
4. What revenue does the whole structure generate, judged by asset turnover and the return metrics?
5. How does the asset architecture compare against direct peers with the same business model?
6. What gaps between carrying value and real worth are accumulating, by component?
7. What would lightening the base look like, and which components could be converted to cash?
8. How did the worst point in the business cycle treat the committed side, through impairments and idle capacity?
9. Does the current non-current share match the strategy management has stated?
10. What asset structure does the company's valuation assume the business needs, and is the trend building toward it or past it?
