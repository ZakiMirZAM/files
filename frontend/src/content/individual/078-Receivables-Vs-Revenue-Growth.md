# Receivables Vs Revenue Growth

## Part 1: Definition

This check compares two growth rates that should move together. The first is the growth of accounts receivable. Accounts receivable is the money customers owe the company for sales already billed but not yet paid in cash. The second is the growth of revenue, which is the total value of sales the company recorded for the year. The check answers one question: is the unpaid sales balance growing at the same speed as sales themselves?

In a normal business the two rise in proportion. More sales create more unpaid invoices, so receivables track revenue closely. The check passes when receivables grow in line with revenue. The check flags when receivables grow much faster, because that gap means each new sales dollar is being collected more slowly than the old ones, or not at all.

| Pattern | What that pattern usually means |
|---|---|
| Receivables and revenue grow at about the same rate | Sales are converting to cash on the usual terms. Ordinary. |
| Receivables grow somewhat faster than revenue | Collection is slowing or terms are loosening. Worth a closer look. |
| Receivables grow far faster than revenue for one year | Often a single large late-year contract, an acquisition, or a customer mix shift. Check the cause. |
| Receivables outrun revenue for several years in a row | A drift in one direction. The forensic literature treats this as a leading sign of low-quality or pulled-forward revenue. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The check computes two growth rates and compares them:

`Divergence = Receivables Growth − Revenue Growth (flag when clearly positive)`

Receivables growth is the year-over-year percentage change in the accounts receivable balance. The balance is taken from the balance sheet, which is the statement listing what the company owns and owes at one date. Receivables belong in the comparison because they are the direct record of sales that have not yet turned into cash.

Revenue growth is the year-over-year percentage change in revenue from the income statement. Revenue is the natural yardstick because receivables are supposed to scale with sales. When the two rates separate, the sales that created the extra receivables are not collecting the way real sales normally do.

This comparison is the matched-pair form of days sales outstanding. Days sales outstanding measures the average number of days a sale waits as an unpaid invoice before cash arrives. Receivables growing faster than revenue is the same fact as days sales outstanding lengthening. The days sales outstanding entry carries the deeper tools, such as the aging schedule that sorts unpaid invoices by how overdue they are.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The comparison is the earliest honest signal about revenue quality the statements contain. The forensic accounting literature on financial manipulation puts recording revenue too soon at the top of its list of tricks. Every version of that trick leaves the same residue. Revenue is booked today, and the cash arrives late or never, so the unpaid amount piles up in receivables faster than revenue can explain.

The check also catches problems that are not fraud. Demand can soften while the sales team pushes product out the door. Customers can weaken while payment terms quietly stretch to keep orders flowing. Each is a real business problem the income statement will confess only later. The receivables line confesses first.

The appeal for an investor is the low cost of the test. The comparison uses two numbers already on the statements. A few seconds of attention can flag the kind of revenue problem that later forces a restatement, which is a formal correction of past financial reports.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The manipulation causes are the revenue-recognition family. Sales can be pulled forward from future quarters. A company can stuff the channel, meaning it ships more product to distributors than they can resell, borrowing next period's revenue into this one. Payment terms can be extended as a hidden discount to drag sales forward. Bill-and-hold and contingent deals can book revenue on sales whose cash may never come.

Several innocent causes raise receivables growth for honest reasons. A shift toward slower-paying buyers lifts receivables legitimately and should show up in segment data. Government, enterprise, and international customers all tend to pay more slowly than the average. An acquisition imports a whole receivables book at once and breaks the year-over-year comparison until the figures are restated on a like-for-like basis. A single large contract signed near year-end distorts one period and resolves the next.

One artifact cuts the other way and hides a real problem. Factoring and securitization both mean selling receivables to a third party for early cash. Selling receivables shrinks the reported balance. A company doing this can suppress its receivables growth and mask the very divergence this check hunts for, so the absence of a flag means less at a company that sells its receivables.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The check matters most where customers pay on credit and payment terms can be negotiated. The check carries little weight where sales are paid in cash or by card at the point of sale.

| Industry type | How the check behaves | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise technology, industrials, contractors, healthcare | Full weight | Collection cycles are long and terms are a lever management can pull, so the divergence is meaningful. |
| Consumer retail, restaurants | Weak signal | Sales are paid at the register, so the receivables base is too small for the comparison to say much. |
| Distributor-sold goods such as hardware, pharma, packaged goods | Sharpest reading | Channel stuffing is the characteristic trick here, so sell-in versus sell-through is the question behind any divergence. |
| Fast-growing companies | Strained | Receivables from late-year sales sit against revenue earned across the whole year, so judge growth against the sales that generated it. |

Compare a company only against peers in the same sector. A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two industries collect cash on different terms, because the normal relationship between receivables and revenue differs by business model.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows this check across several years. The reading is about direction, not any single year. A single flagged year with a stated, checkable cause, such as a large contract or an acquisition, is ordinary business.

The pattern that matters is persistence. Receivables outrunning revenue for several consecutive years is a relationship drifting one way, and timing noise does not drift. The most serious configuration is that drift compounding while reported revenue growth accelerates. Growth made increasingly of unpaid invoices, sold to investors as a strong growth story, is the signature the forensic literature flags hardest.

Read this check beside two other charts. The days sales outstanding chart shows the same fact measured in days. The income quality trend shows whether cash is arriving for the reported profit. The resolution years complete the record. Divergences that ended in write-offs or restatements, set against those that simply collected out, give the company its own base rate for how its flags tend to resolve.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Sales Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days sales outstanding measures the same fact as this check but expresses it as a number of days rather than a growth gap. Receivables growing faster than revenue is identical to days sales outstanding rising. The days sales outstanding entry carries the diagnostic tools this check only summarizes, including the aging schedule and the questions about customer mix. Reading the two together converts a flag into a direction.

**What the combination tells you.** When this check flags and days sales outstanding is also climbing, the divergence is confirmed and the aging schedule shows where the unpaid balance is building. When this check flags but days sales outstanding is flat, an acquisition or a definitional change is the likely cause rather than slowing collection. The pair separates a genuine collection problem from a measurement artifact. Together they point to exactly which invoices to examine.

### Income Quality (CFO / NI)

**Why read the two together.** Income quality compares cash flow from operations against net income, testing whether reported profit is backed by cash. Receivables growing faster than revenue is one of the main reasons cash lags profit. Booked sales that have not been collected raise profit without raising cash. The two metrics describe the same gap from the income side and the cash side.

**What the combination tells you.** A receivables divergence combined with weak income quality is the double signature of low-quality revenue. Profit is rising, cash is not following, and the missing cash is sitting in uncollected receivables. When receivables diverge but income quality stays strong, the cash is arriving despite the higher balance, which softens the concern. When both deteriorate together, the revenue itself is in doubt.

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Revenue is the denominator of this whole comparison, so its own trend changes how a flag should be read. A fast-growing company strains the proportionality test because late-year sales sit against a full year of revenue. A flag against accelerating revenue means something different from a flag against flat revenue. Reading the revenue trend frames the divergence.

**What the combination tells you.** Receivables divergence alongside slowing revenue suggests terms are being stretched to prop up a fading top line. Receivables divergence alongside accelerating revenue is the more dangerous combination, because it can mean the growth itself is increasingly made of uncollected sales. Stable revenue with a sudden receivables jump points to a one-off event to identify. The revenue trend tells you whether to worry about the growth or about a single period.

### Sloan Accruals

**Why read the two together.** The Sloan accruals ratio measures the share of earnings that comes from accounting accruals rather than cash, scaled against total assets. A rising receivables balance is one of the largest accruals on the balance sheet. This check sees the receivables build directly, while the accruals ratio sees it as part of the total non-cash earnings mass. The two view the same buildup at different zoom levels.

**What the combination tells you.** When receivables diverge and the accruals ratio is high, the receivables build is large enough relative to assets to weigh on future earnings, because research links high accruals to weaker later profit. When receivables diverge but the accruals ratio stays low, the build is small in the context of the whole balance sheet. The pair tells you whether the receivables problem is big enough to matter for the bottom line.

## Part 8: Limitations

The check compares aggregate totals, so it misses divergences hidden inside a favorable mix and flags ones that a mix shift fully explains. A segment-level version is the sharper tool when the data exists. Factoring and securitization defeat the check silently by suppressing reported receivables, so the footnotes must be read at any company that monetizes its receivables. Acquisitions break the comparison until the figures are restated on a like-for-like basis.

The check says nothing directly about whether the receivables will ever be collected. Questions about aging and bad-debt reserves live in the days sales outstanding tools. A clean result certifies only the relationship tested, not the honesty of the revenue. Sales to a related party that always pays on time can pass the screen while still being low quality, because the related party is funded to pay. The check is the first question about revenue quality, asked automatically. The entries around it are the investigation.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How many consecutive years has receivables growth exceeded revenue growth, and is the gap widening, holding steady, or closing?
2. What explanation does management give for the divergence, and does the segment and footnote evidence actually confirm it? Is the stated cause a mix shift, a geographic change, an acquisition, or contract timing?
3. Does the days sales outstanding trend confirm the same divergence, and what does the aging schedule show about where the unpaid balance is building?
4. Does the company sell through distributors, and what does sell-through evidence show against the sell-in revenue being booked?
5. Have payment terms changed recently, and is the sales team buying revenue with longer terms instead of price?
6. Does income quality confirm or contradict the revenue, meaning is the cash arriving for the sales being booked?
7. Has factoring or securitization started or grown, and would this check be flagging if the sold receivables were counted back in?
8. Where does the divergence sit against the incentive calendar of guidance targets, bonus thresholds, and loan-condition tests? Did the divergence begin when that pressure began?
9. Are bad-debt reserves growing along with the receivables and their aging, or is under-reserving adding to the revenue question?
10. If the divergent portion of receivables never collects, what reduction in revenue and earnings would that imply, and what do the valuation models show with the corrected numbers?
