# Inventory

## Part 1: Definition

Inventory is the stock of goods a company holds to sell or to use in making what it sells. Accounting splits inventory into three parts. Raw materials are the inputs bought but not yet used. Work in progress is the goods part-way through production. Finished goods are the completed products waiting to be sold. Inventory is carried on the books at the lower of what the goods cost or what the company can realistically sell them for. This rule, called lower of cost or net realizable value, forces a write-down when goods lose value before they sell.

The balance sheet is the statement that lists what a company owns, what it owes, and what is left for owners. Inventory sits in the current assets, the assets expected to convert to cash within one year. The line is capital the company has tied up in physical goods that have not yet been sold. In the vertical analysis table the app shows inventory as a percent of total assets, the sum of everything the company owns. The percent shows how much of the whole company is locked in goods on hand.

Common readings of the line as a percent of total assets:

| Inventory share of total assets | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | A service or software business that holds little or no physical stock. |
| 5 to 20 percent | A normal level for many manufacturers and wholesalers. |
| 20 to 40 percent | An inventory-heavy business such as a retailer or a parts maker. |
| Rising fast versus sales | A warning. Inventory growing faster than revenue can signal weak demand or coming write-downs. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Inventory is reported directly, not computed from a ratio. The line is the sum of its three stages:

`Inventory = Raw Materials + Work In Progress + Finished Goods`

Raw materials and work in progress represent goods not yet ready for sale. Raw materials are valued at their purchase cost. Work in progress adds the labor and overhead spent so far in turning those materials into product. Both stages tie up cash that the company will recover only after the goods are finished and sold.

Finished goods are completed products awaiting sale, valued at their full production cost. The total of the three stages is the inventory line. The cost method the company uses to value the stock matters. Two common methods are FIFO, which assumes the oldest goods are sold first, and LIFO, which assumes the newest goods are sold first. The two methods can produce different inventory values when prices change, so the method is disclosed in the notes.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Inventory shows how much capital a product business has frozen in goods. Every dollar in inventory is a dollar the company spent that has not yet returned as a sale. The line tells the investor how much working capital, the money locked in day-to-day operations, the business needs to keep running. A company that holds less inventory to support the same sales is using its capital more efficiently.

Inventory is a leading clue to demand. Goods that pile up faster than they sell signal that customers are buying less than the company expected. A swelling inventory line is one of the earliest warnings of a coming slowdown, often visible before revenue falls.

The line also carries a hidden quality risk. Inventory is valued at cost until the company sells it or admits it has lost value. Stock that becomes outdated, damaged, or unsellable must eventually be written down, which cuts profit. Inventory that the company cannot move is a future loss waiting to be recognized.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Sales volume and production plans move the line first. A company building stock ahead of an expected sales increase raises inventory on purpose. A company facing falling demand can see inventory rise by accident as goods fail to sell. The same higher inventory can mean opposite things, so the cause matters.

Supply chain and pricing move it next. Stockpiling materials to guard against shortages lifts the line. Rising input prices raise the recorded cost of the same physical stock. The valuation method, FIFO or LIFO, changes how fast price changes flow into the inventory figure versus the cost of goods sold.

Accounting choices distort the line. Delaying a write-down keeps unsellable goods on the books at full cost, which props up both inventory and profit. Overstating inventory is one way a company can understate its expenses and inflate earnings in the short term. A buildup of stock that the company refuses to write down is a common warning sign of both demand problems and aggressive accounting.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

How large inventory should be depends entirely on what the company makes and sells.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and services | Near zero | There is no physical product to stock. |
| Retailers and wholesalers | High inventory share | The business is built around holding goods for sale. |
| Manufacturers | Moderate to high share | Raw materials and work in progress sit on hand through production. |
| Perishable-goods firms | Low share, fast turnover | Goods spoil, so stock must move quickly and cannot pile up. |
| Heavy equipment and aerospace | High and lumpy share | Long build cycles keep large work-in-progress balances for months. |

A cross-sector comparison of inventory share is meaningless when one business holds no stock and another is built on it. Compare a company with direct peers, and always read inventory against the company's own revenue trend rather than in isolation.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows a multi-year chart of inventory. The most useful test is the comparison with revenue. Inventory should grow at roughly the same pace as sales. A steady rise in inventory in line with revenue is normal and healthy. A rise in inventory that outpaces revenue is a warning, because goods are accumulating faster than customers are buying them. A buildup like this often precedes a write-down or a period of discounting that hurts profit.

A steady decline relative to sales usually means faster-moving stock or tighter inventory management, which frees up cash. A step change in one year can mark a new product launch, a stockpiling decision, or a large write-down. High volatility points to a seasonal or project-based business. The base effect to watch is total assets, since the inventory percent can fall even when the dollar balance grows if total assets grow faster. Read the dollar balance, the percent of total assets, and the comparison with revenue together. The app's days inventory outstanding line turns this comparison into a single number of days.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Days Inventory Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days inventory outstanding measures the average number of days goods sit in stock before they sell. The metric is built directly from the inventory balance and the cost of goods sold. Inventory is the stock of goods on hand, and days inventory outstanding turns that stock into a holding time. Reading the two together converts a balance into the speed at which goods move.

**What the combination tells you.** An inventory balance that grows while days inventory outstanding holds steady is simply tracking a larger business and is no cause for concern. An inventory balance that grows while days inventory outstanding rises means goods are sitting longer and selling more slowly. Reading the two together separates healthy growth from a demand problem that the raw balance alone could hide.

### COGS / Inventory

**Why read the two together.** Cost of goods sold divided by inventory is the inventory turnover ratio. The ratio counts how many times a year the company sells and replaces its stock. Inventory is the denominator of that ratio. A high turnover means goods move fast, and a low turnover means goods linger. Reading turnover beside the balance shows the efficiency behind the stock on hand.

**What the combination tells you.** A falling turnover alongside a rising inventory balance confirms that the company is selling its goods more slowly over time. A stable turnover means the larger balance is the natural result of a larger business. Reading the two together tells the investor whether the inventory line reflects growth or a worsening sales pace that may lead to write-downs.

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the cost of goods sold. Inventory becomes cost of goods sold when it is sold, so the two lines are linked. A company forced to discount slow-moving inventory sells those goods at a lower margin. Reading margin beside the inventory trend shows whether a buildup is being cleared cheaply.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising inventory balance followed by a falling gross margin suggests the company is cutting prices to move stock that did not sell at full price. A stable margin alongside steady inventory means goods are selling as planned. Reading the two together reveals whether an inventory problem is already eating into profitability or still lies ahead as a write-down.

## Part 8: Limitations

Inventory is valued at cost until the company sells it or admits it has lost value, so the line can overstate what the goods are truly worth. A balance that looks healthy may hide outdated or unsellable stock that will be written down later. The valuation method also limits comparison. A company using LIFO and a company using FIFO can report different inventory values for the same physical goods when prices change, so two firms are not always measured on the same basis.

The line shows a single balance and says nothing about the mix inside it. A large finished-goods balance is a different risk from a large raw-materials balance, yet both appear on one line. The notes reveal the split, but the headline figure does not. Inventory is a useful early read on demand and capital efficiency. The line is a poor last word. The real risks live in the age of the stock, the valuation method, and the share that may never sell at full price.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is inventory growing faster than revenue over the years on the chart? If so, is the company building stock for expected growth, or are goods failing to sell?
2. What has happened to days inventory outstanding over the same period? Are goods moving at a stable pace, or sitting longer before they sell?
3. Which valuation method does the company use, FIFO or LIFO, according to the notes? How would a switch change the reported inventory value?
4. What is the split between raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods? Is a buildup concentrated in finished goods the company cannot sell?
5. Have there been recent inventory write-downs? Do they suggest stock was carried at too high a value in prior years?
6. Has gross margin fallen as inventory rose? Is the company discounting to clear stock that did not sell at full price?
7. How perishable or fashion-sensitive are the goods? How quickly does unsold stock lose value in this business?
8. How does the inventory share of total assets compare with direct competitors? Is the company holding more stock than peers to support the same sales?
9. Is the inventory line lumpy because of long production cycles, and if so, where in the cycle does the company stand now?
10. Does management explain a rising inventory balance as a deliberate buildup, and is that explanation consistent with the revenue and order trends?
