# Invested Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Invested capital is the total money put into the operating business by the people who fund it. Two groups fund a company. Lenders supply borrowed money such as bank loans and bonds, and shareholders supply equity, which is the owners' stake in the business. Invested capital adds those two funding sources together and then removes any cash the business does not need to operate. The number answers one question: how much money is actually tied up in running the business?

Invested capital is the base that returns and charges are measured against. Return on invested capital, written ROIC, is the operating profit the business earns divided by this base. The capital charge, which is the yearly cost of the funding, is this base multiplied by the cost of that funding. A dollar of profit only counts as value created once the business has paid for every dollar of invested capital it uses.

Common ways to read the base:

| Reading | What that reading usually means |
|---|---|
| A large base relative to sales | The business is capital-heavy and needs a lot of money in assets to produce each dollar of revenue. |
| A small base relative to sales | The business is capital-light and produces revenue with little money tied up. |
| The base growing faster than profit | The company is adding capital faster than it is adding profit, which lowers the return on each dollar. |
| The base growing in step with profit | The company is holding its return steady as the business grows. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The app uses the financing view of the formula:

`Invested Capital = Total Debt + Total Equity − Non-Operating Cash`

Total debt is the first part of the sum. Total debt is every interest-bearing borrowing the company owes, short-term and long-term, including bonds and bank loans. Total equity is the owners' capital, made up of the money shareholders paid in plus the profit the company has kept over the years rather than paying out. Adding debt and equity captures every dollar that funders have committed to the company.

Non-operating cash is the amount subtracted. Non-operating cash is money the business holds beyond what it needs to run day to day, such as a large balance held in short-term securities. Cash that just sits and earns interest is not part of the operating business, so leaving it in would overstate the capital that operations actually use. Removing it keeps the base tied to the business that produces the profit.

The same base can be built from the asset side instead, and the two routes give the same number.

| View | How it is built | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Financing view | Total debt plus total equity minus non-operating cash | Where the money came from. |
| Operating view | Net working capital plus net fixed assets | Where the money is put to work. |

Net working capital is the money tied up in short-term operating items, namely inventory and money owed by customers, minus the bills owed to suppliers. Net fixed assets are long-lived assets such as buildings and machinery, measured after the depreciation charged against them so far is removed. Depreciation is the accounting spread of an asset's cost over its useful life. The operating view shows that invested capital is simply the money locked into these operating assets.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Profit on its own does not say whether a company is using its funding well. A large profit earned on a very large amount of capital can be a weaker result than a smaller profit earned on a small base. Invested capital supplies the denominator that turns raw profit into a rate of return, so the profit can be judged against the money it took to produce.

Invested capital also sets the size of the yearly capital charge. Every dollar of funding carries a cost, because lenders charge interest and shareholders expect a return. The larger the base, the larger the charge the business must clear before it adds any value. The same profit therefore means very different things at a capital-light company and a capital-heavy one.

Invested capital is the anchor of the app's EVA valuation. The model treats the base as value that already exists, then adds the present value of the profit earned above the capital charge. Present value is today's worth of a future amount, found by discounting it back at a rate. A company is worth its invested capital plus whatever it can earn on top of the cost of that capital.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Invested capital rises when the company puts money to work. Building or buying factories, equipment, and other long-lived assets raises the base. Acquiring another company raises it too, often steeply, because the purchase price is added to the assets. Funding more inventory or letting customers take longer to pay raises net working capital and lifts the base as well.

Invested capital falls when the company shrinks the money tied up in operations. Selling assets, collecting from customers faster, and running leaner inventory all reduce the base. Depreciation lowers net fixed assets year by year unless fresh spending replaces the worn assets.

Accounting choices move the base without the business changing. Goodwill is the clearest case. Goodwill is the premium a company pays above fair value when it buys another business, and it sits in the base as an asset. Including goodwill charges the company for the prices it paid on past deals, while excluding goodwill measures only the capital the company built itself. The treatment of leases and of research spending shifts the base in the same way, because both can be counted as assets or as plain expenses depending on the rules applied.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The size of invested capital relative to sales depends heavily on how asset-heavy the business is. A meaningful base is the whole point of the measure in some sectors and almost absent in others.

| Industry type | Typical behavior of the base | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | Very large base | The business runs on expensive long-lived networks that must be built and maintained. |
| Capital-heavy industrials | Large base | Factories, machinery, and inventory tie up a great deal of money. |
| Retailers | Moderate base | Stores and inventory require capital, though less than heavy industry. |
| Capital-light software and brands | Very small base | The real assets are code and reputation, which accounting treats as expenses rather than capital. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Capital is set by regulation and borrowing is raw material, so the operating base does not mean the same thing. |

A cross-sector comparison of invested capital is meaningless when the businesses use fundamentally different amounts of physical capital. A software firm and a utility cannot be judged on the same scale, because one keeps its value in expensed intangibles and the other in a large asset base.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The multi-year chart of invested capital shows how fast the company is committing money to operations. A steady rise means the business is investing to grow, which is healthy only if profit is rising at least as fast. Reading the invested capital line next to the profit line is the key check. A base that climbs while profit stays flat is a warning that the new capital is not producing an adequate return.

A steady decline in the base can mean two very different things. A shrinking business that is losing assets shows a falling base, and so does a healthy business that has learned to operate with less capital. The profit trend separates the two cases. A step change usually marks a large acquisition or a big disposal, and the base jumps or drops in a single year. High volatility in the base often reflects swings in working capital rather than real changes in the long-lived asset base.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC is return on invested capital, the operating profit the business earns divided by the invested capital base. Invested capital is the denominator of that ratio, so the two numbers are read as a pair by construction. A change in ROIC can come from the profit on top or from the capital base underneath, and only reading both shows which.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising base with steady ROIC means the company is finding new places to invest at the same good return. A rising base with falling ROIC means the extra capital is earning less than the capital already in place. Watching the base and the return together shows whether growth is adding value or just adding size.

### Sales / Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Sales divided by invested capital measures how much revenue each dollar of capital produces. Invested capital is the denominator here as well, so the ratio is a direct read on how hard the base is working. A high ratio means the base turns over quickly into sales, and a low ratio means a lot of capital is tied up for each dollar of revenue.

**What the combination tells you.** A company can raise its return either by widening its profit margin or by producing more sales from the same base. Reading sales over invested capital alongside the base itself shows which lever the company is using. A falling ratio while the base grows warns that new capital is being added faster than it is producing sales.

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** EVA is economic value added, the profit left after the company is charged for the capital it uses. Invested capital sets the size of that charge, because the charge is invested capital multiplied by the cost of capital. The larger the base, the higher the bar the profit must clear before any EVA appears.

**What the combination tells you.** Two companies with the same profit can post very different EVA if their invested capital differs, because the one with the larger base pays a larger charge. Reading EVA against the base shows whether a big profit is genuinely large or merely the product of a large capital base. A company that grows its base without growing EVA is investing without adding value.

### Enterprise Value

**Why read the two together.** Enterprise value is the value of the whole business to all its funders, debt and equity together. In the EVA framework enterprise value equals invested capital plus market value added, which is the extra worth the company creates above its capital base. Reading the two shows how much of the company's value is money already invested and how much is value added on top.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose enterprise value sits far above its invested capital is priced for large future excess returns. A company whose enterprise value is close to its invested capital is priced as though it will earn about its cost of capital and little more. The gap between the two is the market value added, and that gap is the part competition can erode.

## Part 8: Limitations

Invested capital is built from book values, and book values can drift far from what the assets are truly worth. Old assets bought decades ago sit at their original cost minus depreciation, which can understate the real capital in the business and flatter every return measured against it. A recent acquisition can do the opposite, loading the base with goodwill that inflates it. The measure depends on which adjustments the data source applies, so two providers can report different invested capital for the same company.

The base is also a poor fit for companies whose real capital is not on the balance sheet. A software or drug company spends heavily on research and brands, and accounting treats that spending as an expense rather than as an asset. Invested capital then looks tiny next to the value the company produces, and returns measured against it look unrealistically high. For banks and insurers the base is a regulatory construct rather than a clean operating figure, so the measure loses its usual meaning.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is driving the current level of invested capital, the long-lived asset base or the working capital tied up in inventory and receivables?
2. Has the base grown faster or slower than profit over the years on the chart? What has that done to the return on each dollar of capital?
3. How much of the base is goodwill from past acquisitions? What does the return look like once goodwill is removed?
4. Did any single year show a step change in the base? Was the jump an acquisition, a large disposal, or a shift in working capital?
5. Does the company keep a large non-operating cash balance? How much does removing that cash change the base?
6. How does the company's invested capital relative to sales compare with direct competitors in the same sector?
7. Is the business asset-heavy or asset-light, and does the reported base capture the research or brand spending that really drives its value?
8. Is the base growing because the company is finding good new investments, or because it is holding more inventory and stretching its own collection times?
9. How much of the company's enterprise value is the invested capital already in place, and how much is value the market expects it to add?
10. Would capitalizing leases or research spending materially change the base, and which version better describes the money truly at work in the business?
