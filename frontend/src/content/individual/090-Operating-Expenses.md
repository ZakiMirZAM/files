# Operating Expenses

## Part 1: Definition

Operating expenses is the total cost of running the company around its products. The line sums every operating cost that comes after the direct cost of the product: research, selling and marketing, general and administrative, and any other operating items. The line sits below gross profit on the income statement, and subtracting it from gross profit gives operating income. The line answers one question: what does it cost to run this business beyond making the products themselves?

Two beginner terms to define first. The direct cost of the product, called cost of revenue, is the cost of the materials and labor that go into what the company sells. An operating cost is any cost of running the business that is not that direct cost. Operating expenses is the sum of all those operating costs. In the vertical analysis table the app shows the line as a percent of revenue. A reading of 30 percent means thirty cents of every sales dollar goes to running the company around its products.

Common readings of operating expenses as a percent of revenue:

| Operating expense share of revenue | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 15 percent | Lean. Common in companies that sell in bulk with little selling or research cost. |
| 15 to 30 percent | Typical for many established companies. |
| 30 to 50 percent | Heavy. Common in companies that spend a lot on selling, brand, or research. |
| Above 50 percent | Very heavy. Most of the gross profit is consumed running the business. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

This line is not a ratio. The company reports the total, and the app converts it to a percent of revenue for the vertical analysis table:

`Operating Expense Share = Operating Expenses ÷ Revenue`

Operating expenses is the top number, and the line is a sum of the operating-cost lines above it:

`Operating Expenses = Selling And Marketing + General And Administrative + Research And Development + Other Operating Items`

Each part is a separate operating cost. Selling and marketing is the cost of winning customers. General and administrative is the overhead of the corporate office. Research and development is the cost of creating new products. Other operating items catch what the named lines do not. The named lines are the detail, and operating expenses is their total. Some companies report selling and administrative combined as SG&A, in which case operating expenses sums SG&A with the rest.

Revenue is the bottom number. Revenue is the total sales for the year. Dividing by revenue states the cost in cents per sales dollar, which puts companies of different sizes on the same scale. The line stops short of two things. The line excludes the direct cost of the product, which is counted separately as cost of revenue. The line also excludes interest and taxes, which come after operating income.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Operating expenses measures the full cost of the organization beyond the product itself. Gross margin shows how profitable the product is on its own. Operating expenses shows how much of that product profit is spent running the company. The two together produce operating income, which is the profit the core business actually earns.

The line is a direct read on operating efficiency. A company that holds operating expenses steady as a percent of revenue while sales grow is spreading its overhead over more sales, which lifts profit. A company whose operating-expense share climbs year after year is letting its cost base grow faster than the business, which drains profit even when revenue rises.

The line also shapes how profit behaves through a downturn. Much of operating expense is fixed cost that does not fall when sales fall. A company with a heavy fixed operating-expense base sees profit drop sharply when revenue dips, because the cost stays while the sales shrink. Reading this line tells you how exposed operating income is to a bad year.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Scale moves the line strongly. A large share of operating expense is fixed, so a growing company spreads it over more revenue and the share falls even as the dollar cost rises. A shrinking company sees the share rise because the same fixed cost is divided over fewer sales. The dollar figure and the share of revenue can move in opposite directions for this reason.

The business model sets the level. A company that sells through heavy advertising and a large salesforce carries high selling cost here. A company that must keep inventing new products carries high research cost. A commodity producer that sells output at a market price carries very little of either. The mix of the underlying lines explains most of the difference between two companies.

One-off items distort single years. A restructuring charge, a legal settlement, or the cost of integrating an acquisition can inflate operating expenses for a year and then fall away. A reader should strip out these one-time items, which often sit in the other-operating line, before judging the underlying cost trend.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal operating-expense share depends on how much selling and research the sector demands.

| Industry type | Typical operating expense share | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer brands | High | Heavy advertising and a large marketing organization dominate the line. |
| Software and pharmaceuticals | Very high | Large research and selling budgets are the core of the cost base. |
| Industrial manufacturing | Moderate | Selling is lean and research is steady, so overhead is contained. |
| Wholesale and distribution | Low | Thin margins force minimal overhead and little selling cost. |
| Commodity producers | Very low | Output sells at a market price with little selling or research effort. |

A cross-sector comparison of the operating-expense share is meaningless when the sectors carry their costs differently. A software company books most of its cost as operating expense, while a manufacturer books most of its cost as cost of revenue, so the same operating-expense share means very different things. Compare the share only within a sector, and read it alongside gross margin to see where each company carries its costs.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read operating expenses as a percent of revenue across the multi-year chart. The raw dollar figure grows with the company and tells you little on its own. The share of revenue tells you whether the cost of running the business is being controlled as the business changes size.

A share that falls steadily while revenue grows is the strongest pattern. A falling share means fixed overhead is being spread over more sales, which is operating leverage at work and lifts profit. A share that rises steadily is a warning that the cost base is growing faster than sales. A step change usually points to an acquisition, a restructuring, or a one-time charge, and the reader should confirm which by checking the underlying lines. High volatility in the share often reflects lumpy one-off items in the other-operating line rather than a real change in the cost base. The most useful move is to break the trend into its parts, because a rising share driven by research spending and one driven by ballooning administration carry very different messages.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of the product, measured before operating expenses. Operating expenses is the next block of cost subtracted, and the gap between gross margin and operating expenses is the operating margin. A high gross margin gives room to carry heavy operating expense, while a thin gross margin leaves little.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high gross margin and a high operating-expense share is spending its product profit on selling and research, which can be healthy if revenue grows. A company with a thin gross margin and a high operating-expense share is in a squeeze, because the operating cost eats the small profit the product earns. Reading the two together shows whether the cost of running the business is affordable given what the product earns. The pair locates where the company's profit is won or lost.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is operating income as a percent of revenue, which is exactly what is left after gross margin minus the operating-expense share. Operating expenses is the bridge between gross margin and EBIT margin. A change in the operating-expense share flows almost directly into the EBIT margin.

**What the combination tells you.** When EBIT margin falls and the operating-expense share is the line that rose, the profit pressure comes from the cost of running the business rather than from the product. When EBIT margin improves as the operating-expense share falls, the company is converting more product profit into operating profit through cost control. Reading the two together tells you whether operating profit is driven by the product or by discipline over overhead.

### Operating Leverage (DOL)

**Why read the two together.** Operating leverage measures how sharply operating income moves when revenue moves, and it depends on how much of the cost base is fixed. A large part of operating expenses is fixed cost that does not shrink when sales fall. A company with heavy fixed operating expense has high operating leverage.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high fixed operating-expense base sees its operating-expense share fall fast in good years and rise fast in bad years, swinging profit widely. A company with a flexible cost base shows a steadier share through the cycle. Reading the two together tells you how much of today's profit would survive a fall in sales, because the fixed part of operating expense stays while revenue drops.

## Part 8: Limitations

Operating expenses is a total, and a total can hide the mix beneath it. A steady operating-expense share can mask a company cutting research while letting administration grow, which weakens the future while looking unchanged. The single number cannot reveal that shift, so the line must be read alongside its component lines rather than on its own.

Comparability across companies is limited. Companies draw the boundary between cost of revenue and operating expenses differently, so a cost that one company counts here another counts in the product cost. That makes the operating-expense share look high for one and low for another with no real difference in the business. One-time charges and accounting choices add further noise to any single year. The line is best read as a trend in its parts, against close competitors, rather than as one figure taken at face value.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is operating expense as a percent of revenue this year, and how does that compare with three and five years ago? Is the cost of running the business getting lighter or heavier?
2. Which component line is driving the change in the total: selling, administrative, research, or the other-operating line?
3. Did the share fall because fixed overhead was spread over rising sales, or because the company cut spending like research that the future depends on?
4. Are there one-time charges inside operating expenses this year, such as restructuring or settlements? What is the share without them?
5. How much of operating expense is fixed and how much is flexible? How far could it fall if sales dropped sharply?
6. Does the company draw the line between cost of revenue and operating expense the same way as its competitors, or is that boundary distorting the comparison?
7. How does the operating-expense share compare with direct competitors in the same sector?
8. Is the gross margin high enough to carry the current operating-expense share, or is operating cost consuming most of the product's profit?
9. In the most recent downturn, how much did operating expense fall as sales fell, and what did that do to operating income?
10. If the share is falling, is it falling for a good reason such as scale, or for a worrying reason such as starving research and selling?
