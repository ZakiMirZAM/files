# Natural Capital Structure

## Part 1: Definition

Natural capital structure is the mix of debt and equity that fits how risky a company's operations are. Debt is borrowed money that carries interest, which is the fee charged for borrowing. Equity is the owners' own money, which has no repayment date and no required payment. The natural capital structure answers one question: does the amount of debt match how steady the business is?

Two forces set the answer. Operating risk is how much the company's cash flow swings from year to year, and cash flow is the money the business produces after its running costs. Financial leverage is the share of the company funded by debt rather than by equity. A business with high operating risk should be funded mostly with equity, because equity absorbs a bad year without forcing a default. Default means failing to pay a debt on time. A business with steady cash flow can safely carry more debt.

The table below reads the four combinations of operating risk and leverage.

| Operating risk and leverage | What the pairing usually means |
|---|---|
| Low operating risk, low leverage | Conservative and safe. The company could carry more debt than it does. |
| Low operating risk, high leverage | Often sound. Steady cash flow can service a heavy debt load, as with regulated utilities. |
| High operating risk, low leverage | Prudent. Equity absorbs the swings, which is the correct choice for a volatile business. |
| High operating risk, high leverage | The dangerous mismatch. A single weak year can push the company toward default. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

There is no single formula. The app measures two inputs and then checks whether the two clash.

`Operating Risk = the volatility of yearly revenue growth`

Operating risk is the first input. Volatility means how widely a number varies around its own average. Revenue that grows a similar amount every year has low volatility, so operating risk is low. Revenue that jumps one year and falls the next has high volatility, so operating risk is high.

Financial leverage is the second input. Financial leverage measures how much of the company was funded by debt instead of equity. The app reads leverage from either of the two ratios in the table below.

| Leverage measure | What the measure states |
|---|---|
| Net Debt ÷ EBITDA | The debt load stated in years of earnings. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, and the depreciation and amortization charges. |
| Total Debt ÷ Total Assets | The share of everything the company owns that was funded by borrowing. |

The mismatch rule combines the two inputs. The app raises a warning when high operating risk is paired with high financial leverage. A high reading on one input by itself is not the warning. The clash between high operating risk and high leverage is the warning.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The amount of debt that is safe for one company can sink another. The difference is how steady the operating cash flow is. Steady cash flow can meet fixed debt payments in almost every year. Cash flow that swings widely will fall short in the weak years, and the missed payments can trigger default.

Equity behaves differently from debt in a bad year. Equity has no repayment date and no required payment, so equity can absorb a run of poor results without forcing a failure. Debt demands scheduled payments of interest and principal no matter how the business is doing. Principal is the borrowed amount itself, separate from the interest fee. A company with volatile operations therefore needs equity as the larger part of its funding.

The pairing of high operating risk with high leverage is a well-known path to distress. Many debt-funded buyouts that later failed had loaded heavy debt onto businesses whose cash flow was too volatile to carry it. Matching the debt load to the operating risk is the core judgment this metric supports.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces lift the volatility of revenue and so raise operating risk. Heavy competition and exposure to the economic cycle make sales harder to predict. Dependence on a few large customers adds further volatility, as does a business tied to swinging commodity prices. A high share of fixed costs raises operating risk again, because fixed costs do not fall when sales fall.

Financial leverage moves for its own reasons. Borrowing to fund an acquisition, a share buyback, or a dividend raises the debt load. Repaying debt from cash flow lowers the debt load. A drop in EBITDA raises the Net Debt ÷ EBITDA ratio even when the dollar amount of debt has not changed.

One-off items can distort either input. A single large acquisition can spike measured revenue growth for one year and inflate the operating-risk reading. A one-time asset sale can cut reported debt for a year and understate the true leverage. The mismatch reading is most reliable when several years are viewed together.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The natural leverage of a business depends on the steadiness of its cash flow and the liquidity of its assets. Liquid assets are assets that can be sold quickly for close to their stated value.

| Industry type | Natural leverage | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Regulated utilities | High | Regulators approve prices, so revenue is near certain and heavy debt is safe. |
| Banks | Very high | Assets are mostly liquid loans and securities, which support a thin equity cushion. |
| Supermarkets | Moderate to high | Demand is stable and inventory turns quickly, so cash flow is fairly predictable. |
| Apparel and fashion retail | Low | Merchandise can go out of style, so cash flow is volatile and heavy debt is dangerous. |
| Software and pharmaceuticals | Low | Large research spending and uncertain product success make cash flow risky, so equity should dominate. |
| Commodity producers | Low | Prices swing with the cycle, so debt sized to good years becomes unbearable at the trough. |

Compare a company only against others in the same sector. The mismatch warning matters most for cyclical and highly competitive businesses, where a downturn can cut cash flow sharply. Banks are judged differently, and the app measures a bank's capital adequacy with the Equity To Total Assets ratio rather than these operating-risk and leverage inputs.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows the two inputs over several years. Read the operating-risk line and the leverage line together. A leverage line that climbs while the operating-risk line stays low can be a deliberate and sound choice. A leverage line that climbs while operating risk is already high is the pattern that deserves the most attention.

The worst case is a mismatch that widens over time. Rising debt taken on just as cash flow becomes less predictable leaves the company exposed in the next downturn. A single unusual year can distort the operating-risk reading, because one large acquisition or one lost contract can spike measured revenue volatility. Judge the trend across the full set of years rather than the latest point.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Natural capital structure asks whether the debt load fits the operating risk. Net Debt ÷ EBITDA states the size of that debt load in years of earnings, where net debt is total debt minus cash. The pair turns the general question of fit into a specific number for the debt side.

**What the combination tells you.** A high Net Debt ÷ EBITDA is safe when operating risk is low and alarming when operating risk is high. Reading the leverage number next to the operating-risk reading shows whether a given debt load is prudent or reckless for this particular business. A rising Net Debt ÷ EBITDA on a business with volatile revenue is the clearest form of the dangerous mismatch.

### Operating Leverage (DOL)

**Why read the two together.** Operating leverage measures how sharply operating profit moves when revenue moves, and high operating leverage comes from a large share of fixed costs. High operating leverage is a major source of operating risk, because fixed costs magnify the effect of a sales decline. Reading operating leverage explains why a company's cash flow is volatile in the first place.

**What the combination tells you.** A business with high operating leverage and high financial leverage carries risk on two levels at once. A fall in sales is amplified into a larger fall in operating profit by the fixed costs, and the debt payments still must be met from that shrunken profit. Low operating leverage makes a given amount of debt far safer, because profit falls gently when sales dip.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest coverage measures how many times operating profit could pay one year of interest on the debt. Natural capital structure sets whether the chosen debt load fits the operating risk, and interest coverage shows how much room the current profit leaves above the interest bill. A thin cushion of coverage is far more dangerous on a volatile business than on a steady one.

**What the combination tells you.** High operating risk paired with low interest coverage is a fragile position, because one weak year can pull coverage below the point where profit no longer covers interest. The same coverage level is much safer for a business with steady cash flow. Reading the two together shows how large an earnings decline the company could survive before the debt becomes a problem.

### Debt To Assets

**Why read the two together.** Debt To Assets states the share of the company's assets that was funded by borrowing rather than by equity. The ratio is the second way the app reads financial leverage, alongside Net Debt ÷ EBITDA. Placing Debt To Assets next to the operating-risk reading tests the balance-sheet side of the fit.

**What the combination tells you.** A high Debt To Assets on a business with volatile cash flow signals the dangerous mismatch from the asset side. A high Debt To Assets can be perfectly safe when the assets are liquid and the cash flow is steady, as with a bank or a utility. Reading the ratio against operating risk keeps a high number from being judged as risky or safe on its own.

## Part 8: Limitations

The operating-risk reading is built from past revenue growth, so the reading looks backward. A business can show years of steady revenue and still face a sudden shock from a new competitor or a lost customer. Revenue volatility also misses other sources of operating risk, such as swings in profit margins or a heavy load of fixed costs that revenue alone does not reveal.

The natural capital structure is a matter of judgment rather than a precise line. There is no single debt level that is correct for a given business, and reasonable analysts can disagree about how much operating risk a company truly carries. The mismatch flag is a prompt to investigate, not a verdict. A company can carry the wrong structure for years before a downturn exposes the problem.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is driving the current operating-risk reading? Is the volatility of revenue growth coming from the underlying business or from a one-time event such as an acquisition?
2. Does the current debt load look large or small next to the steadiness of the company's cash flow over the past several years?
3. In the company's worst revenue year on the chart, how far did cash flow fall? Could the current debt payments have been met from that year's cash flow?
4. How much of the company's cost base is fixed rather than variable? How much would operating profit fall if revenue dropped by a tenth?
5. Is the operating risk of this business closer to a regulated utility or to a fashion retailer? Does the current leverage match that level of risk?
6. Has debt been added recently to fund a buyback, a dividend, or an acquisition? Did the operating risk of the business change at the same time?
7. How liquid are the company's assets? Could assets be sold quickly to raise cash in a downturn without crippling the business?
8. If the mismatch flag is raised, is the main cause high leverage or high operating risk? Which of the two could management realistically reduce?
9. How does the company's leverage compare with direct competitors that face the same operating risks? Which competitor would be forced to cut back first in a long downturn?
10. Does the balance sheet hold enough equity to absorb a multi-year run of weak cash flow without breaching the company's loan terms?
