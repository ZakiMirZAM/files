# Sustainable EBIT

## Part 1: Definition

Sustainable EBIT is the operating profit a company can earn in a normal year, averaged across the ups and downs of a full business cycle. EBIT is earnings before interest and taxes, the profit a business makes from its normal operations before paying lenders or the government. The word sustainable means the figure is normalized, stripped of one-time items and of the peaks and troughs of the cycle. The metric answers one question: what level of operating profit can this business repeat year after year, rather than in a single lucky or unlucky year?

A single year's EBIT can be distorted by a boom, a recession, or a one-off event. Sustainable EBIT removes those distortions to show the underlying earning power. The app builds it by applying the company's through-the-cycle operating margin to current revenue. Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue, stated as a percentage. Using a mid-cycle margin instead of the latest one keeps a single strong or weak year from setting the whole figure.

Common readings of sustainable EBIT against the latest reported EBIT:

| Sustainable EBIT versus latest EBIT | What that comparison usually means |
|---|---|
| Well below latest EBIT | The most recent year was unusually strong. Current profit is above the level the business can repeat. |
| Close to latest EBIT | The latest year was near normal. Reported profit is a fair guide to earning power. |
| Well above latest EBIT | The most recent year was unusually weak. Current profit understates the business's normal earning power. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Sustainable EBIT = Through-the-Cycle Operating Margin × Current Revenue`

The through-the-cycle operating margin is the first input. The margin is the middle or average operating margin across all the years on file, which smooths out the best and worst years. Revenues are far steadier than margins across a cycle, so holding the margin at a mid-cycle level is the reliable way to normalize. Greenwald advises using an average margin over a period that spans at least one full cycle, and ideally two.

Current revenue is the second input. Applying the normalized margin to today's revenue ties the figure to the company's current size, not to a size it had years ago. The method assumes revenue is a fair base, which holds except for highly cyclical businesses whose sales swing as much as their margins. The product of the two inputs is sustainable operating profit, the same normalized EBIT the app carries into its earnings-normalization section and its EPV calculation.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A permanent value cannot be built on a single year's profit. One year can be lifted by a boom or a one-time gain, or sunk by a recession or a write-off. Sustainable EBIT strips those effects out and leaves the profit the business can actually repeat. A valuation built on that figure is far steadier than one built on the latest number.

The figure matters most because it feeds the valuation directly. Sustainable EBIT is taxed and then capitalized to give the earnings power value, the no-growth worth of the company. An error in sustainable EBIT flows straight into that value. Starting from a normalized figure rather than a single year is what keeps the valuation from rising and falling with every swing in the cycle.

Sustainable EBIT also exposes how far the latest year sits from normal. Comparing it with reported EBIT shows whether current profit is running hot or cold. A latest year far above sustainable EBIT is a caution against assuming the good times last. A latest year far below it is a hint that depressed profit may recover.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Two inputs drive the figure, and each can distort it. The margin is set by the years on file. A history that spans a full cycle produces a fair margin, while a short history full of boom years produces a margin that is too high. The choice between a median and a mean also matters, because a few extreme years pull a mean around more than a median. The app uses a mid-cycle margin to limit that effect.

Current revenue is the other lever. Because the margin is applied to today's revenue, a revenue base swollen by a temporary demand surge lifts sustainable EBIT even when the margin is honest. An acquisition or a divestiture changes revenue suddenly, which shifts the figure without any change in underlying profitability. One-time items buried inside past operating profit can also distort the historical margins, which is why normalization removes them before the margin is set.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

How well sustainable EBIT describes the future depends on how stable margins are and on whether revenue itself swings with the cycle.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer staples and established brands | Sustainable EBIT is most reliable | Margins hold steady across cycles, so the normalized margin describes the future well. |
| Cyclical manufacturers and commodity producers | Use with care | Both margin and revenue swing with the cycle, so a mid-cycle margin on peak revenue overstates the figure. |
| Capital-light software | Margin trend needs judgment | Margins often rise as the business scales, so an old average can understate current earning power. |
| Research-heavy pharma and technology | Sustainable EBIT understated | Research is expensed at once, which lowers reported operating profit and the margins behind the figure. |
| Early-stage or fast-growing firms | Sustainable EBIT unstable | A short history gives no reliable cycle to average, so the normalized margin is a guess. |
| Banks and insurers | Not used this way | Interest is the core revenue of financial companies, so operating profit before interest has no clean meaning. |

For a cyclical business, the safest step is to normalize revenue as well as margin, because a mid-cycle margin on peak revenue still overstates the figure. A cross-sector comparison of sustainable EBIT margins is only meaningful when the businesses share similar cost structures and cycle exposure.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Sustainable EBIT moves slowly, because a mid-cycle margin barely shifts when one new year joins a long record. Most of the year-to-year change comes from current revenue rather than the margin. A steady rise driven by growing revenue at a stable margin is a genuine gain in earning power. A steady rise driven only by a rising margin deserves a check on whether the margin is truly sustainable or is being flattered by cost cuts.

A falling sustainable EBIT is the pattern to watch most. A decline means the normalized earning power itself is eroding. The cause may be a shrinking margin, a falling revenue base, or both. A single sharp move up or down usually reflects a swing in current revenue rather than a real change in the business. Comparing sustainable EBIT with the reported operating profit line each year shows how far the latest results sit above or below the normal level.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is operating profit stated as a percentage of revenue. Sustainable EBIT is built by applying a through-the-cycle EBIT margin to current revenue, so the margin is the engine of the figure. Reading the two together shows whether the normalized margin is close to the latest one or far from it. A wide gap between the current margin and the normalized margin flags a year that is running hot or cold.

**What the combination tells you.** A current EBIT margin well above the through-the-cycle margin warns that sustainable EBIT is deliberately lower than the latest profit, which is a caution against extrapolating the good year. A current margin below the normalized one suggests reported profit understates earning power, so sustainable EBIT sits higher. Watching the EBIT margin trend shows whether the normalized margin should be drifting up or down over time. A margin that is steadily changing means the historical average is slowly going stale.

### NOPAT

**Why read the two together.** NOPAT is net operating profit after tax, the operating profit a company keeps once tax is deducted but before any interest. Sustainable EBIT is a pre-tax figure, and taxing it produces the normalized NOPAT that a no-growth valuation capitalizes. The two are the same earning power measured before and after tax. Reading them together shows how much of the operating profit the tax rate removes.

**What the combination tells you.** A sustainable EBIT that looks healthy can shrink sharply after tax if the effective tax rate is high, so the after-tax NOPAT is the figure a valuation actually uses. Comparing a normalized NOPAT with reported NOPAT shows whether an unusual tax year is distorting current after-tax profit. When sustainable EBIT is steady but NOPAT jumps around, the tax rate is the source of the noise. Separating the operating result from the tax effect keeps a one-off tax change from being mistaken for a change in earning power.

### Owner Earnings

**Why read the two together.** Owner earnings is the cash a business produces for its owners after the spending needed to maintain the business. Sustainable EBIT measures normalized operating profit, while owner earnings measures normalized cash after maintenance capital spending. Both try to capture repeatable earning power rather than a single year. Reading them together shows whether accounting profit is turning into cash the owner can actually take.

**What the combination tells you.** A healthy sustainable EBIT paired with much lower owner earnings warns that heavy capital spending is consuming the profit before it reaches the owner. A sustainable EBIT close to owner earnings suggests the business is capital-light and its profit converts cleanly into cash. Comparing the two over several years shows whether rising maintenance spending is quietly eroding the value of the operating profit. The wider the gap, the less the headline EBIT represents money the owner can keep.

## Part 8: Limitations

Sustainable EBIT is only as good as the history behind the margin. A record too short to cover a full cycle, or one dominated by boom years, produces a normalized margin that is still too high. The method also assumes current revenue is a fair base, which fails for a cyclical business at a peak or a trough. A past reshaped by acquisitions can make the historical margins describe a company that no longer exists.

The figure can also lag a real change in the business. When margins have permanently improved or deteriorated, an average that still includes old years understates or overstates the true earning power. Expensed research and other investments in future growth depress reported operating profit, so sustainable EBIT can understate the earning power of research-heavy firms. The figure is a disciplined estimate of normal profit rather than a precise forecast, and it is silent for financial companies whose operating profit before interest has no clean meaning.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How many years of history sit behind the normalized margin, and do those years include at least one full downturn?
2. Is the through-the-cycle margin close to the latest reported margin, or is the current year running hot or cold?
3. Is current revenue near a cyclical peak or trough, and would normalizing revenue as well as margin change the figure?
4. Have acquisitions or divestitures reshaped revenue in a way that makes the historical margins misleading?
5. Has the company's margin trended steadily up or down, so that a simple average is going stale?
6. Are one-time gains or charges buried in past operating profit, and were they removed before the margin was set?
7. Does expensed research depress the operating profit, so that sustainable EBIT understates the real earning power?
8. How different is sustainable EBIT from the latest reported EBIT, and what explains the gap?
9. After tax, how much of sustainable EBIT survives as NOPAT, and is the tax rate used a normal one?
10. If the next few years looked like the worst years in the record, would sustainable EBIT still hold?
