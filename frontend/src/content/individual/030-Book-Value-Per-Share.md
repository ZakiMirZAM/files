# Book Value Per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Book value per share measures the accounting net worth that stands behind each share of stock. Book value is the company's total assets minus its total liabilities, which is the value of everything the company owns after subtracting everything it owes. Book value is also called shareholders equity or stockholders equity. Dividing it across all the shares answers one question: according to the accounting records, how much of the company's net worth does a single share own?

A figure of thirty means that by the books, each share owns thirty dollars of the company's net worth. That net worth is built from two sources: the money shareholders originally paid in, and the profits the company has kept rather than paid out over the years. Book value per share is the balance sheet's answer to what a share is worth, which is separate from the market price printed beside it every trading day. The two often differ widely, because the market prices a share on its expected future, while the books record only its accumulated past.

Common readings of the figure:

| Trend in book value per share | What that trend usually means |
|---|---|
| Rising steadily | The company is retaining profits and building net worth for each owner. |
| Flat while profits are earned | Earnings are being paid out fully, so net worth per share is not growing. |
| Falling | The company is losing money, paying out more than it earns, or buying back stock above book value. |
| Negative | Accumulated losses have wiped out the equity. Liabilities exceed assets on the books. |
| Far below market price | The market values future earning power well above the recorded net worth. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Book Value Per Share = Shareholders Equity ÷ Shares Outstanding`

Shareholders equity is the top number. Shareholders equity is total assets minus total liabilities, taken straight from the balance sheet. When a company has preferred stock, which is a senior class of shares paid before common owners, the value of the preferred stock is removed first. The figure then reflects only the equity belonging to common shareholders. Shareholders equity belongs on top because it is the residual claim, meaning the value that remains for owners after every creditor and senior claim has been satisfied.

Shares outstanding is the bottom number. Shares outstanding is the count of common ownership units in public hands. The app uses the share count from the balance sheet date, because book value is itself a balance-sheet figure measured at a single point in time. Dilution is the rise in the share count when the company issues new shares, and dilution lowers book value per share unless the new shares are sold for more than the existing book value. A balance sheet records assets largely at their original purchase cost reduced by depreciation, so the book value of an asset usually drifts away from its true market value as time passes.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Book value per share gives a stable, simple measure of net worth that an investor can compare against the share price. For investors who distrust forecasts of future cash, the book value is a concrete benchmark grounded in the balance sheet rather than in projections. The figure answers what owners would have a claim on, in accounting terms, if the company stopped growing and simply held what it had.

Book value per share works even when earnings do not. A company with negative profit cannot be valued on earnings, but it can still be measured against its book value, and far fewer companies have negative book value than have negative earnings. The figure is a useful anchor for cyclical companies and for businesses passing through a loss-making patch.

Book value per share is also the denominator of the price-to-book ratio, which divides the share price by book value per share. A low price-to-book ratio has long been read as a sign of a possible bargain, and a high ratio as a sign the market expects strong future returns on the recorded equity. The figure is one half of that widely used comparison.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The top number grows when the company earns a profit and keeps it inside the business as retained earnings. The top number shrinks when the company loses money, when it pays out dividends, or when it buys back stock above its book value. A buyback below book value raises book value per share, while a buyback above book value lowers it, which is why buybacks can push the figure either way depending on the price paid.

Accounting choices shape the figure heavily. Depreciation rules lower the recorded value of assets over time, often well below what those assets would sell for. Writedowns cut book value in a single step when an asset is judged to have lost value. Internally built brands, patents, and software are usually not recorded as assets at all. A company rich in those intangibles can show a book value far below its true worth. Share issuance moves the bottom number and dilutes book value per share unless the new shares are sold above the existing book value.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Book value per share carries very different meaning across industries, depending on how much of a company's worth sits in recorded tangible assets. The comparison must stay inside one sector.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Banks and insurers | Book value is meaningful | Their assets are mostly financial and recorded near market value, so book value closely tracks real net worth. |
| Software and service firms | Book value understates worth | Their value lies in people, brands, and code that the books do not record as assets. |
| Heavy manufacturers and utilities | Book value is moderately useful | Large tangible assets are recorded, though at depreciated cost rather than market value. |
| Asset-light consumer brands | Book value understates worth | The brand is the main asset and is almost never on the balance sheet. |
| Companies after large writedowns | Book value distorted downward | A single charge can slash recorded equity below the true value of the business. |

A cross-sector comparison of book value per share or of the price-to-book ratio is meaningless because accounting captures tangible assets far better than intangible ones. A bank and a software firm cannot be judged against each other on this figure. Compare within a sector, and lean on book value most where assets are tangible and financial.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in book value per share is the core sign of a company compounding net worth for its owners. The business is earning profits and retaining enough of them to build the equity behind each share year after year. The smoothness of the rise matters, because a steadily growing book value points to consistent profitability, while a jagged line points to losses or large one-time charges interrupting the build.

A flat line while the company is profitable means earnings are being paid out fully rather than retained, so net worth per share is not growing. A decline signals losses, payouts beyond earnings, or buybacks made above book value. A sharp single-year drop usually marks a writedown, where an asset is suddenly recorded at a lower value. A negative book value, reached after sustained losses, means accumulated deficits have erased the recorded equity entirely. Always check whether a falling book value reflects genuine losses or a buyback program that is returning cash to owners at prices above the recorded equity.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### P/B

**Why read the two together.** The price-to-book ratio divides the share price by book value per share, so this figure is its denominator. The two are mechanically linked, and the ratio shows how many dollars the market pays for each dollar of recorded net worth. Reading them together connects the accounting net worth of a share to the price investors place on it.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio near one means the market values the company close to its recorded net worth, common for asset-heavy businesses such as banks. A ratio far above one means the market expects future earnings well beyond the recorded equity, common for asset-light firms whose real assets are not on the books. A ratio below one can signal a possible bargain or a market judgment that the recorded assets are worth less than the books claim, so the cause must be checked.

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity divides net income by shareholders equity, the same equity that stands behind book value per share. The two share the same base, so reading them together shows both the size of the net worth behind a share and how much profit that net worth produces. Book value measures the capital, while return on equity measures its productivity.

**What the combination tells you.** A high return on equity paired with rising book value per share means the company is both earning well on its capital and retaining enough to grow that capital, the ideal compounding pattern. A low return on equity with rising book value means net worth is growing but earning little, so the retained capital may be better returned to owners. The pair shows whether the equity behind a share is being put to good use.

### Tangible Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Tangible book value per share strips out intangible assets such as goodwill, which is the premium a company paid above book value when buying other companies. Book value per share includes those intangibles. Reading the two together shows how much of the recorded net worth rests on hard, sellable assets versus accounting entries from past acquisitions.

**What the combination tells you.** A small gap between the two means the net worth is backed mostly by tangible assets and is solid. A large gap means much of the book value is goodwill and other intangibles that could be written down to nothing if an acquisition disappoints. For a company built through acquisitions, the tangible figure is the more conservative measure of what truly stands behind a share.

## Part 8: Limitations

Book value rests on accounting conventions that often diverge from real worth. Assets are recorded largely at original cost minus depreciation, so a building bought decades ago may be worth far more than the books show, while obsolete equipment may be worth far less. The biggest gap is in intangibles. Internally built brands, patents, customer loyalty, and software are usually not recorded as assets at all. A company whose value lies mainly in those intangibles can have a book value that badly understates its true worth.

Book value can also be distorted in both directions by accounting events. A large writedown slashes it in a single step, and goodwill from acquisitions inflates it with a number that may never be realized. Book value can turn negative after sustained losses or after large buybacks funded by debt, even when the business still operates normally. Book value per share is a useful anchor for asset-heavy and financial companies, and a weak guide for companies whose worth is intangible.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is book value per share rising, flat, or falling? Is the cause retained profits, full payouts, losses, or buybacks above book value?
2. How much of the company's real worth lies in intangible assets that the balance sheet does not record, such as brands or software?
3. What is the gap between book value per share and tangible book value per share? How much of the recorded equity is goodwill from acquisitions?
4. Did any sharp drop in the line come from a writedown? What asset was written down, and does the charge reflect a real loss of value?
5. Are buybacks being made above or below book value per share? Is management raising or lowering the recorded net worth per owner through them?
6. How does book value per share compare with the share price? What does the price-to-book ratio say the market expects beyond the recorded equity?
7. For this company's industry, is book value a meaningful measure of net worth, or do unrecorded intangibles make it misleading?
8. Is the equity growing because the company retains profits, or because it keeps issuing new shares and raising fresh capital?
9. What return on equity is the company earning on this book value? Is the retained capital producing strong profits or sitting idle?
10. If book value is negative or thin, is the business genuinely impaired, or has it been reshaped by debt-funded buybacks while still operating well?
