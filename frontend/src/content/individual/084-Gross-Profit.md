# Gross Profit

## Part 1: Definition

Gross profit is what is left of revenue after the direct cost of making the product is taken out. Revenue is the total value of goods and services sold. Direct cost, called cost of revenue, is the materials and the factory labor that go straight into the product. Gross profit is the gap between the two. Gross profit answers one question: how much money does the core product make before any other expense is paid?

In the vertical analysis table this app shows, gross profit appears as a percentage of revenue, and that percentage is the gross margin. A gross profit of forty cents on every revenue dollar is a gross margin of 40 percent. Gross profit is the first profit line on the income statement. Every expense below it must be paid out of this gross profit. That includes marketing, research, overhead, interest, and tax. A company with a thin gross profit has little to work with no matter how well it controls the rest.

Common readings of the gross profit line:

| Gross profit as a share of revenue | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Very high, well above half of revenue | The product sells for far more than it costs to make, a sign of strong pricing power or a light-cost product. |
| Moderate, around a third to a half | A normal range for many manufacturers and consumer goods companies. |
| Thin, well below a third | The product earns little over its direct cost. The company must rely on high volume to make money. |
| Rising over time | The company is gaining pricing power or producing more cheaply. A healthy direction. |
| Falling over time | Prices are slipping or input costs are climbing. Profit is being squeezed at the source. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of the goods and services sold:

`Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost Of Revenue`

Revenue is the top number. Revenue is the total the company charged customers for what it delivered during the period, counted when the sale is earned rather than when the cash arrives. Revenue is the starting point because gross profit measures what survives from sales, so the calculation must begin with the full value of those sales.

Cost of revenue is the number subtracted. Cost of revenue is the direct cost of making the product, such as raw materials and the labor on the production line. Only direct costs are subtracted at this stage. Costs that are not tied to making each unit, such as advertising, head-office salaries, and research, are left for later lines on the income statement. Subtracting only the direct cost is what makes gross profit a clean measure of how profitable the core product is on its own, before the cost of running the wider company is counted.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Gross profit is a measure of the company's efficiency at its core task: turning inputs into a product worth more than those inputs. A wide gross profit means the company controls its direct costs while charging a price the market accepts. That gap is the raw material for every other kind of profit. Marketing, research, overhead, interest, and tax all come out of it.

Gross profit also signals pricing power. A company that can hold a high gross margin year after year usually has a product customers will pay up for, whether through a strong brand, a patent, or a hard-to-copy design. A company forced to discount to keep sales shows a shrinking gross margin, which warns that its pricing power is weak. The level and the steadiness of gross profit are among the clearest signs of a durable competitive position.

Because gross profit sits so high on the statement, problems show up here first. A company in trouble at the product level cannot fix that by cutting overhead, because the shortfall begins above overhead. Gross profit is the first place to look when judging whether the business model itself works.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Two forces set gross profit: the price the company charges and the direct cost of making the product. Higher selling prices lift gross profit when costs hold steady, and a company with a strong brand or a unique product can raise prices without losing customers. Lower input costs lift gross profit the same way, whether from cheaper materials, cheaper energy, or more efficient production. A shift in product mix toward higher-margin items also raises the blended gross profit even when no single price changes.

Accounting choices can move gross profit without the business changing. Because gross profit depends on what the company counts inside cost of revenue, moving a cost such as depreciation or freight out of cost of revenue and into operating expenses raises reported gross profit while leaving total profit untouched. The inventory valuation method shifts which costs land in cost of revenue when input prices move, which changes gross profit in either direction. One-time events, such as a temporary discount campaign or a spike in a key raw material, can distort a single year and should not be read as a lasting change.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Gross profit levels vary enormously by business model, so a number that is excellent in one sector is poor in another.

| Industry type | Typical gross profit behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and digital services | Very high share of revenue | The cost of delivering one more copy is almost nothing, so most of each sale is gross profit. |
| Branded consumer goods | High and stable share of revenue | A trusted brand lets the company charge well above its production cost. |
| Manufacturers | Moderate share of revenue | Physical materials and labor consume a meaningful part of each sale. |
| Grocery and discount retail | Thin share of revenue | The business resells goods at a small markup and depends on high volume. |
| Banks and insurers | No standard gross profit line | Revenue and cost are built from interest and claims, not the production of a physical good. |

Comparing gross profit across two different industries leads to wrong conclusions, because each model carries a different natural level. The comparison is only meaningful between direct competitors selling a similar product. Even then, check that both companies define cost of revenue the same way before reading the gap as real.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows gross profit as a multi-year line and as a percent of revenue, which is the gross margin, in the vertical table. The percent-of-revenue line is the more revealing view, because it removes the effect of the company simply selling more. A flat gross margin over many years means the company is holding its pricing power and its production efficiency steady, which is the mark of a strong, stable business. A gross margin that rises over time means the company is charging more relative to its costs or producing more cheaply, both healthy signs.

A gross margin that falls year after year is a warning. The cause is either prices slipping under competition or input costs rising faster than the company can pass them through. A slow decline at the gross level is hard to reverse, because the problem sits at the heart of the business model. Watch for base effects that fake a trend. A one-year spike in a raw material can dent the margin for a single year and then recover, so a single weak year is not the same as a lasting slide. Cross-check the gross margin against revenue. Revenue growing while gross margin falls means the company is buying its growth with discounts.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Gross Margin

**Why read the two together.** Gross profit and gross margin describe the same gap in two forms. Gross profit is the dollar amount left after direct cost, and gross margin is that same amount as a share of revenue. Reading the two together separates the size of the profit from its quality. A large company can earn a big gross profit in dollars while running a thin and falling margin.

**What the combination tells you.** Gross profit growing while gross margin holds steady means the company is scaling a healthy product without giving up pricing power. Gross profit growing while gross margin falls means the extra profit is coming from volume at thinner margins, which is lower quality growth. A rising margin on flat gross profit shows efficiency gains offsetting weak sales. The pair shows whether the core business is getting stronger or just bigger.

### Cost Of Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Gross profit and cost of revenue are two parts of the same revenue dollar. What is not consumed by cost of revenue survives as gross profit, so the two always move in opposite directions as a share of revenue. Reading them together shows exactly where a change in gross profit came from. A falling gross margin traces straight back to a rising cost of revenue.

**What the combination tells you.** A stable cost of revenue share and a stable gross profit share confirm a predictable core business. A rising cost of revenue share dragging gross profit down points to an input or production problem, and the cost line names the source. A falling cost of revenue share lifting gross profit signals efficiency or pricing gains. The pair lets an investor diagnose the cause of any margin move rather than just observe it.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** Gross profit shows how profitable the product is, but not how much capital the company tied up to earn it. ROIC, the return on invested capital, measures the profit the company earns against all the money invested in the business. Reading the two together shows whether a strong product margin actually turns into a strong return for owners. A high gross margin can still produce a weak return if the business is heavy on assets.

**What the combination tells you.** High gross profit with high ROIC is the best combination, a product that earns a lot and does so without consuming much capital. High gross profit with low ROIC means the company makes good margins but needs heavy investment to do it, which dilutes the return. A widening gross margin alongside a rising ROIC confirms the company is compounding value. The pair connects product-level profitability to the return the business delivers on the money it uses.

## Part 8: Limitations

Gross profit depends entirely on what the company counts as cost of revenue, and companies draw that boundary differently. A firm that keeps depreciation, freight, or some labor out of cost of revenue will report a higher gross profit than a rival that includes them, even when the two businesses are identical. The inventory valuation method moves the line further when input prices change. These choices make gross profit hard to compare across companies without first checking the accounting policy.

Gross profit is also only the first profit line, so a strong gross profit does not guarantee a profitable company. A business with a high gross margin can still lose money once the costs below are paid. Those costs are marketing, research, overhead, interest, and tax. Gross profit says nothing about how much capital the business consumes, so two companies with the same gross margin can deliver very different returns. Gross profit is the right first test of a business model and never the final verdict on a company.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is driving the current gross margin: pricing power, low input costs, product mix, or production efficiency? Which of those is durable?
2. Has the gross margin held steady, risen, or fallen over the years on the chart? What explains the direction?
3. Does the company define cost of revenue the same way as its direct competitors, or does an accounting boundary inflate the reported gross profit?
4. Is revenue growing while gross margin falls? If so, is the company buying its growth with discounts?
5. Which input cost or product line most affects the gross margin, and how exposed is the company if that input price rises?
6. Has any cost been moved between cost of revenue and operating expenses, changing gross profit without changing total profit?
7. How does the gross margin compare with the closest competitor selling a similar product? What explains the gap?
8. How did gross profit behave in the worst year on the chart? Does the margin hold up in a downturn or collapse?
9. Does the high or low gross margin translate into a matching ROIC, or does the capital the business consumes wipe out the product-level advantage?
10. What gross margin does the app's discounted cash flow value assume going forward? How does that assumption compare with the company's own multi-year history?
