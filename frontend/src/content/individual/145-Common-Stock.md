# Common Stock

## Part 1: Definition

Common stock is the basic ownership share of a company. A common shareholder owns a slice of the business and has the last claim on its assets and profit, behind lenders and behind preferred holders. On the balance sheet, the common stock line travels with a partner line called additional paid-in capital. The two together record the money shareholders actually put into the company over its life. Additional paid-in capital is the amount paid for shares above their nominal par value, so the two lines together hold the full contributed capital.

Common stock answers one question: how much of the company's funding came from money the owners contributed, as opposed to profit the business kept? The contributed money includes the proceeds from the first public sale of shares, later share sales, the value of shares issued to employees as pay, and the cash from employees exercising stock options. In the app's vertical analysis, common stock is shown as a percent of total assets, so a reader sees how much of everything the company owns was funded by owner contributions.

The contributed layer sits beside retained earnings in the equity section. Retained earnings is the profit the business has kept rather than paid out. The relationship between the two tells the funding history. A company living on its shareholders' money shows a large contributed layer next to small retained earnings, while a long self-funding company shows the reverse.

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Common stock accumulates from every share the company has issued:

`Common Stock + Paid-In Capital = Sum of Proceeds from All Shares Issued`

Each share sold adds its proceeds to the contributed layer. The split between par value and additional paid-in capital is an accounting convention with no economic meaning, so the two are read together. Shares issued to employees as compensation add their grant value, and option exercises add the strike price the employees paid.

Buybacks, where the company repurchases its own shares, are treated in two ways. Some companies record repurchased shares as treasury stock, a contra-account that reduces total equity without touching the contributed layer. Other companies retire the shares directly, which reduces this layer. The treatment moves how the row looks, while the full record of contributions over time lives in the statement of changes in equity.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Common stock dates the company's funding history. Read against retained earnings, the contributed layer shows when a company graduated from living on raised money to funding itself from its own profit. A shrinking contributed share alongside growing retained earnings is the mark of a maturing, self-funding business.

The growth of the contributed layer is also a record of dilution. Dilution is the reduction in each existing owner's stake when new shares are issued. Every capital raise and every batch of shares granted to employees adds to this layer, so its pace shows how much the owners have been diluted over time. The per-share measures the app reports have their dollar counterpart in the building of this row.

The shape of the funding history reads here as well. A serial raiser shows an ever-growing contributed layer as it returns to the market again and again. A company that raised money once and never again shows a static layer. The difference separates businesses that consume outside capital from those that fund their own growth.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Common stock rises when the company issues shares: a capital raise, employee compensation vesting, or convertible securities turning into common stock all add to it. The retirement of repurchased shares reduces the layer where a company uses that treatment. Little else moves the line, because the contributed layer is simply the running ledger of what owners have put in.

The share-of-assets reading also falls when total assets grow while the contributed layer stays flat. A mature company that grows its asset base without issuing new shares will show a shrinking contributed share even though the dollar amount has not changed. A quiet line therefore usually means an absence of new issuance, which is itself a sign of funding discipline.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The weight of the contributed layer maps the funding history of the business, which varies sharply by sector.

| Industry type | Typical common stock behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Venture-built technology | Heavy contributed layer | Funded through repeated capital raises before reaching profit. |
| Biotech and mining | Growing with each round | Long pre-profit phases require serial share issuance. |
| Mature compounders | Static and shrinking as a share | Long self-funded, so the old contributions fade against growth. |
| Bootstrapped firms | Thin contributed layer | Built mostly from retained profit rather than raised capital. |
| Heavy stock-pay employers | Steadily rising | Continuous share grants to employees build the layer over time. |

A cross-sector comparison of common stock is meaningless on its own, because the layer reflects funding history rather than operating quality. The figure is read against the company's own retained earnings, not against an industry average. The same contributed share means different things for a young raiser and a mature compounder.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows common stock as a percent of total assets over several years, and the path tells two stories at once. The dollar level steps up at each capital raise and each vintage of share grants, and a company that pays employees heavily in stock shows the layer building continuously. Each step usually ties to a named event in the financing records.

The share-of-assets path reads the company's maturation. A contributed share shrinking against a growing enterprise is the signature of self-funding, where retained earnings and scale compound past the old contributions. A contributed share that keeps rising marks a company still leaning on outside capital. The buyback treatment brackets the optics, because a company retiring shares can hold the layer down even while issuing new stock, so the gross record in the statement of changes in equity should be checked behind the net figure.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Retained Earnings

**Why read the two together.** Common stock is the money owners contributed, and retained earnings is the profit the business kept. The two together make up the bulk of the equity section. Reading them side by side dates the company's funding history, because the ratio between contributed and earned capital shows when the business began funding itself. The pair is the biography of the equity.

**What the combination tells you.** A thin contributed layer beside a large retained earnings means the business has long funded itself from profit, which is the strongest configuration. A heavy contributed layer beside small retained earnings means the company still relies on raised money rather than its own earnings. The pair shows whether the company has graduated to self-funding.

### Equity Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Equity per share measures the total equity belonging to each share, and the contributed layer is one component of that equity. Reading common stock alongside it frames how the full equity section is built. The contributed layer and the retained layer together form the per-share equity. The pair shows the composition behind the per-share figure.

**What the combination tells you.** A per-share equity built mostly from retained earnings reflects accumulated profit, while one built mostly from contributed capital reflects raised money. Reading the contributed layer alongside equity per share shows which source dominates. The pair shows whether per-share equity grew from earnings or from issuance.

### SBC / Revenue

**Why read the two together.** SBC / Revenue measures stock-based compensation as a share of sales, meaning the value of shares paid to employees relative to revenue. Those share grants flow into the contributed layer, so SBC is one source of its growth. Reading the two together connects the pay expense to the dilution it creates on the balance sheet. The pair shows how employee stock pay builds the contributed layer.

**What the combination tells you.** A high SBC / Revenue paired with a steadily rising contributed layer means employee stock pay is a real and continuing source of dilution. A low SBC / Revenue with a static layer means dilution from pay is minor. The pair shows how much the contributed layer's growth comes from paying employees in stock.

### Common Stock Issued

**Why read the two together.** Common Stock Issued is the cash-flow line that records the money raised from selling shares in a period. The contributed layer on the balance sheet is the running total those issuances build. Reading the flow alongside the stock shows which years drove the layer's growth. The pair connects each raise to its mark on the balance sheet.

**What the combination tells you.** Large amounts in Common Stock Issued explain step-ups in the contributed layer, identifying the years of major capital raises. Years with little issuance explain a flat layer. The pair shows the timing and size of the raises behind the contributed total.

## Part 8: Limitations

Common stock splits par value and additional paid-in capital in a way that carries no economic meaning, so the two must be read together. The buyback treatment also varies, with some companies recording treasury stock and others retiring shares directly, so the row's optics differ across companies. The true and full ledger of contributions and repurchases lives in the statement of changes in equity, not on the face of the balance sheet.

The layer is historical, recording contributions at the values of the moments they were made, so it does not reflect current market value. Common stock alone is only half the equity story, because its meaning depends on the retained earnings beside it. The figure says nothing about the quality of the business, only about how it was funded. Common stock is the record of contributed capital and the dilution ledger, read against the earned layer rather than on its own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is the ratio of contributed capital to retained earnings, and what does it say about when the company began funding itself?
2. Which capital raises and vintages of share grants built the contributed layer over the years on the chart?
3. How does the contributed share of assets read the company's maturation as the layer recedes against growth?
4. Does the company record buybacks as treasury stock or retire shares directly, and how does that change the row's optics?
5. What price did each capital raise fetch, and what dilution did existing owners accept?
6. How does the pace of stock-based compensation building the layer compare with the offsetting effect of buybacks?
7. How does the contributed layer compare with peers that share the same funding history, bootstrapped against repeatedly raised?
8. What does the full statement of changes in equity show that the net balance-sheet figure hides?
9. Has the enterprise effectively repaid its contributions through cumulative distributions to shareholders?
10. Is the company likely to reach for new contributions, or is its self-funding sufficient for its strategy?
