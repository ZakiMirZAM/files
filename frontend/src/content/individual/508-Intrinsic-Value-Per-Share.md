# Intrinsic Value per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Intrinsic value per share is the model's estimate of what one share is worth. Intrinsic means the worth that comes from the underlying business, rather than the price the market happens to quote. The estimate is the number an investor compares against the market price to decide whether a share is cheap or dear. A share priced well below its intrinsic value is the kind of buy this app is built to find.

Intrinsic value per share is the final output of the valuation chain. The model first values the whole operating business as enterprise value, then adjusts to equity value, the part that belongs to shareholders. Dividing that equity value by the number of shares gives the worth of a single share. Enterprise value is the worth of the operating business, and equity value is the shareholders' slice of it after lenders are paid.

How the model-based value compares with the market price:

| Situation | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Intrinsic value well above the market price | The model sees the share as undervalued, leaving a margin of safety. |
| Intrinsic value near the market price | The model and the market roughly agree on the share's worth. |
| Intrinsic value below the market price | The model sees the share as expensive, with no cushion for error. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Intrinsic Value per Share = Equity Value ÷ Diluted Shares`

Equity value is the top number. Equity value is the worth of the business that belongs to common shareholders, after lenders and other prior claimants are paid. Equity value comes from the earlier step, where cash is added to enterprise value and debt is taken away. The whole point of dividing is to turn that group figure into a value for one owner.

Diluted shares are the bottom number. The diluted share count is the number of shares that would exist if all options and convertible securities were turned into stock. Options are rights held by employees or investors to buy shares at a set price. Convertible securities are bonds or preferred shares that can be swapped for common stock. Counting these possible extra shares gives a fuller and more cautious share count than the plain shares outstanding.

| Share count | What it includes | Effect on the value |
|---|---|---|
| Basic shares | Only the shares issued today | Higher per-share value, because the count is smaller |
| Diluted shares (this app) | Today's shares plus options and convertibles | Lower and more cautious per-share value |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Intrinsic value per share is the one figure that can be set directly against a stock quote. Enterprise value and equity value describe the whole business, but neither can be compared to a share price. Only a per-share number lines up with what the market shows.

Intrinsic value per share also turns a valuation into a decision. A share trading below its intrinsic value offers a cushion, while a share trading above it offers none. The comparison is the heart of value investing.

Intrinsic value per share lets very different companies be judged on one scale. A giant company and a small one cannot be compared by total equity value, since size alone dominates. Per share, both reduce to the same question, namely whether one share is worth more than its price.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Every driver of equity value flows into the per-share figure, since equity value is the top number. A stronger cash-flow forecast or a lower discount rate raises equity value and therefore the per-share value. New debt lowers equity value and drags the per-share figure down with it.

The share count is the other lever. A buyback shrinks the share count and lifts the value of each remaining share, even when the business is unchanged. A new share issue or a wave of option exercises swells the count and dilutes each share. Because the app uses the diluted count, a large overhang of unexercised options lowers the per-share value before those options are ever used.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The per-share value itself is not comparable across companies, because share counts and price levels differ. What matters by sector is how reliable the estimate is and how much the share count distorts it.

| Industry type | What to watch in the per-share value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Firms with heavy option pay such as technology | Diluted count well above the basic count | Generous option grants add many potential shares, lowering the honest per-share value. |
| Capital-heavy firms such as utilities | Value swings with the discount rate | Long-lived assets make the estimate sensitive to the required return. |
| Cyclical firms such as miners and carmakers | Value swings with the forecast base year | Forecast cash flow depends on the cycle, so the estimate moves with the starting point. |
| Firms with convertible bonds | Hidden shares in the diluted count | Convertible bonds can turn into stock, raising the share count and cutting the value. |
| Banks and insurers | Underlying bridge is unreliable | The debt-and-cash adjustments behind equity value do not fit these firms, so the per-share figure inherits that weakness. |

A per-share value from one company should never be compared with a per-share value from another as if the larger number were the better bargain. The only fair comparison is each company's intrinsic value against its own market price.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in intrinsic value per share is the healthiest pattern, especially when it comes from a growing business rather than a shrinking share count. A rise built only on buybacks is thinner, because the business itself may be flat. A steady decline usually means falling forecast cash flow, rising debt, or a swelling share count.

A sudden step in the per-share line often traces to a change in assumptions rather than the business. A new discount rate or terminal growth rate can move the figure in one jump. Compare the per-share line with the share-count line over the same years. A per-share value that rises only because shares are being retired tells a different story from one that rises because equity value is growing.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Margin of Safety

**Why read the two together.** Margin of safety is the gap between intrinsic value per share and the market price, stated as a percentage of intrinsic value. Intrinsic value per share is one of the two inputs to that gap. Without an intrinsic value, there is no margin of safety to measure.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide margin of safety means the market price sits well below the model's estimate, leaving room for error in the assumptions. A thin or negative margin means the price already meets or beats the estimate, so any disappointment hits the shareholder. Reading the two together turns a valuation into a clear buy-or-wait signal.

### Equity Value

**Why read the two together.** Equity value is the whole amount that belongs to shareholders. Intrinsic value per share is that amount split across the diluted share count. The per-share figure cannot exist until equity value is fixed.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising equity value does not guarantee a rising per-share value, because new shares can absorb the gain. Comparing the two shows whether growth is reaching each owner or leaking away through dilution. When equity value climbs and the share count holds, each share genuinely gains worth.

### EPS

**Why read the two together.** EPS, short for earnings per share, is the company's yearly profit divided by the share count. Intrinsic value per share is the estimated worth of a share, while EPS is the yearly profit behind one share. Both rest on the same denominator, the share count, so both react to buybacks and dilution.

**What the combination tells you.** Dividing intrinsic value per share by EPS gives an implied earnings multiple, which shows how many years of current profit the estimate is paying for. A very high implied multiple means the value depends on strong future growth rather than today's earnings. Comparing the implied multiple with the market's actual price-to-earnings multiple shows where the model and the market disagree.

### Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Book value per share is the accounting value of equity divided by the share count, drawn from the balance sheet. Intrinsic value per share is a forward-looking estimate from projected cash flow. The two describe the same share from different angles, one from recorded history and one from the expected future.

**What the combination tells you.** An intrinsic value far above book value per share means the model expects the business to earn well above the value of its recorded assets. An intrinsic value near or below book value suggests the model sees little worth beyond the assets already on the books. The gap between the two hints at how much of the value rests on future performance rather than existing assets.

## Part 8: Limitations

Intrinsic value per share is the last link in a long chain, so it collects every error made along the way. The estimate is only as good as the cash-flow forecast and the discount rate behind it, and small changes in either can move the per-share figure sharply. A precise-looking number can rest on very uncertain assumptions.

The share count adds its own uncertainty. The diluted count depends on how options and convertibles are treated, and different methods give different answers. A company can also change its share count quickly through buybacks or new issues, so a value that looks right today can shift tomorrow. The figure is a single point estimate rather than a range, so it can suggest more precision than the underlying forecast supports. A sensible reader treats intrinsic value per share as the middle of a range, and demands a margin of safety before acting.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How much would the intrinsic value per share change if the discount rate were raised by one percentage point?
2. Is the intrinsic value driven mainly by the forecast years or by the terminal value far in the future?
3. How large is the gap between the basic share count and the diluted count, and what causes the difference?
4. Does the diluted share count include all options and convertible securities, or could hidden shares still dilute the value?
5. Has the share count been falling through buybacks or rising through new issues over the past several years?
6. What margin of safety does the current market price leave against this intrinsic value?
7. Is the intrinsic value above or below book value per share, and what does that say about how much rests on future growth?
8. Which single assumption, if wrong, would most damage the intrinsic value per share?
9. Does the intrinsic value per share assume buybacks or dilution that management has not actually committed to?
10. For this company's sector, is the underlying valuation bridge reliable, or does the business resemble a bank or insurer where the method strains?
