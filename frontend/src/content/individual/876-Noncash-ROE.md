# Noncash ROE

## Part 1: Definition

Noncash ROE measures the return the operating business earns on shareholders' money, with the company's cash pile removed from both sides of the calculation. Return on equity, or ROE, is profit divided by book equity. Book equity is the accounting value of shareholders' ownership. A company holding a large cash balance mixes two very different investments into that one ratio: an operating business that may earn 20 percent and a cash account that earns a few percent. Noncash ROE separates them and reports the operating business alone.

The ratio answers one question: how good is the business itself, ignoring the cash it happens to sit on? The engine uses the median of the yearly ratios across the available history rather than a single year, so one unusual year cannot set the figure. The result drives the equity-track growth formula, where growth equals the equity reinvestment rate times noncash ROE.

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.08 | The operating business earns less than shareholders could demand elsewhere. |
| 0.08 to 0.15 | An ordinary business earning near its cost of equity. |
| 0.15 to 0.25 | A good business with a real competitive advantage. |
| Above 0.25 | An exceptional franchise, or a book equity shrunken by buybacks and writedowns. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Noncash ROE = (Net Income − Interest Income × (1 − Tax Rate)) ÷ (Book Equity − Cash And Short-Term Investments)`

The top number is noncash net income. Interest income is what the cash and securities earned during the year, and multiplying by one minus the tax rate converts it to its after-tax amount. Subtracting it leaves the profit the operating business produced on its own. The tax rate used is the company's effective rate, meaning tax expense divided by pre-tax income.

The bottom number is noncash book equity: total shareholders' equity minus cash and short-term investments. The subtraction must happen on both sides for the ratio to make sense. The cash produced the interest income, so removing the income while keeping the cash would divide operating profit by a base that includes a non-operating asset. Removing both leaves operating profit over operating equity, which is the matched pair the ratio needs. The engine computes the ratio for each available year and takes the median.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The plain ROE of a cash-rich company is dragged down by the cash. A business earning 25 percent on its operations can report a 15 percent ROE simply because a third of its equity sits in low-yield deposits. An analyst reading the plain ratio would call the business mediocre when only the balance sheet is conservative. Noncash ROE removes that distortion.

The correction matters most inside the growth formula. Reinvested equity goes into the operating business, not into the cash account, so the return that reinvestment earns is the noncash return. Funding a growth forecast with the diluted plain ROE understates what reinvestment actually produces.

The ratio also makes companies comparable. Two identical businesses with different cash policies show different plain ROEs and identical noncash ROEs. The second comparison is the one that says which business is better.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Operating profitability moves the ratio first, through margins and turnover just as with plain ROE. The cash adjustment adds its own movers. A large buyback funded from cash shrinks the denominator's subtraction and the equity at the same time, and the ratio can jump without any operating change. A large equity raise parked in cash leaves the ratio untouched by design, which is exactly the point.

Accounting choices matter at the edges. Interest income must be separately disclosed for the top adjustment to work, and a filer that nets it into other income hides part of the correction. Writedowns shrink book equity and inflate the ratio for years afterward. The median across years protects the figure from any single distorted year.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cash-rich technology companies | Noncash ROE far above plain ROE | Large securities portfolios dilute the plain ratio; the adjustment removes them. |
| Mature industrials | Small gap between the two ratios | Modest cash balances leave little to correct. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Interest-earning assets are the business itself, so stripping them removes the company. Financials are valued on dividends instead. |
| Companies with negative noncash equity | Not computable | Buybacks can push equity below the cash balance, and the ratio has no base to divide by. |
| Young loss-makers | Not meaningful | Negative profit makes any ROE variant unreadable. |

A cross-sector comparison is fair only among non-financial companies where the ratio computes on a positive base.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady noncash ROE across years is the signature of a durable franchise, and it is the single best support for assuming the return persists into the forecast. A declining series means competition is reaching the operating business itself, which no cash policy can hide. A rising series deserves a check of the denominator before celebration, since shrinking equity from buybacks lifts the ratio without any operating improvement.

Compare the series against the plain ROE series. A widening gap between the two means cash is accumulating, and the question shifts to why the company cannot deploy it.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** Plain ROE is the same ratio without the cash correction, so the pair isolates exactly what the correction does. The gap between the two is the drag or flattery contributed by the cash position. Reading both prevents attributing a balance-sheet effect to the business.

**What the combination tells you.** Noncash ROE well above plain ROE marks a strong business hidden behind a conservative cash pile. The two ratios close together mean the plain ratio can be trusted as an operating measure. Plain ROE above noncash ROE is rare and usually signals unusual items inside interest income or a distorted equity base worth investigating.

### Cash Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Cash per share sizes the cash pile that this ratio strips out. The larger the cash position relative to the company, the more the plain ROE misleads and the more this correction matters. The two metrics together show both the size of the distortion and the corrected result.

**What the combination tells you.** High cash per share with a high noncash ROE describes a strong business choosing to hold safety. High cash per share with a low noncash ROE removes the excuse: the business is weak even before the cash drag. Low cash per share means the two ROE variants should nearly agree, and a large gap flags a data problem.

### Interest Income

**Why read the two together.** Interest income is the exact line subtracted from the numerator, so its size shows how much of reported profit the cash produced. A company can grow reported earnings simply because rates rose on its deposits. This pairing separates rate luck from operating progress.

**What the combination tells you.** Interest income that is a large share of net income means reported earnings overstate the business, and the noncash ratio corrects a big distortion. A negligible interest line means the correction is cosmetic. Interest income rising while noncash ROE falls is the clearest sign that the operating business is deteriorating behind a growing cash cushion.

## Part 8: Limitations

The ratio depends on disclosure quality. A filer that nets interest income into other income lines defeats the numerator adjustment, and the ratio then only corrects the denominator. The cash figure also mixes true idle cash with working cash the operations need, and stripping all of it slightly flatters a business that must keep cash on hand to operate.

Book equity carries its usual accounting scars. Buybacks and writedowns can shrink the base until the ratio turns extreme or incomputable, and a negative noncash equity produces no ratio at all. The median across years steadies the figure but also means the ratio reacts slowly to a genuine recent change in the business.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How large is the gap between noncash ROE and plain ROE here, and how big is the cash position that explains the gap?
2. Is interest income separately disclosed, or is part of the correction lost in a netted other-income line?
3. How stable is the yearly noncash ROE series that the median summarizes, and does the latest year sit near or far from that median?
4. Has book equity been shrunk by buybacks or writedowns that flatter the ratio without operating improvement?
5. What return does the cash itself earn after tax, and how much of reported profit growth came from rising rates rather than the business?
6. Does the noncash ROE justify the growth rate the equity-track valuation assumes when multiplied by the equity reinvestment rate?
7. How does the company's noncash ROE compare with direct competitors after their own cash positions are stripped?
8. If the ratio is unusually high, is the cause franchise strength or a denominator hollowed out by financial engineering?
9. Why does the company hold its current cash level, and what would redeploying the excess do to the plain ROE?
