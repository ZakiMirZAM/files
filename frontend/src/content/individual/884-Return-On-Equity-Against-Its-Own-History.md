# Return on Equity Against Its Own History

## Part 1: Definition

The five year average return on equity is the mean of a company's return on equity across the last five reported financial years. Placed beside the current year, the pair answers one question: is the return the company earned this year the level the business normally earns, or is this year a departure from that level?

The current figure and the average are not rivals. Each answers a different question. The current figure reports what happened last year. The average reports what the business has tended to produce through good conditions and bad. The distance between the two is the finding.

| Reading | What the reading suggests |
|---|---|
| Current close to the average | The latest year is representative. The average adds little |
| Current well above the average | Either the business improved, or the year was flattered |
| Current well below the average | Either the business deteriorated, or the year was depressed |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Five year average ROE = (ROE₁ + ROE₂ + ROE₃ + ROE₄ + ROE₅) ÷ 5`

Each yearly return on equity is net income divided by shareholders' equity for that year. The gap is then:

`Gap = Current ROE − Five year average ROE`

The gap is reported in percentage points, because it is the difference between two percentages rather than a percentage change.

This app reports the median alongside the mean. The mean moves when any single year is extreme. The median does not, so a wide distance between the mean and the median is itself a signal that one year in the window is unusual.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used

A single year of return on equity is one observation of a business that operates across a cycle. Reading one year alone treats a peak as permanent and a trough as terminal.

Returns on equity also revert. A high return draws competitors toward the business and a low return drives capital away from it, so the return tends to pull back toward the middle unless something blocks that process. The average is a first estimate of where the return is being pulled.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The numerator moves with the operating business, with the tax rate, and with any item that does not repeat. The denominator moves for reasons that have nothing to do with performance at all.

Shareholders' equity falls when a company repurchases its own stock and rises when a company issues stock. Equity also falls when a company writes down an asset. Any of these moves the return on equity without the underlying business changing, and any of these can break the comparison between the current year and the average, because the two are then measured on different bases.

Leverage deserves separate attention. Debt shrinks the equity base without improving the business, so a company that borrows more will report a higher return on equity on identical operations.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

An average carries no direction. A company whose return fell in every year of the window can post the same average as a company whose return rose in every year. Read the series before reading the average.

A window that opens or closes inside a boom moves the average without saying anything about the company. Check where the five years sit in the cycle before treating the average as normal.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return on Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is measured after the effect of borrowing, and return on invested capital is measured before it. The distance between the two is what leverage contributed. A company whose return on equity has climbed while its return on invested capital has stayed flat has not improved its operations. It has borrowed.

**What the combination tells you.** When both returns rise together, the operating business improved and the equity return is earned. When only the equity return rises, the gain came from the balance sheet, and it carries the risk that borrowing carries.

### Financial Leverage

**Why read the two together.** Financial leverage is assets divided by equity, and it is the exact multiplier that turns a return on assets into a return on equity. Reading it beside the return on equity tells you how much of the return is the multiplier and how much is the business underneath.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising return on equity alongside rising leverage is a warning, because the same multiplier that lifts returns in good years deepens losses in bad ones. A rising return on equity alongside flat or falling leverage is the stronger result.

## Part 8: Limitations

An average across five years assumes the five years describe one business. A company that made a large acquisition, sold a division, or wrote down a substantial asset inside the window is not one business across it, and the average then blends periods that should not be blended.

The average is also silent on why. It reports that the current year differs from the norm without saying whether the business changed or the accounting did. That question belongs to the reader, and the questions beneath this definition are where it is answered.
