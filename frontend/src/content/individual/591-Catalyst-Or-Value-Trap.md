# Catalyst or Value Trap

## What it is

A stock can trade below its intrinsic value for a long time. Intrinsic value is what a business is truly worth based on the cash the business can produce over its life. A stock is cheap when its price sits below intrinsic value. A low price on its own does not tell you which of two very different situations you are looking at.

The first situation is a value trap. A value trap is a stock that looks cheap on the numbers but is cheap for a good reason. The business may be shrinking, the profits may be fading, or the value inside the company may be impossible for an outside owner to reach. In a value trap the low price is honest. The price is low because the business is worth less than it used to be, and a value trap can stay cheap for years.

The second situation is a catalyst. A catalyst is a specific event that forces the market to recognize value that is already there. Common catalysts include several kinds of events, each defined below.

1. A spinoff happens when a parent company separates one of its divisions into a new independent company. The new company's shares usually go to the parent's existing owners. Once the division stands alone, the market can price the division on its own strengths.
2. A restructuring happens when a company reorganizes its operations or its finances. A restructuring can mean selling a weak division, closing a money-losing unit, or paying down debt.
3. New management is a change in the people who run the company. A capable new team can fix problems that the old team created or ignored.
4. A capital return is cash paid out to owners. A capital return can take the form of a dividend or a buyback of the company's own shares.
5. A margin recovery is a rise in profit measured as a share of sales, back toward a normal level after a weak stretch.

## Catalyst versus value trap

| Question | Catalyst | Value trap |
|---|---|---|
| What is it | An event that closes the gap between price and value | A stock that is cheap for a good reason and stays cheap |
| Why is the price low | The market has not yet priced in a coming change | The business is deteriorating or the value cannot be realized |
| What happens next | A specific event pushes the price toward intrinsic value | The price stays low or falls further as the business weakens |
| What is your job | Name the event and judge how likely it is | Prove that the cheapness is real rather than a warning |
| How long does it take | The gap can close once the event arrives | The wait can last for years with no reward |

## Why it matters for valuation

Howard Marks makes a point that every bargain hunter needs to hear. The asset that the market regards as the worst is often the cheapest. Cheapness and low quality often appear together, because low quality is what scares buyers away. A low price is therefore a fact about the market rather than a reason to buy.

Marks also explains why a catalyst matters. When the market works properly, price moves toward intrinsic value over time. A catalyst is what makes that convergence happen on a schedule instead of someday. Without a catalyst a cheap stock can stay cheap long past the point where the investor runs out of patience.

For valuation this distinction changes what a low price means. A cheap price paired with a real catalyst is an opportunity. A cheap price with no catalyst and a fading business is a trap that destroys capital slowly.

A disciplined investor asks three questions before buying anything cheap. The first question is why the stock is cheap. The second question is what event would close the gap between price and value. The third question is what single fact would prove the situation is a trap.

## Common traps

Careless investors fall into a familiar set of errors when a stock looks cheap. The list below names the most common ones.

1. Treating a low price as proof of value. A low price shows only that other investors are afraid. The price says nothing about whether the fear is justified.
2. Buying without a catalyst. A stock with no event on the horizon can stay cheap for years. The wait can cost more than the discount was ever worth.
3. Inventing a catalyst that does not exist. Hope is not a catalyst. A real catalyst is a specific event with a real chance of happening rather than a wish that the market will change its mind.
4. Ignoring why the business is cheap. Every cheap stock has a reason. The disciplined investor names the reason first, then decides whether the reason is temporary or permanent.
5. Skipping the disproof test. Ask what single fact would prove the situation is a trap. An investor who cannot name that fact does not yet understand the risk being taken.
