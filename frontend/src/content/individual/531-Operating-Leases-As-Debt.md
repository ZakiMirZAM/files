# Operating Leases as Debt

## Part 1: Definition

An operating lease is a rental contract for an asset the company uses but does not own. A leased store, warehouse, or aircraft is a common example. Under an operating lease the company pays a fixed rent for years, yet older accounting rules kept that promise off the balance sheet and reported the rent as an ordinary operating cost. Treating operating leases as debt reverses that choice. The method values the stream of future lease payments as a loan and adds that value to the company's reported debt.

The debt value of the leases is the present value of the future lease payments. Present value is today's worth of a set of future payments, found by discounting each payment back at an interest rate. This adjustment answers one question: how much would the company owe if every signed lease were counted as borrowed money?

Common readings of the adjustment:

| Size of the lease adjustment | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The company owns most of its assets or rents very little. Reported debt already reflects true leverage. |
| Small next to reported debt | Leases matter only at the margin. The leverage picture changes just a little. |
| About the same as reported debt | Leasing is a real second source of funding. Conventional debt alone understates leverage. |
| Larger than reported debt | Leases are the main way the company finances its assets. The balance sheet badly understates leverage. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Debt Value of Leases = Present Value of Future Lease Payments`

The future lease payments are the amounts the company has already promised to pay over the remaining life of its leases. A company discloses these commitments year by year in the notes to its financial statements. The payments belong in the calculation because a signed lease is a fixed obligation the company cannot walk away from. That fixed, unavoidable quality is the defining feature of debt.

The present value converts those future payments into one number in today's money. Each payment is discounted at the company's pretax cost of debt, the interest rate the company would pay on a new loan before any tax saving. A payment due far in the future is worth less today, so distant lease payments add less to the debt value than near ones. The resulting debt value is then added to conventional debt, such as bank loans and bonds, to give a fuller measure of total debt.

Two ways exist to estimate the debt value:

| Method | How it works | When it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Discounted lease schedule | Each disclosed future payment is discounted at the pretax cost of debt, then summed. | The precise method, used when the company publishes its payment schedule. |
| Multiple of annual rent | The yearly rent is multiplied by a fixed factor, often around eight. | A quick estimate when the full schedule is not available. |

This app uses the present value of the disclosed lease commitments, the same figure companies now report as a lease liability.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A company that rents its stores and a company that buys its stores with borrowed money run almost the same economics. Both have locked in fixed payments for years. Counting only conventional debt would make the renter look far less indebted than the buyer, even though the two carry similar fixed obligations. Adding the lease debt puts the two companies on the same footing.

The adjustment also keeps returns honest. When leases become debt, operating income is raised by the imputed interest, the interest portion buried inside the rent. Operating income is the profit the business earns from normal operations before interest and taxes. Raising it this way measures the return on all the capital the business uses, including the leased assets, not only the owned ones.

A truer leverage figure changes how risky the company looks. A firm with no reported borrowing can still be heavily committed through its leases. Lenders and investors who ignore the lease debt would understate the chance the company struggles to meet its fixed payments in a bad year.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move the debt value of the leases. The first is the size of the lease commitments. A company that signs more leases, or renews at higher rents, raises the future payments and so raises the debt value. The second is the length of the leases. Longer leases stretch payments over more years and add more total obligation. The third is the discount rate. A higher pretax cost of debt shrinks the present value, because future payments are discounted more heavily. A company whose credit weakens sees its cost of debt rise, which can lower the reported lease debt even as the underlying commitments grow.

Accounting choices also shift the number. The disclosed lease schedule often stops at a single lump sum for all years beyond the fifth, so a judgment about how long that lump sum lasts affects the total. Companies with mostly short renewable leases show a smaller debt value than their true long-term commitment. A recent shift from owning assets to leasing them raises the lease debt sharply without any change in the size of the business.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The lease adjustment matters most where companies rent the assets they operate. Asset-heavy service businesses lean on leases. Asset owners barely use them.

| Industry type | Typical lease adjustment | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Retailers and restaurant chains | Very large | Hundreds of leased stores mean lease debt can dwarf reported debt. |
| Airlines | Very large | Aircraft and terminal space are often leased rather than owned. |
| Logistics and shipping | Large | Warehouses, depots, and vehicles are commonly leased. |
| Manufacturers and energy producers | Small to moderate | Most plant and equipment is owned and already financed with reported debt. |
| Software and other asset-light firms | Small | Apart from an office lease, few long-term assets are rented. |
| Banks and insurers | Usually minor | Leverage comes from deposits and policies, not from leased assets. |

A cross-sector comparison of reported debt is meaningless until leases are capitalized. Comparing a retailer's stated debt with an airline's stated debt ignores the leases that dominate both. Convert leases to debt first, then compare.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in the lease debt means the company is expanding its rented footprint by opening stores or leasing more equipment. Rising lease debt is not automatically bad. The right question is whether the new leased assets earn more than they cost. A steady fall means the company is buying assets outright, letting leases expire, or shrinking.

A sudden step up usually marks a large new lease program or a change in how leases are measured. The 2019 rule change that pulled most leases onto the balance sheet created exactly such a step for many companies, with no real change in the business. When you compare the lease debt across years, check that the same measurement method was used throughout. That check keeps a rule change from being mistaken for a strategic shift.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity divides total debt by shareholders' equity to show how much of the company relies on borrowing. Shareholders' equity is the owners' stake in the company. The ratio uses only conventional debt unless leases are added, so capitalizing the leases raises the debt figure. The true debt to equity is therefore higher than the reported one for any lease-heavy company.

**What the combination tells you.** A low reported debt to equity next to a large lease adjustment means the company is far more leveraged than its balance sheet first suggests. Recomputing the ratio with lease debt included reveals the real cushion between the company and its lenders. Two firms with identical reported ratios can carry very different real leverage once their leases are counted.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** WACC, the weighted average cost of capital, is the blended annual cost of the company's debt and equity funding. The debt weight in that blend depends on how much debt the company carries. Adding the lease debt raises the debt weight, which usually lowers the WACC because debt costs less than equity.

**What the combination tells you.** Leaving leases out of the WACC understates the debt weight and overstates the cost of capital for a lease-heavy firm. Including the lease debt gives a discount rate that reflects how the business is really financed. A lower, lease-adjusted WACC raises the estimated value of the company's future cash flows.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest coverage divides operating income by interest expense to show how many times earnings can pay the year's interest. Once leases become debt, the rent hides an interest portion that belongs in interest expense. A complete coverage figure adds the imputed lease interest to the reported interest bill.

**What the combination tells you.** A coverage ratio built on reported interest alone flatters a company that finances itself through leases. Adding the lease interest to the bottom of the ratio lowers the coverage and shows the real burden of the fixed payments. Reading the two together separates a genuinely low-debt firm from one that has simply moved its borrowing into lease form.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net Debt / EBITDA compares total debt minus cash against a year of earnings before interest, taxes, and non-cash charges. The ratio counts only conventional debt unless the lease debt is added in. Including the lease debt in the top of the ratio raises it for any company that leases heavily.

**What the combination tells you.** A comfortable-looking Net Debt / EBITDA can turn stretched once the lease debt joins the total. Recomputing the ratio with leases included shows the real number of years of earnings needed to clear all fixed obligations. The gap between the reported and lease-adjusted ratio is largest in the sectors that rent most of their assets.

## Part 8: Limitations

The debt value of the leases is only an estimate. The disclosed lease schedule lumps distant payments into a single figure, so the present value rests on a judgment about how long those payments run. A different discount rate or a different guess about the lump sum can move the debt value noticeably. The adjustment also assumes the company cannot escape its leases, yet some leases carry cancellation options that make them less binding than ordinary debt.

The method can overstate leverage for a business whose leases are short and easily dropped. Capitalizing leases changes several figures at once. Reported debt, operating income, and invested capital all move together. Invested capital is the total funding in the business from both debt and equity. A single ratio viewed in isolation can therefore mislead. The adjustment says nothing about the quality of the leased assets or whether the leased stores actually earn their rent. Treating leases as debt is a better starting point than ignoring them, not a final verdict on the company.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How large is the debt value of the leases next to the company's reported debt? Does counting the leases change the company from lightly indebted to heavily indebted?
2. What share of the company's assets is leased rather than owned? Is that share rising or falling over the years shown?
3. Do the leases carry cancellation options, or is the company locked in for the full term? How binding are the fixed payments in a weak year?
4. Was any recent jump in lease debt caused by real expansion, or by the 2019 accounting change that moved leases onto the balance sheet?
5. What pretax cost of debt was used to discount the lease payments? Would a higher rate meaningfully lower the reported lease debt?
6. After adding the lease debt, does the company's debt to equity still look conservative next to direct competitors?
7. Do the leased assets, such as new stores, earn a return above the cost of the leases? Or is the company expanding its rented footprint without adding value?
8. How long do the lease commitments run? How much of the total comes from the estimated lump sum beyond year five?
9. If rents rise at renewal, how much would the lease debt and the fixed payments grow?
10. Does the company rely on leases far more than its peers, and if so, is that a deliberate strategy or a way to keep reported debt low?
