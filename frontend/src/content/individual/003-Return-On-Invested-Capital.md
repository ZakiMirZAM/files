# Return On Invested Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Return on invested capital, or ROIC, measures how much after-tax operating profit a company earns for every dollar of capital invested in the business. The capital comes from two groups. Lenders supply debt, and shareholders supply equity. ROIC asks how well the business turns all of that money into profit.

An ROIC of 15 percent means the company earns fifteen cents of after-tax operating profit for every dollar invested in operations. The source of the dollar does not matter to the ratio. A dollar from a lender and a dollar from a shareholder are treated the same way. The ratio measures the quality of the business, not the way the business is funded.

Common readings of the ratio:

| ROIC level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 5 percent | The return sits below a typical cost of capital. The business is likely destroying value as it grows. |
| 5 to 8 percent | The return is near or just under a typical cost of capital. Value creation is thin or absent. |
| 8 to 12 percent | The return clears a typical cost of capital by a modest margin. Acceptable for most established companies. |
| 12 to 20 percent | A strong return that comfortably beats a typical cost of capital. The app's quality score awards a pass at 12 percent or higher. |
| Above 20 percent | An elite return. The useful question becomes whether the advantage producing it can last. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Invested Capital`

NOPAT is the top number. NOPAT stands for net operating profit after tax. NOPAT is operating income multiplied by one minus the tax rate. Operating income is the profit the business earns from normal operations before interest and taxes. NOPAT is the profit the business earns before any financing cost, with taxes removed. Net income is not used here, because net income already subtracts interest. Using net income would mix financing into a ratio meant to ignore it.

Invested capital is the bottom number. Invested capital is the total money put to work in the business. Invested capital is debt plus equity. Debt is the money borrowed from lenders. Equity is the money supplied by shareholders. Excess cash is usually removed, because excess cash sits idle and is not used in operations. Including idle cash would unfairly lower the return of a cash-rich company. Goodwill is sometimes removed too. Goodwill is the premium an acquirer paid above the fair value of a purchased company's assets.

Two variants of invested capital exist:

| Variant | What the variant changes |
|---|---|
| With or without goodwill | Removing goodwill measures the return on the operating assets alone. Keeping goodwill measures the return on the full price paid, acquisitions included. |
| Average invested capital | Some sources use the average of the start and end balances for the year, rather than the year-end figure, so a large change during the year does not distort the ratio. |

The Valuation Handbook chapter 9 covers the economic profit approach. The chapter defines invested capital as net of excess cash. Marketable securities are also removed. Goodwill from acquisition premiums is removed as well.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

ROIC answers a question that return on equity cannot. Return on equity, or ROE, measures profit against shareholder money alone. ROE rises when a company borrows more, even if the underlying business has not improved. ROIC includes both debt and equity in the denominator and uses a profit figure measured before financing. ROIC therefore ignores how the business is financed. The result isolates the quality of the underlying business. ROE mixes in the effect of leverage.

Value creation has a precise meaning. A company creates value only when its ROIC is above its cost of capital. The cost of capital, often called WACC, is the blended return that lenders and shareholders require for supplying their money. When ROIC sits below that level, growth destroys value rather than building it. The app charts this comparison directly in the ROIC − WACC Spread tile.

A durable high ROIC is the clearest accounting sign of a competitive moat. In a competitive market, high returns attract rivals who compete the excess away. A return that stays high year after year suggests something structural is protecting the business.

## Part 4: What influences the output

ROIC is operating margin multiplied by capital efficiency. Capital efficiency means the sales a company generates per dollar of capital. Anything that moves either part moves the ratio. On the profit side, the drivers are pricing and input costs and operating discipline. The tax rate matters too, because NOPAT is an after-tax figure. A tax change shifts ROIC with no change in operations at all.

On the capital side, working capital and capital spending set the size of the denominator. Acquisitions matter most. Buying a company at a premium loads invested capital with goodwill. The goodwill swells the denominator and depresses ROIC, often sharply in the year of the deal. A large cash pile can distort the figure when cash is not excluded. A writedown shrinks the denominator and flatters every later year. One-off gains or losses inside operating income can move the top number for reasons unrelated to the ongoing business.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Capital intensity sets the baseline. Compare a company only against direct peers and against its own cost of capital.

| Industry type | Typical range or behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Asset-light software and services | Very high | The business needs little capital per dollar of profit, so the return runs high. |
| Branded consumer goods | High | Strong pricing and modest assets produce returns well above the cost of capital. |
| Industrials and manufacturers | Moderate | Heavy plant and equipment hold the return to the low or mid teens at best. |
| Utilities, telecom, and capital-heavy firms | Low, near the cost of capital | Regulators set returns close to the cost of capital by design, not by failure. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Debt is the raw material of the business, not financing, so ROIC does not apply. Use ROE instead. |
| Commodity and cyclical firms | Swings with the cycle | The return rises in booms and falls in slumps. Judge the figure across a full cycle. |

Comparing ROIC across different sectors is meaningless. A software firm and a utility are built on different amounts of capital. The right benchmark is always the company's own cost of capital and its direct peers.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Durability is the point of the chart. A steady high ROIC across many years is strong evidence of a durable advantage. A steady decline is the most important warning the chart can show. A fading return usually means competition is biting, or capital is growing faster than profit. A slide often follows a run of acquisitions, where new capital earns less than the old base did. Check the Marginal ROIC tile to see what the newest dollars are earning.

A step change usually marks an event. An acquisition, a divestiture, or a writedown can reset the level in a single year. High volatility points to a cyclical business whose return rises and falls with the economy. Base effects can mislead the reader. A large writedown shrinks invested capital, so the return looks better in every later year for a purely mechanical reason. A large acquisition does the reverse and depresses the return until the bought earnings grow into the enlarged capital base.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC − WACC Spread

**Why read the two together.** ROIC measures the return the business earns. WACC measures the return that lenders and shareholders require. The spread is ROIC minus WACC. The spread states value creation as a single number that the level of ROIC alone cannot show.

**What the combination tells you.** A positive spread means the company earns more than its capital costs, so growth builds value. A negative spread means growth destroys value, no matter how fast revenue rises. A wide and durable positive spread is the strongest quantitative sign of a moat. The spread, not the raw ROIC, decides whether the business should reinvest or return cash.

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** ROE measures profit against shareholder equity alone. ROIC measures profit against debt and equity together. ROIC isolates the quality of the business. ROE adds the effect of leverage on top of that quality.

**What the combination tells you.** A large gap between ROE and ROIC shows that borrowing is amplifying the return to shareholders. A small gap means the returns come from the business itself, not from debt. Leverage can lift ROE in good years and deepen losses in bad ones. Reading the pair separates business quality from financing risk.

### Reinvestment Rate

**Why read the two together.** The reinvestment rate is the share of profit the company puts back into the business. ROIC is the return that reinvested capital earns. ROIC multiplied by the reinvestment rate sets the growth rate that creates value.

**What the combination tells you.** A high ROIC paired with a high reinvestment rate is a compounding machine, because each retained dollar earns a high return and funds more growth. A high ROIC paired with little reinvestment signals limited room to grow, so the company should return cash instead. A low ROIC paired with heavy reinvestment is the worst case, because the company is funneling money into low-return projects.

### Marginal ROIC

**Why read the two together.** The reported ROIC is an average across all the capital ever invested. Marginal ROIC is the return on the newest capital alone. The marginal figure leads the average, because new investment shapes tomorrow's reported return.

**What the combination tells you.** A marginal ROIC above the average means recent investment is lifting the business, so the reported figure should rise over time. A marginal ROIC below the average means new capital is earning less than the old base, so the average will fade even while it still looks healthy. The marginal figure is the early warning that the average cannot give.

## Part 8: Limitations

Invested capital is a book figure. The figure rests on the historical cost of old assets, which can sit far below today's replacement cost. Goodwill from acquisitions inflates the figure, while writedowns shrink it. Operating profit is measured on an accrual basis, not on cash, so the ratio can overstate returns when a company books profit faster than it collects cash. Definitions of invested capital differ between sources, especially over the treatment of cash and goodwill and leases. The differences hurt comparability across data providers.

ROIC is meaningless for banks and insurers, because debt is their raw material rather than financing. A high ROIC on a tiny capital base can be fragile, since a small business can lose its edge quickly. ROIC also says nothing about the price paid for the stock. A wonderful business bought at a foolish price is still a poor investment.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ROIC driven more by high margins or by high capital efficiency? The Sales To Invested Capital tile splits the two directly, and the answer names the company's actual competitive weapon.
2. How much goodwill sits inside invested capital? What does ROIC look like on operating capital with the goodwill removed? The gap measures how much value past acquisitions must still justify.
3. How long has the ROIC stayed high? A return high for a decade is moat evidence. A return high for two years is an unproven stretch.
4. Does ROIC exceed the company's cost of capital? By how much, and for how many consecutive years? The ROIC − WACC Spread tile shows the gap.
5. What is the Marginal ROIC on capital deployed in the last three years? Why does the newest capital earn more or less than the company-wide average?
6. Is the effective tax rate behind NOPAT sustainable? Part of a high ROIC can be a temporary tax artifact rather than an operating result.
7. Has a recent acquisition or writedown reset the level? Check whether a step in the chart lines up with a deal or an impairment rather than real operating change.
8. How does ROIC compare against direct peers in the same sector? The company with a persistently higher return almost always owns a structural advantage worth naming.
9. How much more capital can the business absorb at returns near the current ROIC? Name the limit that eventually caps reinvestment, whether market size or regulation or management bandwidth.
10. Does the working capital trend show the capital base tightening or loosening? Track receivables and inventory and payables. Decide whether the movement is deliberate strategy or slippage.
