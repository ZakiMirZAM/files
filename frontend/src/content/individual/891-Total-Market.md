# Total Market

## Part 1: Definition

The total market is what customers spend in total today on everything that does the same job as the company's products. The job is the task the customer pays for, judged by what the customer buys rather than by the company's industry label. The total market therefore includes rival products and substitutes. A substitute is a product from another industry that performs the same function for the customer. The total market answers one question: how large could the company ever become?

The total market caps revenue. Revenue is the total sales the company records in a year, and revenue equals the company's share of its market times the size of that market. The share can never exceed 100 percent. A company whose revenue grows faster than its market year after year must end up holding the whole market, and then growth has to slow. The story valuation starts revenue from the market for exactly that reason, so the ceiling is visible from the first number.

How the market is drawn:

| How the market is drawn | What that usually means |
|---|---|
| Around one product or the industry label | Too small. The market leaves out substitutes that customers would switch to, so the ceiling understates how far the company can grow. |
| Around the job customers pay for, including the substitutes customers would actually move to | The right ceiling. The market holds every product that serves the same purpose on the same occasions. |
| Around every nearby spending category | Too large. The market counts spending the company cannot reach with its business model, which turns a ceiling into a hope. |
| Worldwide for a business that sells locally | Too large. Goods that are costly to ship, or that customers must travel to reach, form separate local markets. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Year-10 revenue = Total market today × (1 + Market growth)^10 × Share in year 10`

Total market today is the first input. The figure is a full amount in the same currency the company reports its revenue in. The figure comes from rivals' reported sales, from industry figures, or from a larger market of known size multiplied by the share of its customers who fit the market the story describes. When the story gives no usable size, the conversion falls back to the total revenue of the company's industry.

Market growth is the second input. Market growth is the yearly rate at which the whole market grows over the next ten years. The power of 10 in the formula compounds that yearly rate over ten years. The story chooses one of four sizes for the market in year 10, and the conversion turns each choice into a yearly rate. The risk-free rate is the base of every choice. The risk-free rate is the return on a government bond with no risk of default, and the rate stands in for the growth of the whole economy.

| The story says the market will | Yearly market growth used |
|---|---|
| Stay the same size apart from inflation | The risk-free rate |
| Grow by about a quarter over ten years | The risk-free rate plus 2.3 points |
| Grow by about half over ten years | The risk-free rate plus 4.1 points |
| Double over ten years | The risk-free rate plus 7.2 points |

The added points come straight from compounding. A market that ends ten years larger by a factor of 1.25 grows about 2.3 percent a year. A factor of 1.5 takes about 4.1 percent a year, and a factor of 2 takes about 7.2 percent a year.

Share in year 10 is the third input. The share is the part of the year-10 market that the company wins, stated as a fraction of the whole. A share of 0.2 means the company sells one fifth of everything the market buys.

The app uses the result in one specific way. The engine solves for the growth rate of years 1 to 5 that lands revenue exactly on the year-10 figure, with growth fading in equal steps over years 6 to 10 toward stable growth. The three inputs only work as a set. Revenue growth keeps the model's own number in three cases, and the conversion table names the rule. The three cases are a missing input, a share above 100 percent, and a year-10 figure that no growth rate can reach.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A growth rate on its own hides scale. Compounding is deceptive, because a yearly percentage that sounds modest can multiply revenue many times over ten years. Starting from the market turns the growth claim into a question anyone can check: whose spending will this revenue come from?

The total market also forces the story to say what business the company is really in. Choosing the business is often the single largest choice in a story, because the market sets the limit on everything that follows. A company described narrowly gets a small ceiling. The same company described as serving a wider need gets a larger one, and the valuation shows exactly how much of the value rests on that description.

The measure also guards against a common failure in hot markets. When many companies chase the same large market, each company can look plausible on its own while the group together is priced for more revenue than the market can ever hold. Stating the market and the share makes that collective overreach visible.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The largest influence is how the market is drawn. Adding a substitute or a nearby group of customers can double the figure without anything in the business changing. The test for what belongs inside is whether customers would move to an alternative in large numbers if the company raised its prices for a year, and whether the alternative serves the same purpose on the same occasions.

Geography moves the figure as well. A business that sells into one country or one city competes in a smaller market than the worldwide total for its product. Costs of shipping and customer travel split many markets into local pieces.

The year of measurement matters. A market measured in a boom year, or in a year of unusually high prices for a commodity, starts from an inflated base. The size is stated in money, so higher prices raise the figure even when the number of units sold has not changed.

The source matters too. Industry reports, rivals' filings and estimates built from a larger known market often disagree. The proof beside the number in the conversion table names the source used, and that source deserves a check before the number is trusted.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

| Industry type | How the market is usually drawn | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer staples and utilities | Close to the industry's own revenue, growing near the economy's pace | Most possible buyers already buy. Growth comes from population and prices rather than new customers. |
| Software and online platforms | Often drawn too wide | Claims of huge addressable markets are common. The part a company can reach with its business model is usually much smaller. |
| Marketplaces and intermediaries | Measured in fees, not in the full value of goods sold | The company's revenue is a fee on each sale. The market must be stated in the same terms as revenue, or the share overstates revenue. |
| Commodity producers | World demand at a price level | The market's size in money swings with the commodity price. A peak-year price inflates the ceiling. |
| Cement, restaurants, hospitals and other local businesses | Many separate local markets | Goods that are costly to ship, or services customers must travel to, limit each seller to a nearby area. |
| Banks, insurers and property trusts | Not used | The story valuation does not run for these companies, because the site's firm-level cash flow valuation does not apply to them. |

Comparing total markets across sectors is meaningless. A market's size in money says nothing about how much of the market any one company can win or keep.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app does not chart the total market, so read the company's revenue history against the market instead. Dividing each year's revenue by the market size gives the company's share of the market over time. A steadily rising share means the company is taking business from rivals. A flat share inside a growing market means the company is simply growing with its market.

A share that has already reached a large fraction of the market leaves little room for further gains, so future growth must come from the market itself. A share that jumps in a single year usually comes from an acquisition or from a change in how the market is measured rather than from winning customers.

Watch for base effects in the market figure. A market that expanded during a temporary surge in demand can shrink back, and a story that starts from the surge year assumes the surge is permanent. Compare the market today with its level several years earlier before treating today's size as normal.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Revenue is the part of the total market the company already holds. Dividing revenue by the market gives today's share, which is the starting point for the share in year 10. A story that moves the share far above today's level needs a reason that explains the jump.

**What the combination tells you.** A small share of a large market leaves room for growth without taking much from rivals. A large share of a slow market means revenue can only grow about as fast as the market grows. When the share in year 10 is several times today's share, the story is claiming that the company will win a large amount of business from named rivals, and the answer to the question about size should explain how.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** A larger market often comes with lower margins. Large markets attract more competitors, and a company that chases every customer in a wide market usually has to accept lower prices. The market and the margin are therefore two sides of the same choice about which business the company is in.

**What the combination tells you.** A story that draws a wide market and also keeps the margin of a narrow niche is usually inconsistent. A wide market paired with a margin near the industry median is the consistent version of a volume story. A narrow market paired with a high margin is the consistent version of an exclusive story, and each version can produce a similar value.

### DCF intrinsic value per share

**Why read the two together.** The intrinsic value per share in story mode rests directly on the revenue the total market allows. A higher market figure raises revenue in every forecast year and raises the terminal value. The pair shows how much of the value depends on the market the story drew.

**What the combination tells you.** When a modest change in the market figure moves the value a great deal, the market definition is the weakest part of the valuation and deserves the most checking. When the value barely moves, the margin and reinvestment carry more of the value than the market does. Testing the value with the market drawn one size narrower shows how much of the price is a bet on the wider description.

## Part 8: Limitations

The total market is an estimate rather than a reported figure. Sources disagree, definitions require judgment, and a market measured in money moves with prices as well as with demand. Two careful readers can draw the same company's market at very different sizes, and both can defend their choice.

The measure says nothing about who wins the market. A large market with many strong rivals can leave every company with a small share and a thin margin. The market also enters the revenue formula through three inputs that multiply together, so modest errors in the market, its growth and the share compound into a large error in year-10 revenue.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What does the customer pay the company to do, and does the market in the story cover that job rather than the industry label?
2. Which substitutes from other industries sit inside the market, and would customers really move to them if the company raised its prices?
3. Is the market measured in the same terms as the company's revenue, or does it count the full value of goods the company only takes a fee on?
4. What source gives the market size, and how far apart are the other available estimates?
5. Was the market measured in a boom year or at a peak price, and what was its size several years earlier?
6. What is today's share, found by dividing the latest revenue by the market, and how does the share in year 10 compare?
7. Which rivals lose the business that the share in year 10 requires, and what stops them from responding?
8. Are other companies priced as if they will win the same market, and could all of them succeed at once?
9. How does the value per share change when the market is drawn one size narrower?
