# Debt To Equity

## Part 1: Definition

The debt to equity ratio measures how much borrowed money a company uses for every dollar that the owners have put in. Debt is money the company has borrowed and must repay, such as bank loans and bonds. Equity is the owners' stake in the company, equal to everything the company owns minus everything the company owes. The ratio answers one question: how much of the company is funded by lenders compared with owners?

A ratio of 0.5 means the company uses fifty cents of debt for every dollar of owner money. A ratio of 2.0 means the company uses two dollars of debt for every dollar of owner money. A higher number means the company leans more on lenders, and lenders must be paid before owners receive anything.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.5 | The company relies mostly on owner money. Borrowing is light and the balance sheet is conservative. |
| 0.5 to 1.0 | A moderate mix of debt and equity. This app's quality score awards a pass at 1.00 or lower. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | Debt now exceeds owner money. The level is normal in steady industries and risky in volatile ones. |
| Above 2.0 | Heavy reliance on borrowing. A downturn or a rate increase can strain the company quickly. |
| Negative | Equity is below zero, usually after years of losses or large buybacks. The ratio stops carrying its normal meaning. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Debt To Equity = Total Debt ÷ Total Shareholders Equity`

Total debt is the top number. Total debt is the sum of all interest-bearing borrowings, including short-term loans, long-term bonds, and the borrowing portion of leases. Some versions of the ratio use total liabilities instead, which adds non-borrowing items such as supplier bills and deferred taxes. This app uses interest-bearing debt for the debt to equity tile and reports a separate liabilities to equity figure for the broader view.

Total shareholders equity is the bottom number. Total shareholders equity is the money owners have contributed plus all profit the company has kept over the years, minus any losses and any stock the company has bought back. Equity is an accounting figure rather than a market price. Equity can be reshaped by buybacks, writedowns, and losses. The bottom number rarely matches what the company would sell for.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The debt to equity ratio shows the shape of the funding mix in one number. A company funded mostly by owners can survive a bad year because owners do not have to be repaid on a schedule. A company funded mostly by lenders faces fixed payments that do not pause when earnings fall. The ratio states which of those two situations the company is in.

Borrowing can lift returns for owners when the business earns more on the borrowed money than the loans cost. The same borrowing magnifies losses when earnings fall, because the interest and repayments still come due. The ratio measures how far the company has pushed that trade-off.

Lenders watch the ratio because a thin equity layer means less cushion to absorb losses before the lenders themselves are at risk. A lower ratio usually earns the company easier access to credit and a lower borrowing cost.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Several forces move the ratio. Taking on new loans to fund acquisitions, dividends, or share buybacks raises the top number and pushes the ratio up. Repaying debt lowers the top number and pulls the ratio down. Retained profit builds equity over time and slowly lowers the ratio when debt holds steady.

Accounting choices can swing the bottom number sharply. A large share buyback reduces equity and can spike the ratio even when borrowing has not changed. An asset writedown cuts equity overnight and raises the ratio for reasons unrelated to new borrowing. Years of losses can drive equity below zero, at which point the ratio turns negative and loses its normal meaning. Equity reflects original cost rather than current value, so a company with valuable old assets can look more leveraged than the company truly is.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The acceptable level depends on how steady the company's cash flows are and how heavy its assets are. Steady cash flows and assets that can be pledged as security support more debt safely.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | Often above 1.0 | Cash flows are near certain and assets back the loans, so lenders accept more debt. |
| Banks and insurers | Very high by design | Borrowing is the raw material of the business, so the ordinary ratio does not apply. |
| Capital-light software and services | Often near zero | Few hard assets to pledge and volatile early earnings keep borrowing light. |
| Cyclical producers such as miners and automakers | Kept lower on purpose | Earnings can fall by half in a downturn, so a thinner debt layer is needed to survive. |
| Mature consumer companies | Moderate, often near 1.0 | Stable demand supports a balanced mix of debt and owner money. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two companies report equity on different bases or when one operates in a borrowing-driven industry such as banking. Compare a company against direct peers and against its own history.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A flat low line means management keeps borrowing modest year after year. A flat high line means management runs the business with heavy debt on purpose, often to lift owner returns. The pattern that deserves the most attention is a steady climb. A ratio that rises year after year means debt is growing faster than retained profit. The cause is usually acquisitions, buybacks, or weak earnings that fail to build equity.

A sudden jump in a single year is usually a buyback or a writedown rather than a real shift in borrowing. Check whether equity fell or debt rose to find the cause. A ratio that falls steadily usually means the company is repaying debt or building equity through retained profit, which strengthens the balance sheet over time.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** The debt to equity ratio measures the size of the debt against owner money. Interest coverage measures whether earnings can pay the yearly interest on that debt. Size and affordability are different questions, and a full view of debt risk needs both answers.

**What the combination tells you.** A high debt to equity ratio paired with high interest coverage means a large debt load that earnings can still service comfortably. A high ratio paired with weak coverage is the dangerous combination, because the debt is large and the earnings barely cover the cost. Reading the pair separates how much the company owes from whether the company can afford what it owes.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** The debt to equity ratio compares debt with an accounting equity figure that can be distorted by buybacks and writedowns. Net Debt / EBITDA compares debt with yearly earnings instead, and subtracts cash from the debt. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA is a rough measure of operating cash generation.

**What the combination tells you.** When equity has been hollowed out by buybacks, the debt to equity ratio can look alarming while Net Debt / EBITDA stays moderate. Comparing the two shows whether high leverage reflects a real debt burden or a shrunken equity base. Agreement between the two metrics gives a more reliable read on true leverage than either one alone.

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** ROE measures the profit earned for every dollar of equity. Borrowing raises ROE when the business earns more than the loans cost, so a high debt to equity ratio can lift ROE without any improvement in the underlying business. Reading the two together shows how much of the return comes from genuine profitability and how much comes from leverage.

**What the combination tells you.** A high ROE built on a high debt to equity ratio is fragile, because the same leverage that lifted returns will deepen losses in a downturn. A high ROE with a low debt to equity ratio points to real operating strength rather than borrowed gains. The app's DuPont ROE decomposition splits the leverage contribution out directly for this comparison.

### Liabilities / Equity

**Why read the two together.** The debt to equity ratio counts only interest-bearing borrowings. Liabilities / Equity counts every obligation, including supplier bills, pensions, and deferred taxes. A company can look lightly borrowed on debt to equity while carrying heavy non-debt obligations.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide gap between the two ratios means the company funds itself heavily through non-borrowing obligations such as supplier credit. A narrow gap means interest-bearing debt is the main obligation. Reading both shows the full claim on the company ahead of the owners, not just the loans.

## Part 8: Limitations

The bottom number is the ratio's main weakness. Equity is an accounting figure that buybacks, writedowns, and losses can distort. The ratio can therefore move sharply without any change in real borrowing. Equity also reflects the original cost of assets rather than their current worth, which makes a company with valuable old assets look more leveraged than it is. When equity falls below zero the ratio turns negative and stops carrying meaning.

The top number raises its own questions. Definitions of debt differ between companies, and lease accounting and off-balance-sheet arrangements can hide real obligations. The ratio says nothing about when the debt comes due, what interest rate it carries, or whether earnings can cover the payments. The ratio is a useful first look at the funding mix and a poor measure of whether the debt is affordable.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current ratio driven by the amount of debt the company carries, or by an equity figure that buybacks or writedowns have reshaped? What does the ratio become if equity is adjusted back?
2. Has equity been reduced by years of losses or large buybacks? Is the equity base close to zero or negative?
3. How does the ratio compare with direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle? Which peer carries the heaviest debt load?
4. Does the company's borrowing cost look consistent with the leverage level, or are lenders charging more than the ratio would suggest?
5. How much of the debt comes due within three years? Would refinancing at current rates change the picture?
6. Does the broader liabilities to equity figure tell the same story as the debt to equity ratio? Or are non-borrowing obligations large?
7. Have recent acquisitions, dividends, or buybacks been funded with new debt? Is management raising leverage at a risky point in the cycle?
8. Would the company's equity cushion survive a downturn that cut earnings in half? What would the ratio look like after such a year?
9. Are the company's assets carried near their real value, or does old original cost understate the equity base?
10. Does management have a stated target for leverage? Is the current ratio above or below that target, and why?
