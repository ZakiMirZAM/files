# Capital Structure Weights

## Part 1: Definition

Capital structure weights are the shares of a company's financing that come from equity versus debt. Equity is the owners' money, raised by selling shares. Debt is borrowed money that must be repaid with interest. The weights answer one question: of every dollar funding this company, how much comes from shareholders and how much from lenders?

The two weights always add to one whole. A company financed 70 percent by equity is financed 30 percent by debt. The weights matter because the cost of capital blends the cost of equity and the cost of debt in exactly these proportions. This app measures the weights from market values, following Aswath Damodaran, not from the values recorded in the accounts.

Common readings of the equity weight:

| Equity weight | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Above 90 percent | Financed almost entirely by shareholders. Common for capital-light or highly profitable firms. |
| 70 to 90 percent | Equity-heavy but using some debt. A conservative mix. |
| 50 to 70 percent | A balanced mix of shareholders and lenders. |
| Below 50 percent | Debt-heavy. More than half the financing is borrowed, which raises risk. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The weight of equity is found first:

`Weight of Equity = E ÷ (E + D)`

E is the market value of equity. The market value of equity is the share price times the number of shares outstanding, also called market capitalization. D is the total value of the company's debt. Adding E and D gives the company's total financing, and dividing E by that total gives equity's share.

The weight of debt is the remaining share:

`Weight of Debt = D ÷ (E + D)`

The two weights always add to one. This app measures D as total debt plus the present value of operating leases. An operating lease is a long-term rental of an asset such as a store or aircraft. The present value of those future lease payments is their worth today once discounted, and it is added to debt because a lease commits the company to fixed payments for years, the defining feature of debt. Ordinary short-term bills owed to suppliers are left out, because they carry no interest.

| Weight basis | What it uses | Why the app uses or avoids it |
|---|---|---|
| Market values | Today's share price for equity and the market value of debt | Used. Market values reflect what the financing is worth now, which is what new capital would cost. |
| Book values | Values recorded in the accounts from past transactions | Not used. Book equity often sits far below true value and distorts the mix. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Capital structure weights decide how much of the cost of capital comes from each source. The cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt are usually very different, and the weights set the blend. Without the weights, the two costs cannot be combined into one discount rate. The weights answer a question the individual costs cannot: how is this company actually financed?

Market weights are used because the cost of capital measures the cost of raising money today. New shares are issued at the market price, and new debt is raised at market value, not at the old figures in the accounts. Book values record what was raised in the past and can be badly out of date. Using market weights keeps the discount rate tied to current reality.

The mix also shapes risk. A debt-heavy company gives more weight to the cheaper after-tax cost of debt, which lowers the blended rate. The same company carries more risk, because interest and repayments are fixed promises that must be met in bad years. The weights show that trade-off in plain proportions.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The weights move mostly with the share price. Because equity is measured at market value, a rising share price lifts E and raises the equity weight, even when debt is unchanged. A falling share price does the reverse and pushes the debt weight up. The mix can shift sharply in a volatile market without any financing decision by the company.

Financing actions move the weights directly. Borrowing more, or signing large new leases, raises D and the debt weight. Repaying debt or issuing new shares raises the equity weight. Buying back shares does two things at once. A buyback lowers equity, and if it is funded by borrowing it also raises debt. A one-off collapse in the share price can make a company look far more debt-heavy than its long-run financing.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The safe amount of debt depends on how steady a business's cash flows are. Steadier cash flows safely support a larger debt weight.

| Industry type | Typical financing mix | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities, telecoms, and real estate | Debt-heavy | Steady, predictable cash flows safely support large borrowing. |
| Established industrials | Balanced | Solid earnings allow a moderate, comfortable debt share. |
| Software and other capital-light firms | Equity-heavy | Few hard assets to borrow against and strong cash generation. |
| Early-stage and biotech firms | Almost all equity | No steady profits, so lenders will not fund them cheaply. |
| Banks | Special case | Debt is the raw material of the business, so ordinary weights do not apply. |

Compare the mix only within a sector. A debt share that is prudent for a regulated utility would be alarming for a biotech with no profits. Cross-sector comparison of the weights is meaningless, because the safe amount of debt depends entirely on how steady the cash flows are.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A steady rise in the debt weight over several years means the company is leaning more on borrowing, whether by choice or because its share price has fallen. A steady rise in the equity weight means the reverse. Check the share price before reading too much into the trend, since a moving stock price shifts the weights on its own. A financing shift and a price swing look identical in the weights but mean different things.

A sudden step change usually follows a large borrowing, a big share issue, or a major buyback. High volatility in the weights often just tracks a volatile share price rather than any real change in strategy. One base effect can fake a trend. A market crash can spike the debt weight for a year, and the mix can drift back as the share price recovers.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** The capital structure weights are the proportions the weighted average cost of capital uses to blend its two costs. The cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt are the ingredients, and the weights decide how much of each goes in. A change in the weights changes the WACC even if neither cost moves. Reading the two together shows how financing mix alone shifts the discount rate.

**What the combination tells you.** A high debt weight pulls the WACC down toward the cheaper cost of debt. A high equity weight pulls it up toward the cost of equity. If the WACC drops while the business is unchanged, a rising debt weight is often the reason. The pair reveals how much of a company's discount rate is a financing choice rather than a business fact.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity and the capital structure weights describe the same financing mix in two forms. Debt to equity is D divided by E, while the debt weight is D divided by E plus D. Both rise as a company borrows more, so they move together. Reading them side by side confirms the direction and scale of the borrowing.

**What the combination tells you.** A high debt-to-equity ratio always means a high debt weight, and the weight puts the figure on a zero-to-one scale that feeds the cost of capital. If one uses book values and the other market values, the two can disagree, which flags a stale book figure. Watching both shows whether leverage is rising because of new borrowing or a falling share price. The pair turns one financing fact into a check on the other.

### Cost of Debt

**Why read the two together.** The capital structure weights decide how much the cost of debt matters to the whole cost of capital. A company can enjoy a low cost of debt, but a tiny debt weight makes that cheap rate almost irrelevant. The weight and the rate must be read together to see the real benefit of borrowing. Reading them together shows how much cheap debt actually helps.

**What the combination tells you.** A low cost of debt with a high debt weight gives the largest reduction in the cost of capital. A low cost of debt with a tiny debt weight barely moves the blended rate. A rising debt weight raises the payoff from cheap borrowing but also the risk from fixed payments. The pair shows whether a company is genuinely capturing the benefit of low-cost debt.

## Part 8: Limitations

Market weights swing with the share price, so the mix can look very different from one month to the next without any decision by the company. A crashing stock can make a sound company appear dangerously debt-heavy for a while. The weights also depend on measuring the market value of debt, which is hard when a company's loans do not trade. Book debt is often used as a stand-in, which reintroduces some of the book-value problem the weights try to avoid.

The weights capture only the split between equity and debt. The weights say nothing about loan terms, maturities, or whether the debt is fixed or floating. Adding the present value of operating leases improves the debt figure but relies on estimates of future payments. For a company with almost no debt, the weights are nearly all equity and add little information beyond that fact.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current equity weight high because of little debt, or because the share price has risen?
2. How much of the debt figure comes from actual borrowing versus the present value of operating leases?
3. Would the weights look very different if measured on book values instead of market values?
4. How much has the mix shifted in the past year, and was that a financing decision or a share-price move?
5. Is the debt weight prudent for this company's sector and the steadiness of its cash flows?
6. How much does the current debt weight lower the company's cost of capital compared with an all-equity mix?
7. Have recent buybacks or new borrowing pushed the debt weight up, and can the company carry the fixed payments?
8. Does the market value of debt rely on a stale book figure because the company's loans do not trade?
9. How does the financing mix compare with direct competitors in the same sector?
10. If the share price fell sharply, how far would the debt weight rise, and how would that change the discount rate?
