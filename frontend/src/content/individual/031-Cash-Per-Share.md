# Cash Per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Cash per share measures how many dollars of cash sit behind each share of a company. Cash here means cash and cash equivalents, which is money in bank accounts plus investments that turn into cash within about ninety days. Shares outstanding is the total number of ownership units the company has issued and that investors currently hold. The metric answers one question: how much of each share is backed by money the company already holds rather than by the business itself?

Cash per share has two uses. As a cushion, the metric shows how much liquid money each share owns, which is the resource that buys time in a downturn. As a valuation adjustment, the metric shows how much of the stock price is money rather than a claim on the operations. A stock at sixty dollars with twelve dollars of cash per share means the operations are really being bought for forty-eight dollars per share.

Common readings of the metric:

| Cash per share level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negligible | Almost no cash backs each share. The company runs lean or has spent its cash, and the share price is almost entirely a claim on the business. |
| Moderate relative to price | A normal working buffer. Cash is a small part of the share price and the rest is the operating business. |
| Large relative to price | A meaningful part of the share price is money the company already holds. The operations are cheaper than the headline price suggests, but idle cash may be dragging on returns. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Cash Per Share = Cash and Cash Equivalents ÷ Shares Outstanding`

Cash and cash equivalents is the top number. The figure is the money in bank accounts plus investments that convert to cash within about ninety days, such as money market funds and short-term government bills. Some versions of the metric also add short-term marketable securities, which are stocks and bonds the company holds and expects to sell soon. These items are combined because they are close to spendable money. The exact contents are worth checking against the balance sheet lines in the app's vertical analysis, where cash and short-term investments each appear across every year.

Shares outstanding is the bottom number. Shares outstanding is the total number of ownership units the company has issued and that investors hold right now. Dividing the cash pile by this count turns one company-wide number into a per-share figure that an investor can compare against the share price. Some analysts use the diluted share count instead of the basic count. The diluted count adds shares that could be created from stock options and convertible securities, so the diluted version gives a slightly lower and more cautious reading.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Cash per share adjusts the share price into a truer measure of what the business costs. Two companies can trade at the same earnings multiple while one holds large cash and the other holds little. The cash-rich company's operations are cheaper than the multiple shows, because part of the price is just money. Cash per share states that money on a per-share basis, so an investor can subtract it from the price before judging the operations.

The cushion reading matters on its own. Cash per share compared against the company's yearly spending shows how long the company could survive without raising new money. A company with a deep cash buffer can keep operating through a recession while weaker rivals are forced to sell assets or cut back.

Cash is also the asset that is easiest to value, because money is already worth its stated amount. This is why cash anchors the deep-value tradition. The net current asset value calculation in the app's Graham section leans on cash as the hardest part of a company's break-up worth.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The cash pile is the leftover of every cash flow the company produces and spends. Operating cash flow builds the pile. Capital spending, dividends, buybacks, acquisitions, and debt repayment drain the pile. Raising money through new borrowing or new share sales refills the pile. The level reflects management's choices about money as much as the strength of the business.

Watch the source behind any rise in cash. Cash earned by the business is a different signal from cash raised by borrowing or by selling new shares. Seasonal businesses such as retailers swing their cash level through the year. Some cash is hard to use, because money held in foreign subsidiaries, in regulated units, or as day-to-day operating float may not be available to pay out. Buybacks pull the figure two ways at once, because a buyback shrinks the share count while also draining the cash, which can leave cash per share looking steady through a large repurchase program.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level of cash per share depends on the business model. The same dollar amount means different things in different sectors.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Banks and insurers | Distorted, exclude from comparison | Cash is the operating inventory of the business, held to meet regulatory rules, not spare money the company can return. |
| Large technology and pharma | Very high cash per share | These firms generate cash faster than they can reinvest it, so cash builds into large hoards that should be valued separately from the operations. |
| Biotech and early-stage | The figure is survival runway | These companies spend cash before earning profit, so cash per share against the yearly burn shows how long the company can last. |
| Capital-intensive makers | Thin cash | Heavy spending on equipment and plants keeps the cash buffer small, sized to the swings in the business. |
| Highly indebted companies | Read net cash, not gross cash | Net cash is cash minus total debt. A large cash pile next to larger debt is borrowed liquidity, not real wealth. |

Cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the role of cash differs between the businesses. Comparing a bank, where cash is held to meet rules, against a software firm, where cash is spare money, compares two different things. Judge the cash pile against the company's own volatility and its own chances to reinvest.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The per-share trends chart shows whether each share's cash backing is building or draining, and the cause decides the meaning. A steady rise fed by operating cash flow is strength building up, and the open question becomes when the company will deploy the cash. The balance sheet vertical analysis tells the same story as cash's share of total assets grows.

A sudden jump usually marks a one-time event rather than earning power. A debt raise or an asset sale can lift cash per share in a single year without the business improving. A steady decline has a wide range of causes. Healthy reasons include cash spent on buybacks at good prices, debt paid off, or high-return investment. Concerning reasons include operations no longer covering the dividend or acquisitions eating the buffer. The cash flow statement's three sections assign the cause year by year. The pattern to watch at a mature company is the pile that grows for a decade while returns on capital stay high, because idle cash earning little is dragging down the company's overall return. A base effect can fake a trend when a buyback shrinks the share count, which can prop up cash per share even as the total cash falls.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Debt Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Cash per share counts the money the company holds. Interest Debt Per Share counts the borrowed money the company owes, expressed on the same per-share basis. Subtracting the second from the first gives net cash per share, which is the figure that truly adjusts the share price. A large cash pile means little if an even larger debt sits beside it.

**What the combination tells you.** Positive net cash per share means the company could repay all its borrowing and still have money left, so the operations are cheaper than the headline price. Negative net cash means the gross cash figure overstates the company's strength, because debt outweighs the cash. Reading the pair stops an investor from treating borrowed liquidity as real wealth.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Cash per share is the level of cash at one moment. Free Cash Flow is the cash the business generates after paying for the equipment and buildings it needs to keep running. The level tells you how much cash is there. The flow tells you whether the business is filling the pile or draining it.

**What the combination tells you.** A growing cash pile alongside weak free cash flow means the cash was built by borrowing or by selling shares, not earned. A shrinking pile alongside strong free cash flow means the company is deploying earned cash into buybacks, dividends, or investment. The pair separates cash the company earned from cash the company raised.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Cash per share looks at cash alone. Net Debt / EBITDA measures the total debt load, after subtracting cash, against yearly earnings. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, a rough measure of operating cash earnings. Cash is one input into the net debt figure, so the two metrics view the same cash from different angles.

**What the combination tells you.** High cash per share with low Net Debt / EBITDA confirms a conservative balance sheet where cash dominates debt. High cash per share with high Net Debt / EBITDA warns that the cash sits beside heavy borrowing, so the company is less safe than the cash pile alone suggests. Together the two show whether the cash is a real buffer or an offset to large debt.

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Cash per share isolates the most liquid asset the company holds. The Current Ratio compares all assets due to turn into cash within a year against all bills due within a year. Cash is the strongest part of those current assets, so the two metrics measure liquidity at two different levels of strictness.

**What the combination tells you.** A strong Current Ratio that rests mostly on cash per share is sturdier than one that rests on inventory or money owed by customers, because cash needs no conversion. A weak cash per share figure inside a healthy Current Ratio means the company depends on selling inventory or collecting from customers to pay its bills. Reading the pair shows how much of the company's short-term safety is hard cash.

## Part 8: Limitations

Cash per share counts gross cash and ignores debt, so the figure overstates safety on its own. Only net cash, which subtracts debt, adjusts the share price honestly. The availability of the cash also varies. Money held in foreign units, in regulated subsidiaries, pledged against loans, or required as operating float is less usable than the headline figure suggests. The metric cannot tell earned cash from raised cash without the cash flow statement beside it.

Cash per share is a snapshot taken on the reporting date, and a company can arrange its cash to look strong on that one day. The metric says nothing about whether the cash is productive, because money earning low interest is a poor use of shareholder capital at a high-return business. Treating a large cash pile as value assumes management will use the cash well, and the record on cash-funded acquisitions is mixed at exactly the companies where the pile is largest. For the runway reading, the yearly spending rate matters more than the pile, and cash per share does not carry that spending figure.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is the cash per share figure once total debt is subtracted, and how much of the current share price does that net cash figure represent?
2. How was the cash pile built across the chart's history: operating cash flow, an asset sale, or a debt or share raise? What does each source say about how durable the cash is?
3. How much of the cash is genuinely available, and how much is held abroad with repatriation costs, trapped in regulated units, or required as operating float?
4. What is management's stated purpose for the current cash balance, and does the deployment record over the past decade justify trusting management with the cash?
5. At the company's worst-year cash consumption, how many years of runway does the current pile provide without raising outside money?
6. What return is the cash earning compared with the company's return on invested capital, and how much does the idle cash cost shareholders each year in blended return?
7. Has the pile grown while the company's reinvestment opportunities shrank, and is a special dividend or larger buyback the honest move that management is avoiding?
8. Does the valuation being paid subtract the net cash properly, and would the operations alone, at price minus net cash per share, still clear the multiples that peers command?
9. What acquisitions did this management make the last time the cash pile peaked, at what prices, and with what later returns on the money spent?
10. If credit markets closed for two years, which competitors would be forced to sell assets, and is this company's cash position sized to be the buyer at that moment?
