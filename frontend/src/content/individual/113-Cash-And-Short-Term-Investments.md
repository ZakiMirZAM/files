# Cash And Short Term Investments

## Part 1: Definition

Cash and short term investments is the company's full pool of money available within one year. The line sums two rows from the balance sheet. The first is cash and cash equivalents, which holds currency, bank deposits, and instruments maturing within three months of purchase. The second is short term investments, which holds securities maturing later than three months but inside one year. Adding the two gives everything the company could turn into cash in a year without selling any operating asset.

The balance sheet is the statement that lists what a company owns, what it owes, and what is left for owners. Cash and short term investments sits at the top of the current assets, the assets expected to convert to cash within one year. The line is the natural unit for liquidity, the ability to raise cash quickly to meet obligations. The split between cash and securities below it is a treasury choice, and treasury teams move money between the two for yield. This combined line cancels that reshuffle and shows the true reserve. In the vertical analysis table the app shows the line as a percent of total assets, the sum of everything the company owns.

Common readings of the line as a percent of total assets:

| Combined liquid share | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 3 percent | A thin reserve. The company depends on steady incoming cash to pay its bills. |
| 3 to 15 percent | A normal working pool for most operating companies. |
| 15 to 35 percent | A comfortable buffer. Common at profitable, cash-generating firms. |
| Above 35 percent | A large hoard. The useful question becomes why so much capital sits liquid rather than working. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

Cash and short term investments is the sum of its two component lines:

`Cash And Short Term Investments = Cash And Cash Equivalents + Short Term Investments`

Cash and cash equivalents is the first input. The figure covers currency, bank deposits the company can draw on at once, and equivalents that mature within three months of purchase. These funds carry almost no price risk and need no sale to be spent.

Short term investments is the second input. The figure covers marketable securities maturing later than three months but within one year, such as treasury bills, agency notes, and certificates of deposit. A marketable security is an investment that can be sold quickly in a public market at a known price. The two inputs differ only in maturity, so summing them gives the total money the company holds in liquid form for the year ahead.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The combined line is the cleanest measure of liquid safety. A company can show a profit and still fail if it cannot raise cash when a bill comes due. The combined pool tells the investor how much immediate and near-immediate money stands behind the company's obligations, without being fooled by where treasury chooses to park it.

The line also measures freedom to act. Dividends, buybacks, debt repayment, and purchases of other companies all draw on this pool. A firm with a deep combined reserve can move in a downturn while weaker rivals are forced to retrench. An investor reads the line to judge both the cushion against trouble and the capacity to seize opportunity.

The line carries the same warning as its parts at the high end. Cash and short safe securities earn little. A very large pool that never gets spent or returned can mean management has run out of good uses for the capital. The percent of total assets puts the size of that pool in plain view.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Operating cash generation drives the line. A business that collects from customers faster than it pays suppliers and that earns real profit feeds the pool every period. Weak collection, thin margins, or rising costs drain it.

Financing and investing decisions move it sharply. Raising debt or issuing shares lifts the pool at once. Paying dividends, buying back shares, repaying loans, or buying equipment and companies lowers it. A single large acquisition can cut the reserve in one period.

Two presentation effects can distort a single reading. Drawing a loan just before the reporting date inflates the pool, and repaying it after deflates it. Cash and securities held abroad may carry a tax cost to bring home, so the reported total can overstate the money truly available. The combined line removes the cash-versus-securities reshuffle, but it does not remove these effects. Read several periods.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a healthy combined share depends on how steady and capital-hungry the business is.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Software and asset-light technology | Large combined share | High margins and low equipment needs let the pool pile up. |
| Capital-intensive manufacturers | Small combined share | Surplus is reinvested in plant rather than held liquid. |
| Utilities and regulated networks | Small combined share | Near-certain revenue makes a thin reserve safe. |
| Banks and insurers | Not comparable | Liquid reserves are core to the business and follow regulation. |
| Cyclical or early-stage firms | Often deliberately high | A deep buffer protects against losses in a weak year. |

A cross-sector comparison of the combined share is meaningless when companies hold liquid reserves for different reasons. Compare a company with direct peers, and read the pool together with the company's debt to judge net liquidity.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows a multi-year chart of this combined line. The chart is more reliable than the cash chart alone, because the combined line ignores the treasury reshuffle between cash and securities. Read both the dollar amount and the percent of total assets. A steady rise in the combined share usually means the business generates more cash than it spends. A rise is a strength when profit funds it and a weaker signal when it reflects hoarding with no plan.

A steady decline deserves attention. A falling combined share can come from heavy reinvestment, from buybacks and dividends, or from a business that no longer produces enough cash. The cause decides whether the decline is healthy. A step change in one year usually marks a single event such as an acquisition, a stock sale, or a large debt repayment. High volatility points to a seasonal or cyclical business. The base effect to watch is total assets, since the percent can fall even when the pool holds steady if total assets grow faster. Always read the dollar amount beside the percent.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Cash Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Cash per share divides the liquid pool by the number of shares outstanding. The combined line is the pool in that calculation. Reading the two together translates the company-wide reserve into a figure tied to a single share, which is what an owner of one share actually holds behind their stock.

**What the combination tells you.** A combined pool that grows while cash per share stays flat means the share count is rising and diluting the per-share reserve. A pool that grows alongside cash per share means owners are gaining liquid backing per share. Reading the two together shows whether a growing reserve is reaching shareholders or being spread thinner.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Net debt subtracts this combined liquid pool from total debt before measuring the load. The combined line is the exact amount subtracted. The pool therefore drives how heavy or light the net debt figure looks.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with high gross debt and a deep combined pool may carry little net debt. A shrinking pool raises net debt even when borrowing does not change. Reading the combined line beside net debt shows whether the reserve is quietly being spent down in a way that raises the true debt burden.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the cash a business produces after paying for the equipment it needs to keep running. Free cash flow is the engine that fills the combined pool over time. Reading the flow beside the stock shows whether the reserve is being fed by the business or drained.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising combined pool backed by positive free cash flow is healthy and self-funded. A rising pool with negative free cash flow means the reserve came from borrowing or share sales, not the business. Reading the two together reveals the source of the liquid pool, which matters more than the level alone.

## Part 8: Limitations

The combined line is a snapshot on one date. A company can move money in or out around the reporting date to flatter the figure, so one reading can mislead. Cash and securities held abroad may carry a tax cost to bring home, so the reported total can overstate the money truly available at home. The line also hides the split between instant cash and securities that must be sold first, which the quick ratio and the underlying rows reveal.

A large combined pool is not always a virtue. Liquid assets earn a low return, so a pool that sits idle drags on the return the company earns for owners. A small pool is not always a weakness, because a business that collects reliably and can borrow easily may choose to hold little. The line says how much liquid money exists. The line does not say whether holding that amount is wise, nor how safe the securities inside it are, and those judgments require reading the notes and the rest of the statements.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Did the combined pool grow because the business generated cash, or did it come from new borrowing or a share sale this period? Where in the cash flow statement does the increase come from?
2. How has the combined share of total assets moved over the years on the chart? Is the trend driven by the pool amount or by changes in total assets?
3. How much of the pool is held abroad, and what tax cost would apply to bring it home? How much of the reported total is truly available to spend?
4. Does the company carry a large liquid pool and large debt at the same time? If so, why is the pool not used to pay down the debt?
5. If the combined share is very high, what does management plan to do with the money? Is there a stated use, or is the pool sitting idle?
6. If the combined share is very thin, how dependent is the company on collecting from customers on schedule to pay its own bills?
7. Has the pool been rising while free cash flow is negative? If so, the reserve is being funded by financing rather than the business.
8. Inside the pool, how much is instant cash versus securities that must be sold first? Does the split change the company's true flexibility in a crisis?
9. How does the combined share compare with direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle? Is the company more or less conservative than its peers?
10. Did a single event such as an acquisition, a dividend, or a debt repayment cause a step change in the line? Is that event likely to repeat?
