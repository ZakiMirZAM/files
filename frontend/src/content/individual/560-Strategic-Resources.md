# Strategic Resources

## What it is

Strategic resources are the assets that actually create value at a modern company. Research programs and brands are common examples. Patents, software platforms, and customer data also qualify. Skilled talent is a strategic resource as well. Baruch Lev and Feng Gu describe these resources in their book *The End of Accounting*, and they explain why standard financial statements hide them. Accounting rules require a company to expense most of the money spent to build these resources. Expensing means the cost is subtracted from profit in the year it is paid rather than recorded as an asset the company keeps. A patent that took years of research to develop can therefore be highly valuable while appearing nowhere on the balance sheet. The balance sheet is the financial statement that lists what a company owns and what it owes. An investor who reads only the reported earnings will miss the very things that make the business valuable. Reported earnings are the profit figure the company publishes each year. The Strategic Resources section studies these assets directly instead.

## How this section works

The Strategic Resources section reorganizes the analysis around the company's strategic resource rather than around reported earnings. Lev and Gu pose five questions, and each one follows the resource from creation to payoff. You answer each question first in your own words. The Explain using AI button beside every question is a tutor that helps only after you have tried. You do the thinking, and the AI supports that thinking rather than replacing it. The five questions run in order:

1. **Inventory: what is the key strategic resource?** A resource inventory names the brand, patent, or customer base behind the company's edge.
2. **Development: how is the resource built?** Development covers the spending that creates the resource, such as research or brand advertising.
3. **Preservation: how is the resource defended?** Preservation asks how the company protects the resource from imitation, disruption, and decay.
4. **Deployment: how is the resource used?** Deployment describes the way the company puts the resource to work, for example by licensing a patent or building it into a product.
5. **Value creation: does the resource actually create value?** Value creation measures the cash the resource returns after charging for the capital used to fund it.

## Why it matters for valuation

Valuation estimates what a business is worth by projecting the cash it will produce and discounting that cash to today. Discounting means reducing a future amount to its value now, because money arriving later is worth less than money in hand. A valuation built only on reported earnings understates a company whose value sits in expensed resources. Research and brand spending lower this year's profit even when they build an asset that pays off for years. Studying the strategic resource shows where the future cash will come from and how durable it is. A durable resource that rivals cannot copy supports earnings for a long time, which raises the value of the business. A resource that is decaying or easily imitated warns that today's earnings may not last.

## What a strong answer looks like versus a weak one

| Question | Strong answer | Weak answer |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | Names the one or two resources that truly drive the edge and explains why they matter | Lists every asset without ranking, or points only to buildings and equipment |
| Development | Traces the specific spending that builds the resource and judges whether it is efficient | Repeats total research spending with no view on what it produces |
| Preservation | Identifies the real threats of imitation, disruption, and decay and the defenses in place | Assumes the advantage is permanent and ignores rivals |
| Deployment | Shows how the resource is put to work and why that path fits the strategy | Describes revenue without connecting it to the resource |
| Value creation | Weighs the cash returned against the full cost of the capital used | Points to rising earnings alone as proof of value |

## Common traps

1. **Treating the balance sheet as complete.** The most valuable resources are expensed and absent from it, so a company can look asset-light while owning a powerful franchise.
2. **Confusing spending with strength.** Large research or advertising budgets show effort rather than results. A high budget that produces weak products destroys value instead of creating it.
3. **Assuming a resource lasts forever.** Brands decay without maintenance, patents expire, and new technology can disrupt an entire business model. Preservation has to be checked every year.
4. **Judging value by earnings alone.** Reported earnings ignore the cost of equity capital, which is the return shareholders require for putting money at risk. A resource creates value only when its cash return clears that cost.
5. **Copying a rival's resource story.** Each industry rests on a different resource, so a template built for one company misreads another.
