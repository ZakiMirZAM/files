# Debt Capacity

## Part 1: Definition

Debt capacity is how much debt a business can safely carry. Arnold Ziegel frames debt capacity as a function of the level and stability of a company's future free cash flow, not of its accounting profit. Free cash flow is the cash left after operating costs and the spending on equipment and buildings needed to keep the business running. The whole question hinges on cash flow, whether that cash flow is steady or volatile. A business with steady cash flow can safely carry far more debt than a business with volatile cash flow.

The app turns the idea into one number. The app divides total debt by a three-year average of free cash flow, which gives a rough count of how many years of free cash flow it would take to repay the debt. Total debt is the company's interest-bearing borrowing across bonds, bank loans, and leases. A lower count is safer. A very high count means many years of the company's cash are already committed to repaying the debt.

Common readings of the metric:

| Debt Capacity Read level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| A low number of years | Debt is small next to yearly free cash flow. The company could repay its debt quickly. |
| A moderate number of years | A manageable load for a business with steady cash flow. |
| A high number of years | Many years of free cash flow are already promised to the debt. Little room remains for a weak year. |
| A very high number of years | Repaying the debt from cash flow would take most of a business cycle. Refinancing, not repayment, keeps the debt alive. |
| Negative | Average free cash flow is negative, so the count cannot be formed. The company is not producing cash to repay debt. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Debt Capacity Read = Total Debt ÷ 3-Year Average Free Cash Flow`

Total debt is the top number. Total debt is the company's interest-bearing borrowing across bonds, bank loans, and leases. The measure asks how large the debt is next to the cash the business produces, so the full borrowing belongs on top.

The bottom number is the average free cash flow over the last three years. Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Operating cash flow is the cash the business generates from normal operations. Capital expenditures are the money spent on equipment and buildings. Cash is what actually repays debt, so free cash flow is the right base rather than accounting profit. The app averages three years to smooth a single unusual year, such as a year with a one-off spike in equipment spending.

Two versions of the measure exist:

| Version | What the version changes |
|---|---|
| Total-debt basis (used here) | Counts all borrowing and ignores any cash the company holds. Gives the more conservative reading. |
| Net-debt basis | Subtracts cash and short-term investments from debt first. Gives a smaller count for a company sitting on a large cash pile. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Debt is not safe or dangerous on its own. The same debt that is safe for a company with steady cash flow can bankrupt a company with volatile cash flow. Ziegel makes the point that debt capacity hinges on the future of cash flow, volatile or stable. The read states the debt as a count of years of the company's own cash, which ties the size of the debt to the company's ability to produce cash.

The measure answers a question that accounting profit cannot. Net income includes non-cash items and can differ from the cash a business actually collects, and cash is what repays lenders. Net income is a company's profit after all costs and taxes. By building the read on free cash flow, the app tests the debt against the cash that could truly be sent to lenders.

Lenders size loans to this kind of read. A lender wants to see that a borrower produces enough cash to repay within a reasonable time. A read that stretches to many years warns a lender that repayment depends on refinancing rather than on the company's own cash. Refinancing means replacing a maturing loan with a new loan.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Two forces move the read: the amount of debt on top and the free cash flow on the bottom. Borrowing for an acquisition, a share buyback, or a dividend raises total debt and pushes the read higher. Repaying debt lowers it. Because free cash flow sits on the bottom, anything that lifts free cash flow pulls the read down toward safety.

Free cash flow is the more volatile input. A year of heavy capital expenditures shrinks free cash flow and inflates the read, even when total debt has not moved. A pause in that spending flatters free cash flow and shrinks the read. Working capital swings move operating cash flow the same way, where working capital is the cash tied up in unsold inventory and unpaid customer bills. A one-off asset sale can lift free cash flow for a single year, so the three-year average softens but does not erase such distortions.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a safe read depends on how steady the company's cash flow is. Steadier cash flow makes a higher read safe, which is exactly Ziegel's point about the natural capital structure of an industry.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities and regulated networks | A higher read is normal and safe | Revenue is near certain and cash flow is steady, so a larger debt load repays reliably. |
| Capital-intensive producers such as telecom and pipelines | Read runs high | Heavy assets require heavy borrowing, but stable cash flow supports it. |
| Cyclical companies such as miners and automakers | Judge at the worst year | Free cash flow can collapse in a downturn, so a three-year average can hide the trough. |
| Capital-light software | Read is tiny or undefined | Little debt and strong cash flow leave almost nothing to repay. |
| Banks and insurers | Not meaningful | Debt is the raw material of the business, so a repayment count does not describe them. The app handles them on a separate credit track. |

Compare a company only against others in its own sector, and weight the worst cash-flow year on the chart above the latest year. A cross-sector comparison of the read is meaningless when the two sectors carry debt for different reasons.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A read that rises year after year means debt is growing faster than free cash flow, which slowly commits more of the company's cash to lenders. A read that falls year after year means the company is repaying debt, or its free cash flow is growing. A single step up usually marks a debt-funded acquisition. A read that jumps around reflects free cash flow that swings, often from uneven capital spending.

One base effect can fake a trend in this read. A single year of heavy equipment spending shrinks free cash flow and spikes the read, even though the company borrowed nothing new. The three-year average dampens that spike but can still carry it. Read this metric next to the free cash flow line, so a swing driven by capital spending is not mistaken for rising debt.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### FCF / Total Debt

**Why read the two together.** FCF / Total Debt is the same relationship as the debt capacity read, turned upside down. The debt capacity read counts years of cash needed to repay the debt, while FCF / Total Debt states the share of the debt that one year of free cash flow could repay. Both use the same two numbers, so they always agree on direction. Reading the share alongside the year count makes a heavy debt load easier to grasp.

**What the combination tells you.** A high FCF / Total Debt and a low debt capacity read both say the same thing: the company throws off enough cash to clear its debt quickly. A low share and a high year count both warn that repayment would take a long time. Seeing the relationship stated both ways guards against misreading either single number. The pair is strongest when free cash flow is stable enough that one year represents the company well.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** The debt capacity read measures debt against free cash flow, the cash left after equipment spending. Net Debt / EBITDA measures debt against EBITDA, a broader earnings figure. EBITDA is operating income before depreciation and amortization are subtracted, where those are non-cash charges spreading an asset's cost over years. Net debt also subtracts the company's cash from its debt first.

**What the combination tells you.** Net Debt / EBITDA usually reads lower than the debt capacity read, because EBITDA is larger than free cash flow and ignores the equipment spending the business needs. A wide gap between the two means capital spending eats most of the earnings before any debt is repaid, so the free-cash view is the honest one. A narrow gap means the business is capital light and both measures can be trusted. Reading both stops a low Net Debt / EBITDA from hiding a heavy real repayment burden.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the entire bottom of the debt capacity read. The read cannot be understood without knowing whether the underlying free cash flow is steady or erratic. A read built on a stable free cash flow means far more than the same read built on a number that swings every year. Checking the free cash flow line first shows how much trust the read deserves.

**What the combination tells you.** A low debt capacity read backed by steady free cash flow is genuinely safe. The same low read backed by wildly swinging free cash flow can turn dangerous in a single weak year. A negative free cash flow makes the debt capacity read meaningless, because a company with no free cash flow cannot repay debt from cash at all. Reading the base explains whether the read is a solid measure or an artifact of one strong year.

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** The debt capacity read asks whether the company can repay the debt, while interest coverage asks whether it can afford the interest on the debt. Interest coverage is operating income divided by interest expense. Repayment and affordability are two different tests, and a company can pass one while failing the other. Reading both covers the full cost of the debt.

**What the combination tells you.** A company can afford its interest easily yet still need many years of cash to repay the principal. Principal is the borrowed amount itself rather than the interest fee. A stretched repayment points to refinancing risk rather than an interest problem, so strong interest coverage with a high debt capacity read is the classic sign of a borrower that leans on rolling its debt over. When both measures look healthy, the debt is safe on the yearly cost and on the eventual repayment. When both look weak, the company has a genuine solvency problem.

## Part 8: Limitations

Free cash flow is volatile, so the read can jump for reasons that have nothing to do with the debt. A single heavy investment year can spike the read even when the debt is unchanged, and a negative free cash flow makes the read impossible to compute. The three-year average smooths one odd year but can still be dragged by a deep trough inside the window. The read also treats all debt as if it came due at once, when in reality repayment is spread across many years.

Several further gaps matter. The read ignores the cash and investments a company already holds against its debt, which the net-debt version would capture. The read ignores the maturity schedule, so it cannot tell whether repayment is due next year or in a decade. A company almost never sends all of its free cash flow to lenders, because dividends and new investment compete for the same cash. The debt capacity read is a rough gauge of how heavy the debt is, not a repayment schedule.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What is driving the current read, the size of the debt or the size of free cash flow? What does the read become if free cash flow returns to its normal level?
2. What was the read in the company's worst free-cash-flow year on the chart? Could the debt still be called safe at that year's cash level?
3. Is any single year inside the three-year average distorted by a one-off, such as a large asset sale or an unusual spike in equipment spending?
4. How much of the total debt actually comes due within the next few years? Does the repayment schedule line up with the cash the company produces?
5. How much cash and short-term investments does the company hold against its debt? How much lower is the read on a net-debt basis?
6. Is free cash flow steady enough that a multi-year average describes the company well, or does it swing too much for the average to mean anything?
7. Has recent borrowing funded acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends rather than assets that will produce new cash flow?
8. How does the read compare with direct competitors in the same industry at the same point in the business cycle?
9. Does the free cash flow used here reflect genuine operating cash, or is it propped up by cutting necessary investment at the expense of future cash flow?
10. If cash flow fell by half in a downturn, how many years would repayment then take, and would that still be survivable given when the debt comes due?
