# Common Stock Repurchased

## Part 1: Definition

Common stock repurchased is the cash a company spends buying its own shares back from the market. A repurchase, also called a buyback, is the company using its cash to purchase shares that the public owns. Common stock is an ownership unit in the company. Once bought back, the shares are either cancelled or held by the company, and in both cases they stop counting toward the shares the public holds. The line answers one question: how much cash did the company spend returning capital to owners by shrinking the share count?

The line sits in the financing section of the cash flow statement. The financing section records cash moving between the company and the people who fund it, meaning lenders and owners. Common stock repurchased is a cash outflow, so the line subtracts from cash. A buyback is one of the two main ways a company returns cash to owners, the other being dividends. A buyback returns cash by raising each remaining owner's share of the business rather than by paying cash directly to every holder.

Common readings of a buyback program:

| Buyback level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Near zero | The company keeps its cash for growth, debt repayment, or dividends instead. |
| Small and steady | The buyback mostly offsets the new shares created by employee pay, keeping the share count flat. |
| Large in a strong year | The company is returning serious excess cash to owners by shrinking the share count. |
| Large in a weak year at a low share price | The most valuable kind of buyback, because the company is buying cheap, if the cash can be spared. |
| Large in a peak year at a high share price | A warning, because the company may be overpaying for its own shares near a market top. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

This line is not a ratio. The line is a raw cash figure taken straight from the financing section:

`Common Stock Repurchased = Cash spent buying back shares during the year`

The number reported is the total cash paid to repurchase common stock over the year. The figure shows as a negative entry in the financing section because the cash leaves the company. The amount is the price paid for the shares, not the count of shares bought, so the same dollar figure can mean many cheap shares or few expensive ones.

The app shows this line as a share of revenue in its vertical common-size table. A common-size table restates each line as a percentage of revenue so its size can be judged against the size of the business. The formula the app applies is plain:

`Common Stock Repurchased as % of Revenue = Common Stock Repurchased ÷ Revenue`

Revenue is the total sales for the year. Dividing by revenue scales the buyback to the size of the business. A useful refinement is to net the buyback against shares issued, because a buyback that only cancels the new shares from employee pay returns nothing to owners. Net buyback means repurchases minus the new shares created that year.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A buyback returns cash to owners by shrinking the share count. With fewer shares outstanding, each remaining share owns a larger slice of the company and a larger slice of every future profit. A reader watches this line to see how much excess cash management is choosing to return this way rather than reinvesting it or paying it as dividends.

The line reveals management's view of its own share price. Buying back stock makes sense only when the shares trade below their true worth, because the company is then buying value at a discount. Buying back stock near a high price destroys value, because the company is overpaying with cash that owners could have kept. The amount spent shows the commitment, and the price paid shows the judgment behind it.

A buyback also raises earnings per share, which is profit divided by share count. Fewer shares lift the per-share figure even when total profit is flat. A buyback is not a free way to grow per-share figures, because the cash spent gives up the interest it could have earned or forces new borrowing. The benefit to each share is real only after that cost is counted.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The biggest driver is how much excess cash the business throws off. A mature company with strong cash flow and few growth projects tends to run a large buyback. A company spending heavily on growth or paying down debt tends to buy back little. The buyback competes for cash with every other use, so its size reflects where management ranks it.

The share price moves the value of the buyback even when the dollar amount holds steady. The same spend buys more shares when the price is low and fewer shares when the price is high. A disciplined company spends more on buybacks when its stock is cheap and pulls back when the stock is dear. Many companies do the opposite, spending most near market peaks when cash is plentiful and the stock is expensive.

Employee stock pay quietly drives part of the number. Companies that pay staff in shares buy back stock just to cancel the new shares those plans create. A buyback that only offsets this dilution keeps the share count flat and returns nothing to owners. Dilution means each old share representing a smaller fraction as new shares appear. The reader must separate buybacks that shrink the share count from buybacks that merely hold it steady.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The normal level of buyback depends on how much excess cash a sector produces and how much it pays in stock.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Mature consumer and industrial firms | Large, steady buybacks | Strong cash flow and limited growth leave excess cash to return to owners. |
| Software and technology | Buybacks that offset stock pay | Heavy stock-based compensation creates new shares, so buybacks often just cancel them. |
| Banks | Buybacks gated by regulators | Capital rules and stress tests can pause or limit a buyback regardless of cash on hand. |
| Cyclical producers such as miners and energy | Lumpy buybacks at cycle peaks | Cash floods in at the top of the commodity cycle, the worst time to buy shares. |
| Early-stage and high-growth firms | Near zero | These companies need every dollar for growth and issue shares rather than buy them. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when sectors differ in cash generation and stock-pay habits. A technology firm's buyback often just offsets dilution, while a consumer firm's buyback genuinely shrinks the share count. Compare the buyback against direct peers, and always check it net of shares issued.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The app shows this line as a multi-year series in raw cash and as a percent of revenue. A steady line that holds at a modest level usually means a routine program, often one sized to offset employee stock pay. A flat near-zero line means the company is returning cash through dividends or reinvesting it instead. The percent-of-revenue view keeps the reading honest as revenue grows, because a fixed dollar of buyback shrinks as a share of a rising base.

The pattern that deserves the most attention is the timing against the share price. Buybacks that swell in strong years and vanish in weak years are badly timed. The company is buying dear in the high-price years and skipping the bargains in the low-price years. Buybacks that hold steady or rise when the stock is cheap are well timed and build the most value. A single large spike can mark a special return of excess cash, and the spike is good or bad depending on whether the shares were cheap that year. Always pair the buyback line with the change in share count, because a large buyback that fails to shrink the count is being eaten by new share issuance.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### SBC / Revenue

**Why read the two together.** SBC stands for stock-based compensation, the value of shares and options paid to employees. SBC creates new shares that dilute owners, while a buyback cancels shares. Reading the two together shows whether the buyback is genuinely returning capital or merely cleaning up the dilution that stock pay creates. A buyback that just matches the SBC each year leaves the share count flat and the owners no better off.

**What the combination tells you.** A large buyback paired with a high SBC / Revenue often means most of the buyback is offsetting dilution, not returning cash. The true return to owners is the buyback net of the new shares from stock pay. When the buyback runs well above SBC, the share count actually falls and owners gain. When the buyback only matches SBC, the program is treading water despite the cash spent.

### EPS

**Why read the two together.** EPS stands for earnings per share, profit divided by share count. A buyback shrinks the share count, the bottom of the EPS calculation, which lifts EPS even when total profit is flat. Reading the two together shows how much of any EPS growth came from the business earning more versus the company shrinking the share base.

**What the combination tells you.** EPS rising while total profit is flat means the gain is coming from the buyback, not from a stronger business. EPS rising alongside growing total profit means the gain is real and the buyback is adding to it. The pair guards against mistaking share-count shrinkage for genuine earnings growth.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow is the cash left after the company pays for the spending needed to run and maintain the business. A buyback should be funded from free cash flow, not from borrowing or from selling assets. Reading the two together shows whether the company is returning cash it actually has or reaching beyond its means.

**What the combination tells you.** A buyback comfortably smaller than free cash flow is sustainable and leaves room for dividends and debt repayment. A buyback that exceeds free cash flow means the company is funding repurchases with debt or cash reserves, which is risky if cash flow later weakens. The pair shows whether the buyback rests on real surplus or on borrowed money.

### Net Cash From Financing Activities

**Why read the two together.** Net cash from financing activities is the total of the whole financing section, with buybacks, dividends, issuance, and debt netted into one number. Common stock repurchased is one outflow inside that total. Reading the two together shows how large the buyback looms among all the company's dealings with its funders.

**What the combination tells you.** A deeply negative financing total driven mostly by buybacks means returning cash to owners is the dominant financing activity. A financing total pulled the other way by new borrowing means the buyback is being funded partly with debt. The pair places the buyback in the full context of capital coming in and going out.

## Part 8: Limitations

Common stock repurchased reports the cash spent but not the price paid per share. The same dollar figure can be a shrewd purchase of cheap shares or a wasteful purchase of expensive ones, and the line looks the same either way. The reader must compare the spend against the share price and the change in share count to judge the quality of the buyback. The line also cannot tell a value-building buyback from one that merely offsets the dilution of employee stock pay.

The line says nothing about timing skill, which is where most buybacks succeed or fail. Companies tend to have the most cash at market peaks and the least at market bottoms, so many buy high and stop low, the reverse of good value. A buyback funded with debt can also raise per-share figures while quietly weakening the balance sheet. The amount alone hides all of this, so the line is a starting point and not a verdict on capital allocation.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How large was the buyback as a percent of revenue, and did it shrink the share count or merely hold it flat?
2. What was the share price in the years of the largest buybacks, and was the company buying cheap or dear?
3. How does the buyback compare with the stock-based compensation expense, and is most of it just offsetting employee dilution?
4. Did the buyback rise in strong, high-price years and fall in weak, low-price years, showing poor timing?
5. Was the buyback funded from free cash flow, or did the company borrow or draw down cash to pay for it?
6. How much did EPS growth over the period come from the buyback rather than from rising total profit?
7. For a cyclical producer, did the buyback peak at the top of the commodity cycle when the stock was most expensive?
8. For a bank, did regulators pause or cap the buyback, and is the program likely to resume?
9. Did management buy back shares while also issuing them, and what was the net change in ownership?
10. Could the cash spent on the buyback have earned a higher return inside the business or as a dividend instead?
