# Interest Debt Per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Interest debt per share measures how many dollars of interest-bearing borrowing stand behind each share of the company. Interest-bearing debt is borrowed money on which the company pays interest, such as bank loans, bonds, notes payable, and the debt portion of leases. Interest-bearing debt excludes non-interest items such as accounts payable, which is money owed to suppliers and carries no interest. Shares outstanding is the total number of ownership units currently held by investors. The metric answers one question: how much borrowing must be serviced and repaid for each share before the equity owners have a free claim on the assets?

Interest debt per share puts borrowing into the same per-share units as the other lines on this page. A reading of twenty dollars on a stock that trades at eighty means twenty dollars of interest-bearing debt sits behind each share. Lenders are paid out of the business before shareholders, so the figure shows the size of the claim that ranks ahead of the equity. The figure becomes most useful when read beside cash per share, earnings per share, and book value per share.

Common readings of the metric:

| Debt-per-share level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Low against the share price and per-share equity | Each share carries little borrowing. The downside is bounded by a clean balance sheet. |
| Moderate against the share price and per-share equity | The company uses borrowing as part of a deliberate financing plan. The level is workable when cash flows are steady. |
| High against the share price and per-share equity | Each share is a thin slice of equity sitting on a large stack of debt. The per-share earnings and book value are amplified by leverage. |
| High and rising into a weak stretch | The borrowing per share is growing at the wrong moment. Refinancing risk climbs as the business slows. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Interest Debt Per Share = Interest-Bearing Debt ÷ Shares Outstanding`

Interest-bearing debt is the top number. The figure adds short-term debt and long-term debt that carry interest. Short-term debt is borrowing due within a year, including the current portion of debt, which is the slice of a long-term loan that must be repaid in the next twelve months. Long-term debt is borrowing due after a year. The debt portion of leases is also included. Accounts payable and other operating accruals are left out because those are supplier credit rather than financing.

Shares outstanding is the bottom number. Shares outstanding is the total count of ownership units held by investors right now. Dividing the debt by the share count spreads the total borrowing across each unit of ownership, which states the leverage in the same per-share form as the rest of the page.

A stricter variant nets out the cash the company holds:

| Version | Formula | What the version changes |
|---|---|---|
| Gross interest debt per share | Interest-Bearing Debt ÷ Shares Outstanding | Counts the full borrowing per share. This is the version the app shows. |
| Net debt per share | (Interest-Bearing Debt − Cash) ÷ Shares Outstanding | Net debt is interest debt minus cash. Subtracting cash first gives the truer claim per share, because the company could use the cash to pay down the debt. |

This metric uses gross interest-bearing debt. Read it beside cash per share to reach the net figure.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

Per-share analysis without the debt line tells the story in the equity's favor. A stock at fifty dollars with five dollars of debt per share and a stock at fifty dollars with forty dollars of debt per share are very different securities. The second is a small slice of equity balanced on a column of borrowing. Its per-share earnings and book value are lifted by financial leverage, which is the use of debt relative to equity to fund the business.

Financial leverage magnifies shareholder returns when the business out-earns the cost of borrowing, and it magnifies losses when the business does not. Interest debt per share labels the size of that leverage in owner units, so the investor can see how much of the per-share figures rests on borrowed money.

The line also makes whole-company thinking simple at the per-share level. Share price plus net debt per share approximates what a buyer pays per share for the entire business. The same per-share debt figure is the starting point for recovery math, which asks what each share would be worth if the assets were sold and the lenders were paid first.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The numerator moves with real financing events. Borrowing to fund capital spending, acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends raises interest-bearing debt. Repaying loans lowers it. Lease accounting changes can add debt obligations without any new borrowing decision.

The denominator deserves close attention because the share count cuts against intuition. Buybacks shrink the share count, which raises debt per share even when total debt is flat. A buyback funded by new debt raises the figure twice over, because the borrowing rises while the share count falls. Issuing new shares spreads the debt across more units and lowers the figure, while diluting every other per-share line at the same time. The trend in interest debt per share should always be checked against the trend in total debt. Debt per share rising while total debt is flat is the share count's doing, which is a different fact from the company borrowing more.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

Sector norms follow the usual leverage patterns. The level that is normal in one industry would be alarming in another.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities, telecom, and real estate | High and stable debt per share is normal | Cash flows are steady and predictable, so lenders accept a large debt load. |
| Capital-light software | Little or no interest debt per share | The business needs few hard assets and funds itself from operations. |
| Cyclical industrials and miners | Dangerous if it stays high into a downturn | Earnings can fall sharply, and a large fixed debt per share does not shrink with the revenue. |
| Banks | The metric is not comparable | Debt is the raw material of the banking business rather than a financing choice. |
| Buyout-owned firms | Very high debt per share by design | The owners deliberately load the business with borrowing to concentrate returns on a thin slice of equity. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the two companies have different normal capital structures. Compare a company against direct peers, against cash per share for the net figure, and against its own history for the direction of travel.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The per-share trend chart shows each share's debt burden over time. The first step is always to decide whether a move came from the debt or the share count, because the two have different meanings. A falling line from repayment is deleveraging, and each share's claim grows freer. A falling line from new share issuance is dilution, which lowers debt per share while weakening every other per-share figure.

A rising line from borrowing should point to what the money bought. The asset and revenue lines show whether the borrowing funded productive capacity or funded distributions. A rising line from debt-funded buybacks is a concentration trade, where the remaining owners accept more debt per share in exchange for a larger slice of the business. A steady level usually means stable financing. A share-count change can move the per-share figure even when total debt is flat, so the total-debt line must be read alongside. Watch the line against the business cycle, because debt per share that ratchets higher at each peak is the pattern that tends to end badly.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Interest Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest debt per share measures the size of the borrowing behind each share. Interest coverage measures whether the company's operating income can carry the interest on that borrowing. Size and affordability are different questions, and a full view of the debt needs both answers.

**What the combination tells you.** A high debt per share with a high interest coverage ratio means a large load carried cheaply, which is exposed to refinancing risk when rates rise. A high debt per share with a low coverage ratio means the borrowing is already straining the earnings. A low debt per share with strong coverage points to conservative finances. Reading the pair separates the amount of the debt from the cost of holding it.

### Cash Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Interest debt per share counts the gross borrowing behind each share. Cash per share counts the liquid assets behind each share. The difference between the two is net debt per share, which is the truer claim against the equity. The gross figure overstates the burden at a company that holds a large cash balance.

**What the combination tells you.** Subtracting cash per share from interest debt per share shows the real leverage each share carries. A company with high gross debt per share and an equally high cash per share is far safer than the gross figure alone suggests. A company with high debt per share and little cash has nowhere to turn if earnings slip. Netting the two should come before any verdict on the debt.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Why read the two together.** Interest debt per share states the borrowing in owner units. Net Debt / EBITDA states the borrowing as a number of years of earnings needed to repay it. The per-share figure is intuitive for an owner, and the years-of-earnings figure is the standard yardstick lenders use. The two describe the same debt from different angles.

**What the combination tells you.** A debt per share that looks large can still be modest in years of earnings if the company earns a lot. A debt per share that looks small can be heavy if earnings are thin. Reading the pair keeps the per-share figure honest by anchoring it to the cash the business actually produces. A high reading on both measures is the clearest sign of a stretched balance sheet.

### Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Book value per share is the accounting equity behind each share. Interest debt per share is the borrowing behind each share. Placing the two side by side shows the capital structure in per-share form, which is the mix of debt and equity funding the business. The relationship is the per-share version of the debt-to-equity ratio.

**What the combination tells you.** A debt per share that dwarfs the book value per share means the business is funded mostly by lenders, and the equity is a thin cushion. A debt per share that is small next to the book value per share means the owners fund most of the business themselves. Watching both lines over time shows whether the company is shifting its funding toward debt or toward equity.

## Part 8: Limitations

Interest debt per share measures gross borrowing and ignores the cash the company holds against it. Net debt per share is the truer figure, so the metric should never be read without cash per share beside it. A single reading is a snapshot. The snapshot does not show when the debt comes due, and it does not show whether the rates are fixed or floating. A floating rate is an interest rate that resets with the market, so a floating-rate debt becomes more expensive as soon as market rates rise.

The metric also says nothing about loan conditions or covenants, which are the rules a lender attaches to a loan. Off-balance-sheet obligations and some lease commitments may be understated, so the true claim can be larger than the figure shows. The per-share framing moves with buybacks even when total debt is unchanged, which is useful for analysis but a trap when the figure is used for quick screening. The metric is the honest counterweight to the other per-share lines and is built to be read beside them rather than alone.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What was the borrowing behind the current debt per share used for, and do the revenue, asset, and cash flow lines show that spending producing a return?
2. When does the debt come due, and how much of the per-share burden must be refinanced within the next three years?
3. What is net debt per share after cash per share is subtracted, and what fraction of the current share price does that net claim represent?
4. Did the trend on the chart come from the debt or the share count, and if buybacks drove it, were the repurchase prices good enough to justify each share carrying more debt?
5. How many years of free cash flow per share would it take to retire the current debt per share, and how does that compare with the company's own history and its sector?
6. What are the interest rates and how much of the debt carries a floating rate, and what happens to coverage if the debt reprices at current market rates?
7. How does debt per share against the share price compare across the closest competitors, and which of them is mostly equity and which is mostly obligation at today's prices?
8. Has debt per share ratcheted higher across the chart's cycles, and were the increases funding productive investment or funding distributions?
9. What loan conditions or covenants reference the debt level, and how large an earnings decline would break them?
10. If the company faced a two-year stretch with no access to new borrowing, would cash per share, free cash flow per share, and the maturity schedule carry it through?
