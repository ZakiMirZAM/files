# Tangible Book Value Per Share

## Part 1: Definition

Tangible book value per share measures what each share owns in hard, physical, sellable assets after every debt is repaid. Shareholders' equity is the company's net worth on the books. Shareholders' equity equals total assets minus total liabilities. Shareholders' equity is what owners would have left if every asset were sold at its recorded value and every debt repaid. Shares outstanding is the total number of ownership units currently held by investors. Tangible book value per share takes shareholders' equity, removes goodwill and intangible assets, then divides by shares outstanding. The metric answers one question: what hard net worth backs each share?

Goodwill is the extra amount an acquirer paid for a business above the recorded value of that business's net assets. Goodwill appears only after an acquisition. Intangible assets are non-physical recorded assets such as patents, brand names, and customer lists. Tangible book value per share removes both layers because their worth depends on appraisals and on past deals working out. What remains is equity backed by assets with independent physical existence such as cash, inventory, and equipment.

Common readings of the metric:

| Level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Negative | Goodwill and intangibles are larger than book equity. Common at acquisitive or brand-driven companies. It is not by itself a sign of failure. |
| Small positive relative to price | The market is paying mostly for earning power and intangible assets, not for hard net worth. |
| Large positive relative to price | A big share of the price is covered by physical, sellable net assets. Common in asset-heavy industries. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Tangible Book Value Per Share = (Shareholders' Equity − Goodwill − Intangible Assets) ÷ Shares Outstanding`

Shareholders' equity is the starting point. Shareholders' equity is total assets minus total liabilities, the net worth recorded on the books. Goodwill is then subtracted. Goodwill is the premium an acquirer paid above the recorded value of an acquired business's net assets. Goodwill does not have to be replaced to keep operating, so it is removed from the hard-asset count. Intangible assets are subtracted next. Intangible assets are non-physical recorded assets such as patents, trademarks, and customer lists. The values of intangible assets come from appraisals rather than from a market sale, so they are also removed.

Shares outstanding is the bottom number. Shares outstanding is the total number of ownership units currently held by investors. Dividing by shares outstanding turns the tangible net worth into a per-share figure that can be compared against the share price.

Tangible book value per share is the same as plain book value per share with one change. Book value per share divides full shareholders' equity by shares outstanding. Tangible book value per share subtracts goodwill and intangible assets first. The gap between the two figures is exactly the goodwill and intangibles layer per share.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The metric shows what the equity is worth when optimism is set aside. Goodwill and intangible assets keep their recorded value only as long as the related businesses and brands perform. Removing both leaves the net worth most likely to survive a bad stretch. The metric answers what each share would still own if the soft assets were written off.

Bank and insurance analysts treat tangible book value as the standard. A bank's goodwill absorbs no losses in a crisis. Regulators watch tangible equity closely, and takeover prices for banks are quoted against tangible book value. The metric states the capital that actually stands behind the deposits and loans.

The metric is also a downside check anywhere. In serious trouble, goodwill is written off first by definition. The tangible figure approximates the layer that creditors and shareholders would actually divide. A company whose book value grows while tangible book value stalls is building net worth from acquisition premiums rather than from real assets.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Retained earnings build the figure over time. Retained earnings are the profits a company keeps instead of paying out as dividends. Dividends, losses, and share buybacks drain the figure. Dividends, losses, and buybacks are the same forces that move plain book value per share.

The distinctive movers are acquisitions and write-downs. Each acquisition usually lowers tangible book value per share right away. The acquirer spends cash or shares that were tangible, and the premium paid becomes goodwill that is then removed. A frequent acquirer can grow overall while its tangible book value shrinks toward or below zero. A write-down, also called an impairment, is an accounting entry that lowers the recorded value of an asset that is no longer worth its book amount. Impairing goodwill cuts book value but leaves tangible book value untouched, because goodwill was already removed. So the gap between the two figures narrows in years when goodwill is written down.

Internally built intangibles never enter either figure. A company that builds its own software or brand spends the money as an expense. Buybacks above tangible book value per share reduce the per-share figure by simple arithmetic, the same trap as with plain book value.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The metric matters in some sectors and is close to useless in others. Net asset value is total assets minus total liabilities, the same idea as book equity. The metric is meaningful only where that net asset value sits in separable physical assets.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisitive companies | Large goodwill, tangible book far below full book value | Years of paying premiums above recorded net assets pile up goodwill that the metric removes. |
| Capital-light software and consumer brands | Tangible book small, zero, or negative | The real value sits in code, brands, and customer lists that were expensed as built, so the metric understates these businesses. |
| Banks and insurers | Tangible book watched closely | Goodwill absorbs no losses, regulators track tangible equity, and crisis and takeover math run on tangible book. |
| Heavy industry, utilities, real estate | Tangible book is most of book value | Worth lives in plant, equipment, and property, all physical and sellable, so the metric is meaningful. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when the businesses hold their value in different forms. Comparing a software firm's tangible book against a utility's tangible book tells you nothing useful. Compare a company only against others in the same sector.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read this line beside plain book value per share, because the pair tells a story neither line tells alone. Both lines rising together is organic growth from retained earnings, the cleanest pattern. The full book line rising while the tangible line flatlines or falls means growth is being purchased through acquisitions rather than built. The wider that divergence grows, the more future write-down risk sits on the balance sheet.

A single-year jump in the tangible line toward the full book line is usually an impairment. The gap is closing because goodwill was written off, not because hard assets grew. A big step down in the tangible line right after an acquisition is the premium becoming goodwill. For a bank, the tangible line's own path is the franchise's report card. Steady compounding through good and bad years marks a business that earns its keep, while a crisis-era plunge measures the losses shareholders absorbed. A tangible book value that has gone negative ends the line's usefulness, since the chart after that point records buyback arithmetic rather than real net worth. Remember that buybacks above tangible book value per share pull the per-share line down.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** Book value per share divides full shareholders' equity by shares outstanding. Tangible book value per share removes goodwill and intangible assets first. Reading both at once shows how much of the recorded net worth is hard and how much is soft.

**What the combination tells you.** The gap between the two figures is the goodwill and intangibles layer per share. A small gap means the balance sheet net worth is mostly physical and sellable. A wide gap means a large part of the book value rests on past acquisitions and appraised intangibles that must keep performing to hold their value.

### P/B

**Why read the two together.** P/B is the share price divided by book value per share. Recomputing the same multiple against tangible book value per share shows the price paid for hard net worth alone. The two multiples together separate the premium for assets from the premium for goodwill and intangibles.

**What the combination tells you.** A low P/B that becomes a high price-to-tangible-book means most of the book value was soft, and the apparent cheapness was thinner than it looked. A price near or below tangible book value means physical net assets cover most of the purchase, with the intangible value coming on top. The pair shows how much of the market price is backed by sellable assets.

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** ROIC is the after-tax operating profit divided by the capital invested in the business. Comparing the return against the tangible base shows what the business earns on real, physical capital. A company can post a healthy ROE while most of its equity is goodwill that earns nothing extra.

**What the combination tells you.** A strong return on a thin tangible base means the business earns its profit from advantages that the balance sheet cannot show, such as brands and customer relationships. An ordinary return on a heavy tangible base means the company competes with assets that any rival can finance and buy. Reading the return against the tangible capital is the stricter test of operating quality.

### Graham Number

**Why read the two together.** The Graham Number is a rough fair-value estimate built from earnings per share and book value per share. Book value per share is one of its two inputs. Swapping in the stricter tangible book value per share shows how much of that estimated floor depends on soft assets.

**What the combination tells you.** A Graham Number that drops sharply when tangible book value replaces full book value was leaning on goodwill and intangibles for its support. A Graham Number that barely moves was already resting on hard net worth. The comparison shows whether the conservative value anchor is backed by sellable assets or by appraisal figures.

## Part 8: Limitations

The recorded values on the balance sheet are historical cost, not current market value. Property bought decades ago may be worth far more than its recorded value, while dated inventory may be worth far less. So even the hard figure is only an approximation of what the assets would fetch in a sale. Removing every intangible is also too harsh for businesses whose real value is genuinely intangible. A purchased patent or a long-term contract can be the firm's most valuable asset, yet the metric strips it out completely.

The metric can go negative for healthy companies and says nothing about earning power. A profitable brand-led company can carry a negative tangible book value and still compound for years. Write-downs depend on management estimates of when goodwill has lost its value, and those estimates can lag reality. So the tangible figure is the right anchor for banks and asset-heavy firms and a useful skeptic's check elsewhere, but it is a measure of worth only where physical assets genuinely are the business.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. How wide is the gap between book value per share and tangible book value per share, and which past acquisitions built that gap?
2. How much goodwill sits on the balance sheet today, and how have the acquisitions behind it performed against the premiums paid?
3. Has the tangible line compounded alongside the full book line across the chart, or has growth been increasingly purchased rather than built?
4. For a bank or insurer, what is the price against tangible book value, and how does that compare with direct peers?
5. Is a negative or small tangible book value here the result of an expensed-asset business model, or of a balance sheet with genuinely thin hard backing?
6. What would each major tangible asset class realize in an orderly sale versus its recorded value, looking at inventory and receivables and property in turn?
7. Which goodwill balances on the books today look most likely to be written down later, and what would each write-down do to the full book line?
8. Have recent buybacks been executed above or below tangible book value per share, and what have they done to the per-share figure?
9. What return does the business earn on its tangible capital alone, and does that tell a different quality story than headline return on equity?
10. Does the current price pay mostly for the tangible layer or for the intangible layer, and which layer does the investment thesis depend on surviving?
