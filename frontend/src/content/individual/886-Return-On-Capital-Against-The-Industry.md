# Return on Capital Against the Industry

## Part 1: Definition

The industry return on invested capital is the average return earned by the companies filed in the same industry as this one, weighted by size. Set beside the company's own return, it asks whether the company earns more than the businesses it competes with.

The spread comparison asks a different and better question. The spread is the return on invested capital minus the weighted average cost of capital. Comparing this company's spread with the industry spread measures value creation rather than raw profitability, and the two comparisons can disagree.

| Comparison | What the comparison measures |
|---|---|
| Return against industry return | Whether the company is more profitable than its rivals |
| Spread against industry spread | Whether the company creates more value than its rivals |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The company's own figures are:

`Spread = ROIC − WACC`

The industry figures are built by computing the same two numbers for every company in the industry and then averaging them, weighted by market value converted to one currency. The comparison is then:

`Return gap = Company ROIC − Industry ROIC`

`Spread gap = Company spread − Industry spread`

Both gaps are reported in percentage points.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used

A return of 52 percent means nothing on its own. It means something once you know whether the businesses this company competes with earn 10 percent or 50 percent.

The spread matters separately because two companies can earn the same return on capital while one creates value and the other destroys it. A company with a lower return but much cheaper capital can be the better business. Comparing returns alone hides that.

## Part 4: What influences the output

An industry average is only comparable when every company inside it is measured the same way. Different capital definitions, different accounting rules, and different reporting currencies all break the comparison, and each breaks it in a specific direction.

Industry membership matters as much as the arithmetic. Labels are assigned to companies as they trade today, so a company can be filed alongside businesses it barely competes with. A benchmark built from companies that are not rivals is not a benchmark.

Survivorship is the largest distortion. An average built only from companies still trading today omits every company that failed, and the failures were the low returns. The average therefore reads high, and a weak company measured against it looks ordinary.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

One year above the industry can be luck. A company that has beaten its industry in most years of a window has evidence of something structural, and the shape of the gap over time separates a durable advantage from a run of good years.

Watch the gap rather than the level. Both the company and its industry move with the cycle, so a company holding a steady gap through a downturn is telling you more than one posting a high return in a boom.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return on Equity

**Why read the two together.** The return on capital comparison is measured before the effect of borrowing and the return on equity comparison is measured after it. Reading both against the same industry separates operating advantage from balance sheet choice.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that beats its industry modestly on return on capital while beating it enormously on return on equity has not found an operating advantage. It has borrowed more than its rivals, and the extra return carries the risk that borrowing carries.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** The spread has two halves, and a spread gap can open because the return rose or because the cost of capital fell. Reading the cost of capital against the industry says which half moved.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose spread beats its industry purely because its capital is cheaper is being rewarded for a lower risk profile rather than a better business. That is still an advantage, but it is a different one and it can be competed away by a rival that refinances.

## Part 8: Limitations

The industry average available here is built in-house from companies trading today, so it carries survivorship bias and its industry labels are current rather than historical. It is a working benchmark, not an unbiased one, and a company that merely matches this average is probably below the true long run average of everyone who ever competed.

The average is also a single number standing in for a distribution. An industry containing two dominant companies and eighty small ones has an average that describes the two, and this company is being compared against that.
