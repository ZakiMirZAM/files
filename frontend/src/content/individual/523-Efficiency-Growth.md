# Efficiency Growth (Changing ROIC)

## Part 1: Definition

Efficiency growth is the part of a company's expected growth that comes from earning a better return on the assets the company already owns. Return on invested capital, shortened to ROIC, is the after-tax operating profit the company earns for every dollar of capital tied up in the business. Most growth calculations count only the growth that new investment buys. A company that raises the return on the asset base it already owns grows without spending a single new dollar, and efficiency growth is that second source.

The term answers one question: how much of the forecast growth is coming from a rising return rather than from new spending? One point must be stated at the start. Efficiency growth is temporary by construction. A return on capital cannot keep rising forever, so once the return stops improving the term falls to zero and only the growth bought with new investment remains.

Common readings of the term:

| Reading | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| 0 | The return on capital did not improve. Every point of forecast growth is being paid for with new investment. |
| 0.01 to 0.03 | A small improvement in the return. The contribution to growth is real but minor. |
| 0.03 to 0.07 | A meaningful improvement in the return. The reason behind the improvement needs to be identified before the growth is trusted. |
| Warned, not counted | The return rose in the latest year only, without a rise the year before. A one-year jump is far more often a restatement or a margin blip than a trend, so the app reports the jump as a warning and leaves the term out of the forecast. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Efficiency Growth = (ROIC this year − ROIC last year) ÷ ROIC last year`

The top number is the improvement in the return, measured in percentage points. A company whose ROIC moved from 10 percent to 11 percent improved the return by one percentage point. The improvement belongs in the calculation because a higher return applied to the same capital base produces more operating profit with no new spending at all.

The bottom number is last year's return, which is the base the improvement is measured against. Dividing by last year's return converts the improvement into a growth rate. The 10 percent to 11 percent move gives 0.01 ÷ 0.10, which is 0.10, so the company grows ten percent from the efficiency gain alone.

The term does not stand on its own. The app adds the term to the growth that new investment buys, so the full expression is:

`Fundamental Growth = (Reinvestment Rate × ROIC) + Efficiency Growth`

The reinvestment rate is the share of after-tax operating profit the company puts back into the business as new equipment, new facilities, and additional working capital. Working capital is the money tied up in inventory and in bills owed by customers, less the bills the company has not yet paid. The first part of the expression is the growth new investment buys. The second part is the growth a rising return on the existing base delivers for free.

Two rules govern how the app applies the term:

| Rule | What the rule does |
|---|---|
| Only an improving return counts | A falling return is not subtracted. The forecast already leans on the company's historical revenue trend, and a company is not assumed to shrink on this account. |
| The term needs a trend | The term is counted only when the return has risen across two consecutive years. Without that rule, one year's jump in the return would dominate a growth forecast that is meant to describe many years; a single-year rise is reported as a warning instead. |

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A growth forecast built only on reinvestment misses a real source of growth. A company that fixes its pricing, cuts waste from its cost base, or gets more sales out of the same factories will earn more next year on assets already paid for. Efficiency growth captures that gain, and a purely backward-looking growth rate cannot see it at all.

The term also separates two kinds of growth that look identical in the earnings line. Growth bought with new investment costs the company money and continues for as long as the company keeps investing at the same return. Growth from a rising return costs nothing and continues only while the return keeps rising. Reading the two parts separately shows which kind of growth the forecast is resting on.

The honest use of the term is as a warning as much as a contribution. A company whose forecast growth comes mostly from efficiency growth is borrowing from a source that runs out. Once the return stops improving the term goes to zero, and the growth that remains is only what reinvestment can buy.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Anything that moves the return on capital moves the term, because the term is nothing more than the percentage change in that return. The return rises when the operating margin rises, meaning the company keeps more operating profit out of each dollar of sales. The return also rises when the company squeezes more sales out of the same capital base, which shows up as a higher ratio of sales to invested capital. A falling effective tax rate raises the after-tax operating profit and therefore raises the return with no operating improvement behind it.

The denominator of the return can move on its own, and the effects are easy to mistake for real improvement. An asset write-off removes book value from invested capital, so the same profit is now divided by a smaller base and the return jumps. Selling a division, running down inventory, or letting the asset base age without replacement all shrink invested capital the same way. A one-time gain sitting inside operating income lifts the return for a single year and then disappears.

Watch the denominator of the metric itself, which is last year's return. When last year's return was very small, the percentage improvement is enormous even when the improvement in the actual return is tiny. A return that moves from 1 percent to 2 percent is an improvement of only one percentage point, and yet the arithmetic reports a doubling. The cap at 0.10 is what keeps that arithmetic from wrecking the growth forecast.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a normal reading depends on how stable returns are in the industry and on how large the capital base is.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Turnaround and restructuring situations | Large positive readings for a few years, then nothing | The return starts from a low base, so small gains in the return produce very large percentage improvements. |
| Cyclical companies such as miners, chemicals, and auto makers | Large positive readings on the upswing, zero or negative at the peak | The return rises with the price cycle rather than with any lasting improvement in the business. |
| Software and other capital-light companies | Large and unstable readings | The invested capital base is small, so any change in profit moves the return sharply. |
| Regulated utilities | Close to zero most years | Regulators set the allowed return, which leaves little room for the return to climb. |
| Mature consumer companies | Small and infrequent | The return already sits near its long-run level, so there is little left to improve. |
| Banks, insurers, and REITs | Do not use | Invested capital is not a meaningful denominator for these companies, so the return it feeds and the term built on it carry no signal. |

Comparing the term across sectors is meaningless. The reading depends on the size of last year's return, and a low-return industry will always produce bigger percentage improvements than a high-return one from the same operating gain. Compare a company against its own history and against direct competitors at the same point in the business cycle.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A line that sits at zero in most years and spikes once is the normal pattern for a healthy, stable company. The spike marks a year in which the return improved, and the return to zero afterwards marks the year the improvement stopped. A single spike is not a problem. A single spike being carried into a long-run growth forecast is a problem.

A line that stays positive for several consecutive years means the return has been climbing year after year. Such a run is worth investigating rather than celebrating. Either the company is genuinely closing a gap against better-run competitors, which is real but finite, or the capital base is shrinking through write-offs and asset sales while profit holds steady. The second case produces the same chart and none of the value.

A line pinned at the 0.10 cap in the latest year means the raw improvement was larger than the cap allowed. Read that reading as a flag rather than as a number. Check whether last year's return was unusually small, because a small base inflates the percentage change without much real improvement behind it. Compare the term against the ROIC chart directly. A ROIC line that climbs smoothly supports the term, and a ROIC line that jumps once after a collapse does not.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** Efficiency growth is nothing more than the percentage change in ROIC from one year to the next. Reading the term without reading the underlying return leaves the reader with a growth number and no idea what produced it. The ROIC chart shows the level of the return and the path the return has taken over several years.

**What the combination tells you.** A high efficiency growth reading sitting on top of a ROIC that is still low means a company recovering from a weak base, and the percentage gain flatters a return that is still poor. A high reading on top of a ROIC that is already strong means the company is pulling further ahead, which is rare and usually cannot last. A ROIC line that has been climbing steadily for years supports treating the term as a genuine trend, while a ROIC line that jumped once means the term is capturing a single event.

### Reinvestment Rate

**Why read the two together.** The reinvestment rate is the other half of the fundamental growth expression the app uses. The reinvestment rate tells you how much growth the company is buying with new money, and efficiency growth tells you how much growth is arriving free from a better return. Splitting the forecast into the two parts shows which one the growth actually depends on.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a healthy reinvestment rate and a zero efficiency term is growing the durable way, because the growth continues for as long as the company keeps investing at the same return. A company with a low reinvestment rate and a large efficiency term is growing on a source that runs out, and the forecast will collapse toward zero as soon as the return stops improving. A negative or near-zero reinvestment rate combined with a large efficiency term is the specific case Damodaran describes for companies that overinvested in the past and are now living off assets already bought.

### Marginal ROIC

**Why read the two together.** Marginal ROIC is the return the company earns on the new money it invested in a year, while ROIC is the return earned across all the capital in the business. Efficiency growth is built on the change in the average return, not the marginal one. Comparing the two returns shows whether the improvement is coming from better new projects or from better use of assets already owned.

**What the combination tells you.** A marginal ROIC well above the average ROIC means new investments are earning more than the old ones, and the average return will keep drifting upward for several years as the new projects grow in the mix. A marginal ROIC below the average return means the average is being dragged down, and any positive efficiency term is coming from somewhere other than new projects. When the average return improves while the marginal return does not, look hard at the capital base for write-offs and asset sales that shrank the denominator.

### EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is operating profit as a share of sales, where operating profit is what the business earns before interest and taxes are taken out. The margin is one of the two levers that move ROIC, the other being how many dollars of sales the company gets from each dollar of capital. Checking the margin identifies which lever produced the improvement in the return.

**What the combination tells you.** A rising EBIT margin alongside a positive efficiency term means the improvement came from pricing or from cost control, both of which are visible in the income statement and can be tested against competitors. A flat margin alongside a positive efficiency term means the improvement came from the capital side, either from genuinely better use of assets or from a capital base that shrank. A margin that jumped once on a one-time gain explains a term that will not repeat.

## Part 8: Limitations

The term is temporary by construction, and treating the term as permanent is the single largest error a reader can make with it. A return on capital that keeps rising every year forever would eventually reach an impossible level, so the improvement must stop. A forecast that leans on efficiency growth for most of its growth is describing a few good years and calling them the long run. Read the term as a contribution that decays to zero rather than as a growth rate the company can hold.

The arithmetic is fragile in ways the number itself does not show. A small denominator, meaning a very low return last year, produces an enormous percentage improvement from a tiny real gain, and the cap at 0.10 exists only because that distortion is so common. The term also cannot tell a real operating improvement apart from a shrinking capital base, because a write-off and a genuine efficiency gain raise the return in exactly the same way. The app counts only improvements and never subtracts a falling return, so the term is a one-sided adjustment by design and gives no warning when the return is deteriorating. Companies with a zero or negative return on capital get no term at all, since a percentage change from a base of zero has no meaning.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What produced this year's improvement in the return on capital? Name the operating change, and check whether the change appears in the EBIT margin or in sales per dollar of invested capital.
2. Was last year's return unusually low? If the base year was a bad year, how much of the reading is real improvement and how much is the arithmetic of a small denominator?
3. Did invested capital shrink this year? Check for write-offs, divestments, and an asset base that is not being replaced, all of which raise the return without any operating improvement.
4. Is the improvement in the return present in the ROIC chart as a steady multi-year climb, or does the chart show one jump after a weak year?
5. How much of the app's fundamental growth comes from the efficiency term rather than from the reinvestment term? If the term were set to zero, what growth rate would remain?
6. How many more years can the return keep rising? Compare the current return against the best competitors in the same industry, because the gap to the leaders sets a rough ceiling on how far the improvement can run.
7. Is the improvement in the return coming from new projects or from existing assets? Compare marginal ROIC against ROIC to find out.
8. Did any one-time gain, a lower tax rate, or an accounting change lift operating profit this year? What is the return with those items removed?
9. If the company is cyclical, is the rising return simply the price cycle turning up? What was the return at the last cycle trough, and what would the efficiency term look like at the next one?
10. Is management describing the efficiency gain as a permanent step change or as a recovery toward a normal level? What specific evidence supports the claim, and does the reinvestment rate look high enough to keep growth going once the gain runs out?
