# Quick Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The quick ratio measures whether a company can pay its short-term bills using only the assets that turn into cash fast. Quick assets are the assets a company can convert to cash within days. The three quick assets are cash, marketable securities, and money owed by customers. Marketable securities are short-term investments that can be sold quickly, such as government bills. Money owed by customers is called accounts receivable. Current liabilities are the bills due within one year. Examples include supplier invoices, wages, and the part of debt due this year. Liquidity is the ability to pay those bills on time.

The quick ratio answers one question: can the company cover its short-term bills without selling inventory? Inventory is the goods a company holds to sell, including raw materials and finished products. Inventory is left out of the quick ratio because selling inventory takes time and sometimes requires a discount. The quick ratio is also called the acid test, because the ratio applies a strict test to short-term financial strength.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 0.50 | Quick assets cover less than half of the bills due this year. The company leans heavily on selling inventory or on new borrowing to stay current. |
| 0.50 to 1.00 | Quick assets cover most but not all of the near-term bills. Steady collections and a healthy cash position make this workable. |
| 1.00 to 1.50 | Quick assets cover every bill due this year with room to spare. This app's quality score awards a pass at 1.00 or higher. |
| Above 1.50 | A wide cushion. A very high reading can also signal cash sitting idle that the company is not putting to work. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`Quick Ratio = (Cash + Marketable Securities + Receivables) ÷ Current Liabilities`

The top number is the sum of quick assets. Cash is money on hand and in the bank. Marketable securities are short-term investments that can be sold quickly. Receivables are the amounts customers owe and are expected to pay within the normal collection period. Each of these can become spendable cash within days, so each belongs in a test of near-term payment ability.

The bottom number is current liabilities, the total of all bills coming due within one year. Inventory and prepaid expenses are excluded from the top number on purpose. A prepaid expense is a bill the company already paid in advance, such as a year of insurance, so a prepaid expense returns no cash. Inventory and prepaid expenses are the hardest current assets to turn into cash quickly, which is why the strict version of the liquidity test leaves both out.

The same ratio can be written two ways:

| Variant | Formula | What the variant changes |
|---|---|---|
| Add up the liquid assets | (Cash + Marketable Securities + Receivables) ÷ Current Liabilities | Sums the most liquid assets directly. This app uses this build. |
| Strip out the slow assets | (Current Assets − Inventory − Prepaid Expenses) ÷ Current Liabilities | Starts from total current assets, then removes inventory and prepaid expenses. |

Both variants produce the same answer when reported items are consistent.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

A company can show a healthy profit and still run out of cash to pay this month's bills. The quick ratio checks that risk by counting only the assets that pay bills fast. A reading at or above 1.00 means the most liquid assets alone cover everything due within a year.

The current ratio answers a looser version of the same question. The current ratio divides all current assets, inventory included, by current liabilities. The quick ratio removes inventory from the top number, so the quick ratio reveals how much of the short-term safety depends on selling goods. A company with a strong current ratio and a weak quick ratio is carrying most of its near-term safety in inventory.

The quick ratio matters most when inventory is hard to sell or might lose value. A weak quick ratio warns that the company must keep selling product on schedule, or keep borrowing, just to meet routine obligations. The metric is the strictest common read on whether the company can pay its bills without depending on the next sale.

## Part 4: What influences the output

Three operating drivers move the ratio. The first is the cash balance, which rises when the business collects more than it spends. The second is how fast customers pay, because slow collections leave receivables uncollected and bills unpaid. The third is the level of current liabilities, which climbs when the company buys more on credit from suppliers or when more long-term debt comes due within the year.

Accounting choices and one-off events can move the ratio without the business truly changing. A large drawdown of a credit line raises cash and the ratio on the reporting date, even though the company now owes more. Selling receivables to a third party for early cash, a practice called factoring, shrinks both receivables and the bills paid with that cash. A single big customer payment landing just before the reporting date can lift the ratio for that snapshot only. Because the quick ratio is measured on one day, the timing of payments near that day can flatter or depress the number.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

What counts as a safe quick ratio depends on how much of the business runs on inventory and on how predictable the cash coming in is.

| Industry type | Typical range or behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Grocers and discount retailers | Quick ratio far below the current ratio, often below 0.50 | Most current assets sit in inventory. Goods sell for cash daily, and suppliers are paid later, so a thin quick ratio is normal. |
| General manufacturing | Quick ratio near 0.80 to 1.20, below the current ratio | Inventory of raw materials and finished goods is large, so removing it lowers the reading meaningfully. |
| Software and services | Quick ratio close to the current ratio, often above 1.00 | These firms hold little or no inventory, so the two ratios nearly match. |
| Capital-light subscription businesses | High quick ratio | Cash arrives upfront and inventory is minimal, so liquid assets dominate. |
| Banks | Not applicable | A bank's balance sheet is built from loans and deposits, so the current-asset and current-liability categories do not carry their ordinary meaning. |

Compare a company only against others in the same sector, because the normal level of inventory differs sharply from one industry to the next. A quick ratio that looks alarming for a software firm can be perfectly healthy for a grocer.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A flat line near or above 1.00 means the company consistently holds enough liquid assets to cover near-term bills. A steady rise can mean stronger cash generation, faster collection of receivables, or a deliberate move to a more conservative balance sheet. A steady decline means liquid assets are shrinking relative to the bills due, often because cash is being spent or because current liabilities are growing faster than collections.

A sharp one-year jump or drop is frequently a timing effect rather than a real shift, because the ratio is measured on a single day. A large loan drawn just before the reporting date, or a major customer payment that lands just after, can swing the snapshot. Compare the quick ratio with the current ratio across the same years. A quick ratio that falls while the current ratio holds steady means inventory is making up a growing share of the company's short-term safety.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The current ratio divides all current assets by current liabilities, with inventory included in the top number. The quick ratio removes inventory and prepaid expenses from that top number. The gap between the two ratios is almost entirely the weight of inventory, so reading them side by side isolates how much of the company's short-term safety sits in goods waiting to be sold.

**What the combination tells you.** A current ratio comfortably above 1.50 paired with a quick ratio below 1.00 means the company depends on selling inventory to stay current on its bills. Two ratios that sit close together mean inventory is small, so the company's liquidity does not hinge on moving product. A widening gap over several years means inventory is building up faster than the liquid assets, which can signal slowing sales or overstocking.

### Days Inventory Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days inventory outstanding counts how many days, on average, goods sit before they are sold. A low quick ratio is only a real problem if that inventory is slow to convert into cash. Reading the two together shows whether the inventory excluded from the quick ratio is moving quickly enough to back up the company's bills.

**What the combination tells you.** A low quick ratio combined with fast-moving inventory is far less worrying, because the goods turn into cash before most bills come due. A low quick ratio combined with slow-moving inventory is a genuine warning, because the company's safety rests on goods that may not sell in time. Watching both over several years shows whether a thin quick ratio is supported by a fast sales engine or exposed by a stalling one.

### Days Sales Outstanding

**Why read the two together.** Days sales outstanding counts how many days, on average, customers take to pay. Receivables are a large part of the quick ratio's top number, so the value of those receivables depends on whether customers actually pay on time. A quick ratio built on receivables that collect slowly overstates the company's true ability to pay its own bills.

**What the combination tells you.** A solid quick ratio paired with fast collections means the liquid assets are genuinely available when bills come due. The same quick ratio paired with slow and rising collections means the receivables may not arrive in time, so the real cushion is thinner than the ratio suggests. A jump in days sales outstanding alongside a steady quick ratio can be an early sign that customers are struggling to pay.

## Part 8: Limitations

The quick ratio is a single-day snapshot, so the number reflects the balance sheet on the reporting date rather than a typical day. A payment or a loan drawdown near that date can lift the ratio without any lasting improvement. The ratio also treats all receivables as good, even though some customers pay late and some never pay, so a company with weak collections looks safer than it is.

The quick ratio says nothing about the timing of the bills inside current liabilities, so a company can pass the test yet still face a cash crunch if large payments cluster in the same week. The ratio ignores undrawn credit lines and other backup funding, which means a company with a low quick ratio and a large untapped credit line may be far safer than the number alone suggests. The quick ratio also penalizes inventory-heavy business models that are perfectly healthy, so the reading is only meaningful against peers in the same industry.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Is the current quick ratio the result of a strong cash balance, a large pile of receivables, or simply a low level of current liabilities? Which of those three is doing the work?
2. How does the quick ratio compare with the current ratio? How much of the company's short-term safety disappears once inventory is removed?
3. Are the receivables in the top number collecting on schedule, or is days sales outstanding rising? What is the quick ratio worth if the slow-paying receivables are excluded?
4. Did any large loan drawdown or one-time customer payment land near the reporting date? What was the ratio on a more typical day?
5. Do the bills inside current liabilities come due evenly through the year, or do large payments cluster in a single period that the ratio cannot see?
6. Does the company hold undrawn credit lines or other backup funding that the quick ratio leaves out? How much liquidity does that add beyond the ratio?
7. For this company's industry, is a quick ratio below 1.00 normal or a warning? How does the reading compare with direct competitors?
8. If the quick ratio is well above 1.50, is the extra cash being put to productive use, or is idle cash dragging on returns?
9. Has the quick ratio trended down over several years while the current ratio held steady? Is inventory quietly becoming a larger share of the short-term cushion?
10. In the company's weakest recent year, did the quick ratio stay above 1.00? Could the bills due that year have been paid from liquid assets alone?
