# Price To Book

## Part 1: Definition

The price to book ratio compares what the market charges for a company's shares against the accounting value of the owners' stake in the company. Book value is the owners' stake recorded on the balance sheet, equal to everything the company owns minus everything it owes. The ratio answers one question: how much premium over the recorded value of the company is the market willing to pay?

A ratio of 2 means investors pay two dollars for every dollar of recorded owner value. A ratio below 1 means the market values the company at less than the recorded value of what it owns minus what it owes. The ratio is anchored to the balance sheet rather than to a single year's profit, which makes it one of the steadier valuation measures.

Common readings of the ratio:

| Ratio level | What that level usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | The market values the company below its recorded net worth. Either the assets are mispriced or the returns are too weak to justify book value. |
| 1.0 to 2.0 | A modest premium. Common for steady companies earning fair returns on their equity. |
| 2.0 to 4.0 | A clear premium. The market expects returns well above the cost of the owners' capital. |
| Above 4.0 | A large premium. Usual for companies whose value sits in brands or software the balance sheet cannot record. |
| Negative | Book value is below zero, usually after sustained losses or heavy buybacks. The ratio stops carrying meaning. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The formula is:

`P/B = Price Per Share ÷ Book Value Per Share`

The price per share is the top number. The price per share is the current market price of one share. The price reflects every expectation the market holds about future profit, growth, and risk. The same ratio can be written as total market value of equity divided by total book value of equity.

Book value per share is the bottom number. Book value per share is total shareholders equity divided by the number of shares outstanding. Shareholders equity is the money owners contributed plus all profit kept over the years, minus losses and buybacks. Preferred stock is excluded, because the ratio measures the common owners' stake. A stricter version divides by tangible book value, which removes goodwill and other intangible assets. The app shows Tangible Book Value Per Share alongside for that comparison.

Theory ties the fair ratio to the company's return on equity. The justified multiple is:

`Justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (r − g)`

Here ROE is return on equity, the profit earned for each dollar of book value. The letter r is the cost of equity, the return owners require for the risk they take. The letter g is the expected growth rate. A company earning exactly its cost of equity deserves a ratio of 1.0. Every point above 1.0 must be earned by a return on equity that exceeds the cost of equity. The app's residual income section computes this justified multiple directly. P/B appears in the valuation panel but is not a quality score check.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to evaluate a company

The price to book ratio prices the company against its steadiest accounting anchor. Book value changes slowly because it builds from retained profit and rarely swings the way a single year's earnings can. The ratio gives a stable benchmark even for a company whose profit is volatile or temporarily negative, where an earnings-based ratio cannot be calculated at all.

The ratio also converts directly into a claim about returns. Buying below book value is only a bargain when the company earns more than its cost of equity. Paying a high multiple of book is only sensible when the return on equity is high and durable. The ratio turns instantly into a testable question about the company's returns.

The ratio is the measure of choice for banks and insurers, whose balance sheets are recorded close to real market values and whose earnings are noisy. The ratio also keeps its historical role as a deep-value screen, where a price below book value flags a company the market believes is destroying value, a verdict that is sometimes correct and sometimes a mispricing.

## Part 4: What influences the output

The top number moves with everything that moves share prices, above all the market's expectation of the return the company will earn on its equity. A rising ratio usually reflects the market upgrading its view of that return power. The biggest driver of a change in the ratio is normally a shift in expected return on equity, which is why the ratio and the return on equity chart should be read together.

The bottom number moves with the quieter machinery of book equity. Retained profit builds it, while losses and dividends drain it. Three forces distort it. A buyback above book value shrinks equity faster than the share count, which mechanically inflates the ratio for years. A writedown shrinks the equity base and raises the ratio overnight. Intangibles cut both ways. Brands and software a company builds itself never enter book value, while goodwill from acquisitions does. The ratio can therefore make a capital-light company look expensive and an acquisitive one look cheap.

## Part 5: How to analyze it by industry and sector

The ratio is most meaningful where book value is most honest, which means where assets are recorded close to their real worth.

| Industry type | Typical behavior | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Banks and insurers | The standard measure | Assets are financial and recorded near market value, so the ratio against return on equity is the main valuation tool. |
| Capital-intensive industrials and utilities | Meaningful, usually 1 to 3 | Book values reflect real plant and equipment, so the ratio carries real information. |
| Software, pharma, and branded consumer | Often very high | The most valuable assets are brands and research that the balance sheet cannot record, so the ratio overstates expensiveness. |
| Real estate investment trusts | Use net asset value instead | Recorded property cost differs from current market value, so book value is the wrong anchor. |
| Mature repurchasers | Often distorted | Heavy buybacks shrink book equity, so the ratio runs high and can even turn negative. |

A cross-sector comparison is meaningless when one company's value sits in recorded assets and the other's sits in brands the books cannot see. Within a sector, plot the company against peers on the ratio versus return on equity. The interesting names are those far off the line, cheap for their returns or expensive for them.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

A ratio rising over years means the market is upgrading its view of the company's return power faster than retained profit builds the equity base. The test is whether the return on equity chart confirms the upgrade. A falling ratio against a steady return on equity is the pattern value investors look for, a cheaper price without a deterioration in the business.

A ratio drifting up while the company buys back stock heavily is often a buyback effect rather than a real revaluation. The honest comparison is against tangible book value and against the company's own history before the buybacks. A ratio falling through 1.0 means the market is pricing in either losses ahead or returns stuck below the cost of equity. The app's residual income section shows exactly what return on equity path the current price implies, which turns the chart from sentiment into a checkable forecast.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROE

**Why read the two together.** The price to book ratio prices the owners' equity, and return on equity measures the profit earned on that same equity. The justified multiple formula links the two directly, so a high ratio is only justified by a high and durable return on equity. Reading them apart misses the relationship that drives the whole ratio.

**What the combination tells you.** A price below book value attached to a return on equity above the cost of equity is the genuinely attractive anomaly, a strong business at a cheap price. A price below book attached to weak returns is simply accurate pricing of a poor business. A high ratio is safe only when the return on equity that supports it is wide and defensible against competitors.

### Tangible Book Value Per Share

**Why read the two together.** The standard ratio uses book value, which includes goodwill and other intangibles from past acquisitions. Tangible Book Value Per Share strips those out and leaves only the hard assets. Comparing the two shows how much of the anchor is solid and how much is acquisition residue.

**What the combination tells you.** A ratio that looks reasonable on book value but expensive on tangible book value means much of the equity is goodwill rather than hard assets. A small gap between the two means the book value is mostly tangible and the anchor is solid. Reading both reveals whether the recorded net worth would survive a writedown of past acquisitions.

### P/E

**Why read the two together.** The price to book ratio anchors price to the balance sheet, while the P/E ratio anchors price to a single year's earnings. The two are linked, because the price to book ratio equals the P/E ratio multiplied by return on equity. Comparing them isolates the return assumption built into the price.

**What the combination tells you.** When the two multiples disagree about how expensive a stock looks, the difference comes down to the return on equity. A high price to book ratio with a moderate P/E points to a company earning strong returns on a small equity base. Reading both together separates the price of the assets from the price of the earnings.

### Graham Number

**Why read the two together.** The Graham Number is a conservative valuation built from both earnings per share and book value per share, the same balance-sheet anchor the price to book ratio uses. Both measures lean on recorded net worth as a floor under the price. Reading them together places the current price against a classic conservative ceiling.

**What the combination tells you.** A price below both book value and the Graham Number marks a stock cheap on the traditional asset-based tests. A price far above the Graham Number means the value rests on growth and returns rather than on recorded assets. Reading both shows whether the stock would appeal to a strict asset-based investor or only to one paying for future returns.

## Part 8: Limitations

Book value is recorded at historical cost filtered through accounting rules, so for modern capital-light businesses it omits the assets that matter most. The ratio is nearly useless for software, services, and brand companies, where the real value never reaches the balance sheet. Buybacks and writedowns distort the equity base for years, and goodwill makes acquisitive and organically grown companies hard to compare. A company with sustained losses can show negative book value, which breaks the ratio entirely.

A low ratio on its own has also become a crowded screen, so cheapness on book carries less of an edge than its historical reputation suggests. The ratio says nothing about earnings power, cash flow, or debt by itself. The ratio is the right primary measure for banks and asset-heavy companies and a supporting measure elsewhere. The ratio is always a question about what explains the premium or discount to book, rather than an answer.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. What return on equity does the current ratio imply the market expects, using the justified multiple? Is that expectation above or below what the company has actually delivered on the chart?
2. How much of book value is goodwill and other intangibles? Does the price still look reasonable against tangible book value per share?
3. Has the equity base been shrunk by buybacks or writedowns? What does the ratio look like against the pre-distortion trend of book value?
4. If the ratio is below 1.0, which is mispriced, the assets or the returns? Does the company earn above its cost of equity, and if not, what would change that?
5. If the ratio is high, what named competitive advantage defends the return on equity the price assumes? What would erosion of that advantage look like in the accounts?
6. How does the company plot against its peers on the ratio versus return on equity? What explains its distance from the peer line?
7. For a bank or insurer, how does the ratio compare with the app's residual income estimate? What cost of equity reconciles the two?
8. Are the assets behind book value carried near real value, or are there stale marks and overstated goodwill that make the anchor itself the mispricing?
9. What has the company historically earned when buying back its own shares relative to book and intrinsic value? Is the current buyback building or consuming per-share worth?
10. Over the full chart, has the premium or discount to peers widened or narrowed? Does the direction agree with the relative trend of returns, or has price moved without the fundamentals following?
