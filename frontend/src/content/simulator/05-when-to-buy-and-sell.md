Buying is the easy half. The price either gives you a cushion below what the business is worth, or it does not. Selling is where most beginners lose money, because emotion takes the wheel. This lesson sets out the buy test, what a price is really telling you, and the rules that keep selling rational.

## Buy with a margin of safety

Intrinsic value is the present value of the cash a business will produce for its owners over its life, discounted for risk. The market price is whatever buyers and sellers agree on today. The two drift apart often, and the gap between them is your margin of safety.

You build in that gap because your estimate is imprecise. Every forecast and discount rate carries error, and the margin absorbs the error without wrecking your capital. Buffett's framing, quoted in *Warren Buffett Accounting*: if the bridge holds 30,000 pounds, drive 10,000-pound trucks across it. How wide the margin needs to be depends on how well you know the business. *Modern Value Investing* suggests a 20 to 30 percent discount for a business you understand with steady cash flows, and 40 to 50 percent or more for something cyclical or opaque. And the margin is always relative to your other choices. If two similar-risk stocks trade at 30 and 50 percent discounts, the 50 wins.

You do not have to buy all at once. Staging in rewards patience and cuts the cost of being early:

| Margin of safety | Action |
|---|---|
| About 20% | Start small, 1 to 2% of the account |
| About 40% | Add meaningfully, 3 to 4% |
| About 60% | Build the full position |

## Let the price tell you what it expects

A normal discounted-cash-flow asks what the stock is worth given your growth assumptions. A reverse DCF flips it: given today's price, what growth does the company have to deliver to justify it? This is the Damodaran and Penman habit of challenging a price with fundamentals.

Penman's finding is useful as a yardstick. Run the exercise on the S&P 500 over long stretches and the implied growth rate usually lands near 4 to 5 percent, close to long-run nominal GDP. At the late-1990s peak it climbed to 8 percent, a quantitative sign that prices had baked in perfection.

| Implied growth | What it means |
|---|---|
| Below long-run GDP (~4 to 5%) | Market is skeptical; a margin of safety may exist |
| Near GDP | Fairly priced; little cushion |
| Twice GDP or more | Priced for extraordinary results; any miss gets punished |

The practical use is a gut check. If a mature industrial that has grown 6 percent for five years needs 15 percent implied growth to justify its price, you are paying for a story. Pass.

## The four valid reasons to sell

Selling is not failure. Holding a fairly priced stock while a cheaper one sits in front of you is the real mistake. *The Art of Value Investing* frames the job as swapping toward bigger discounts. There are four sound triggers.

| Trigger | What changed | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis broken | A key assumption behind the buy is now false | Sell, whatever your cost |
| Better opportunity | A clearly cheaper stock at equal or lower risk appears | Swap, even if the current one is still cheap |
| Overvaluation | Price now exceeds your value estimate, or the reverse DCF implies the implausible | Trim or exit |
| Position too large | A winner has grown to dominate the account | Trim back to a sane weight |

On the first trigger, the practitioner Jed Nussdorf offers a sharp rule: if something cuts his earnings estimate by more than 15 percent, he sells. A 15 percent earnings cut with a 25 percent price drop does not make a stock cheaper in any real sense. It turns a bet on improving fundamentals into a hope for a turnaround. Those are different bets.

## The two traps that cost the most

Behavioral research, summarized in Nofsinger's *The Psychology of Investing*, names the sell-side errors that drain accounts.

The disposition effect is selling winners too fast and holding losers too long. The data: individual investors hold losing stocks about 43 days on average and winners about 20, twice as long on the losers. The cause is loss aversion. A loss hurts about twice as much as an equal gain feels good, and selling a loser turns a paper loss into a real one, so people stall. The damage cuts both ways. Selling winners early cuts off compounding, and holding losers ties up money in a declining business.

The second trap is the refusal to sell until you break even. Your cost basis is irrelevant to the only question that matters: if you held cash today, would you buy this position at this price? If the answer is no, sell. The market neither knows nor cares what you paid.

Professionals are not immune. The same wiring shows up in fund managers, who realize gains faster than losses even when the losers would have done better held.

## Decide before you feel

The one reliable defense is to make the call before emotion is engaged, at the moment you buy. Write down the three or four assumptions that have to hold, the value range you are buying against, and your sell triggers. Then judge the position against that note, not against the ticker or your cost.

| Condition | Rule |
|---|---|
| Price reaches your value estimate | Begin trimming; exit unless a new thesis supports more |
| Estimates fall more than 15% from your original case | Sell, unless one specific one-time event explains it |
| A clearly better opportunity appears | Swap |
| One position grows past 15 to 20% of the account | Trim toward target |
| You can no longer say why you own it | Sell |

One last rule worth burning in: a falling price is not a sell signal on its own. It is a signal to re-check the thesis. If the thesis holds and the discount has widened, a lower price is a reason to add. If the thesis is broken, sell however far it has already fallen. Confusing "price is down" with "thesis is broken" is the most common beginner error there is.
