International holdings widen your opportunity set and they add a kind of risk that does not show up on a balance sheet: politics, war, and trade. Darrin Erickson's *Global Investing* takes a clear position on how to handle it. You cannot forecast these events reliably, so build a portfolio that can absorb any single one, apply a few hard screens before you invest, and watch closely enough to see real escalation coming.

## Two kinds of geopolitical risk

Erickson splits the risk in two, because the response differs.

| Type | Source | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Internal | One country | Rule changes, limits on foreign ownership, tax shifts |
| External | Two or more countries | Sanctions, trade wars, economic coercion, military conflict |

Internal risk changes the rules for a business you already own. External risk can damage a whole region quickly and with little warning, which is why it needs the more active watch.

## Screen one: a sovereign rating floor

The first rule is a strict minimum sovereign credit rating, treated as a hard line rather than a preference. Sovereign ratings from Fitch, Moody's, and S&P summarize a country's debt load, fiscal health, institutions, and ability to pay. Erickson uses investment grade as the floor. Anything below it is out before company analysis even begins.

A sub-investment-grade sovereign usually carries at least one condition that hits equity holders directly: heavy government or consumer debt, high or rising inflation alongside an overheated housing market, a record of inflating debt away (which erodes returns through a falling currency), weak protection of shareholder rights, or financial statements too unreliable to analyze. Erickson points to Reinhart and Rogoff on the link: inflation crises and currency crises have traveled together in the large majority of cases across history. The rating screen catches most of this in advance. Its weakness, which Erickson names, is that agencies are slow to downgrade when a confrontation is escalating fast, so the screen is necessary but not sufficient.

## Screen two: watch the escalation ladder

The second rule is to track where a dispute sits on the path from words to war, not just its current state.

1. Symbolic tariffs on minor goods
2. Broad tariffs
3. Full suspension of trade
4. Sanctions
5. Threat of force
6. Military conflict

Erickson's trigger for action is the point where a military confrontation looks likely to do serious economic damage to a country. There he is explicit: avoid companies based in the countries involved. When a conflict is merely possible and the likely portfolio impact is small, stay the course and keep your attention on business fundamentals. Low-level disputes are always running somewhere, and reacting to all of them just burns returns through turnover.

To judge escalation, Erickson uses a two-part test. How vital does each side consider the issue, and are their desired outcomes incompatible? When both sides treat the matter as essential and their goals cannot be reconciled, the odds of escalation rise. That test points attention at the genuine hotspots while keeping you calm about the rest.

## Screen three: no decisive concentration

The third rule is that no single country or region should be large enough in your portfolio that one shock there decides your whole outcome. This works at the supply-chain level, not just the head-office level. A company based in a safe country but sourcing most of its parts from a hotspot carries the same exposure. Diversifying across countries and regions is not a matter of taste here. It is the main structural defense.

## Real growth versus inflationary growth

Before choosing where to invest, separate two things that look identical in a headline. Real growth comes from producing more. Inflationary growth comes from prices rising while real output does not move. Erickson anchors the gap with a figure: of the roughly 67 trillion dollar rise in world GDP over the two decades he studied, about 44 trillion was just rising prices and only about 23 trillion was real growth. Roughly two-thirds of the headline was inflation.

The rule that follows is simple. A country growing nominal GDP at 8 percent with 7 percent inflation is producing 1 percent in real terms. Adjust for inflation before you call growth genuine, or you will chase a number that creates no lasting value.

## Why construction beats forecasting

Erickson's summary line is direct: setting a minimum sovereign rating and diversifying by country and region is the most effective way to manage geopolitical risk. He does not ask investors to predict conflicts. The reasoning is that conflicts have usually had a limited or short-lived effect on markets, because people adapt and prices tend to recover faster than the fear suggests, so selling everything at the first shot is often the wrong move. The events themselves are low-probability and hard to time. If no one country can decide your result, then being wrong about a specific conflict does not ruin you. The portfolio absorbs the shock.
