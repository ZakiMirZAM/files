Picking the right stock feels like the whole job. The data says it is the smaller part. The bigger lever is how you split your money across asset classes and how widely you spread it. This lesson covers diversification, the stock and bond mix, position sizing, and rebalancing.

## Diversification removes a risk you are not paid to take

Every stock carries two kinds of risk. One is specific to the company: a recall, a fraud, a lawsuit, a failed product. The other is market-wide: recessions, rate shocks, credit crises. Diversification erases the first kind and leaves the second.

The reason is that company-specific shocks point in random directions across many holdings, so they tend to cancel out. Damodaran's *Investment Valuation* walks through the mechanism. Mankiw's *Principles of Economics* gives the rough pace: a single stock has a standard deviation near 49 percent; ten stocks cut about half of that risk; moving from ten to twenty trims another tenth; past thirty the extra benefit is small. Greenwald's *Value Investing from Graham to Buffett and Beyond* sets a practical floor around 30 names spread across industries. The point is blunt: firm-specific risk is uncompensated. The market does not pay you extra for concentrating in one name when you could have removed that risk for free.

## The stock and bond split does most of the work

A 1986 study of pension funds, which Bogle cites often, found that the stock-versus-bond allocation explained 94 percent of the variation in returns across funds. The choice of which specific stocks or bonds to hold explained the other 6 percent.

Graham's old guardrail still holds up: never less than 25 percent and never more than 75 percent in stocks, with the rest in bonds. Stocks and bonds earn from different engines. Stocks pay through earnings growth and dividends. Bonds pay through the coupon yield you buy at. Shifting your split from 60/40 to 75/25 changes your long-run wealth and your worst-case drawdown more than any single stock pick inside those buckets will.

Cost ties into this. Bogle showed that a low-cost 25/75 portfolio can beat a high-cost 75/25 one. The allocation decision and the cost decision are the same decision in practice.

## Do not let one name take over

Even inside your stock sleeve, a single oversized position brings back the firm-specific risk you diversified away. Professional investors quoted in *The Art of Value Investing* converge on a ceiling around 4 to 5 percent per name for most positions. The logic is concrete. When Merck pulled Vioxx in 2004 the stock dropped 25 percent overnight, and no analysis could have timed it. A 5 percent cap turns that shock into roughly 125 basis points of portfolio damage instead of a wound. A total-market index fund handles sizing for you, since it weights each company by size and rebalances on its own. If you pick individual names in this simulator, watch that no single winner quietly grows into a position that could sink the account by itself.

## Rebalancing keeps the risk you chose

Rebalancing means selling what has grown above its target weight and buying what has fallen below, to restore your plan. After a long bull market a 60/40 portfolio can drift to 80/20. That is now a riskier portfolio than you signed up for. Rebalancing pulls it back.

There is a second, quieter benefit. Ilmanen's *Expected Returns* shows that rebalancing lifts long-run compound return by holding down portfolio variance, even when returns follow a random walk. Two ways to do it:

| Approach | How it works |
|---|---|
| Calendar | Rebalance on a fixed schedule, such as once a year |
| Threshold | Rebalance when any holding drifts past a set band, such as 5 points off target |

*Global Investing* adds the rule that makes it work: the trigger has to be mechanical. The moment you wait because a winner "might keep running," you have stopped rebalancing and started guessing.

## Practice this here

Build a small target allocation in the simulator, for example a broad US fund, an international fund, and a bond fund, and write down the weights you want. Let prices move for a while, then rebalance back to those weights and notice what you had to sell and buy. Doing it with virtual money builds the habit of selling strength and buying weakness, which is exactly the habit emotion fights hardest in a real account.
