Before you place a single trade in this simulator, it helps to know what the evidence says about the simplest strategy of all: own the whole market at the lowest possible cost and hold it. John Bogle spent a career making this case, and most of it comes down to arithmetic.

## The math that does not change

All investors together own the entire market. As a group they earn the market's return before costs, then split it after costs. That is an accounting identity, not a theory. A broad index fund holds every stock in proportion to its size and charges almost nothing, so it keeps nearly the whole gross return. The average actively managed fund earns that same gross return and then hands back a much larger slice in fees and trading costs. So the average active dollar has to trail the index by the size of its costs. Burton Malkiel put it plainly in Bogle's *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*: active managers as a whole cannot beat the market as a whole, so on average they must trail it by their expenses.

The record matches the math. Over the twenty years that *Rethinking Investing* surveyed, about 89 percent of US mutual funds trailed the S&P 500. Over ten years it was 90 percent. The few that won tended not to keep winning.

## Why a one percent fee is not small

A fee sounds trivial as a yearly number. Run it across a lifetime and it stops being trivial. Bogle's own example: start with 10,000 dollars, assume stocks return 7 percent a year, and compare a near-free index fund with a fund that charges 2 percent a year.

| Years held | Index at 7% | Active fund at 5% (after 2% costs) | Share lost to costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | $19,700 | $16,300 | ~17% |
| 30 | $76,100 | $43,200 | ~43% |
| 50 | $294,900 | $114,700 | ~61% |

After fifty years the high-cost investor ends with roughly 114,700 dollars against 294,900. The investor put up all the money and took all the risk, and kept less than 40 percent of the reward. Bogle's line is worth remembering while you trade here: where returns are concerned time is your friend, but where costs are concerned time is your enemy.

## Past winners rarely stay winners

The natural move when picking a fund is to buy whatever just did well. Bogle's data on reversion to the mean is a warning against exactly that. When he sorted funds into performance groups, the top group in one period scattered almost randomly across all groups in the next. The CGM Focus Fund returned 917 percent from 1998 to 2007 while the market did 78 percent, then collapsed as money piled in at the top. Stars, in his words, become meteors.

Reversion to the mean is the tendency of unusually high or low returns to drift back toward the average over time. It is why a hot three-year record is a weak reason to buy.

## What this means for your practice account

You have virtual money here precisely so you can test ideas that you would not risk in real life. Use it. But treat the index approach as the baseline that any active bet has to beat. A reasonable first move is to put a core of the account into a broad market fund such as VOO or VTI, then let any individual stock or sector bet prove itself against that core. If your stock picks cannot beat a fund you could have bought for almost nothing, the simulator has taught you something real before it cost you anything.
