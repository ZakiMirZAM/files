There are two very different ideas that often get mixed together: tilting a portfolio toward stronger sectors, and using a leveraged fund like TECL to express a sector view. The first can be reasonable. The second carries costs that make a long hold almost always a losing bet. Keep them separate.

## Tilting toward a sector, done well

A tilt is a deliberate overweight relative to the market's own weighting. A total-market fund already puts roughly 30 percent in technology, around 13 percent in financials, and about 3 percent in utilities. Going beyond those weights means accepting more tracking error in exchange for exposure you believe pays off over time.

A defensible tilt rests on structure, not momentum. *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* is careful here: when factor or "smart beta" tilts earn extra return, it is usually pay for extra risk, not free money. A sound technology overweight rests on durable economics, high returns on invested capital, real pricing power, and a sensible entry price. An overweight bought because the sector rose 40 percent last year rests on nothing.

| Way to tilt | What it does | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Factor fund | Overweights high-profitability firms | A permanent, low-turnover tilt |
| Sector ETF overweight | Adds 5 to 10 points beyond the market weight | A multi-year structural view |
| Individual stocks | Buys undervalued names in the sector | Needs real research; brings single-name risk |

Keep tilts modest and rule-based. A 5 to 10 point overweight is a tilt. A 30 to 50 percent bet on one sector is speculation wearing a tilt's clothing.

## The performance-chasing trap

Sector rotation, moving into whatever sector should lead the next phase of the cycle, looks great on paper. *Investment Analysis* documents the cycle map and then delivers the verdict: rotation can be very profitable and very risky, and to win you have to be right far more often than wrong, which almost nobody does consistently. The edge you would need is already priced in by participants who are faster and cheaper than you.

The behavioral version is worse. Morningstar's investor-return studies keep finding that the average fund investor earns less than the average fund, because money arrives after the good years and leaves after the bad ones. Buying technology after a decade of technology winning is buying a track record, not an opportunity. Bogle's reminder fits here: today's winning factors are all too likely to be tomorrow's losing ones.

## How a 3x daily fund actually behaves

A fund like TECL does not promise 3x the technology sector over a month or a year. It promises about 3x the sector's return for a single day, and it resets every day to keep that exposure. That daily reset is where the damage comes from.

Watch what two days do. The index drops 1 percent over the two days. The 3x fund drops 9 percent.

| Day | Index move | Index | 3x fund move | 3x fund |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | | 100.00 | | 100.00 |
| 1 | +10% | 110.00 | +30% | 130.00 |
| 2 | -10% | 99.00 | -30% | 91.00 |

This is volatility decay, also called beta slippage. The math behind it is short:

`Compound return = Average return − (Variance ÷ 2)`

Tripling the daily move triples the return but multiplies the variance by nine, because variance scales with the square of the multiplier. So the drag on compounding is about nine times the index's drag, not three. For a technology index near 25 percent annual volatility, the unleveraged fund loses roughly 3 percent a year to this drag while the 3x fund loses something closer to 28 percent, before any fee.

Decay is path-dependent, not direction-dependent. In a smooth uptrend a 3x fund can deliver close to 3x the gain, which is what TECL did in 2023. In a choppy decline it does far worse than 3x the loss. In 2022 the technology sector fell about 33 percent and TECL fell more than 75 percent. A 33 percent loss needs a 50 percent gain to recover. A 75 percent loss needs a 300 percent gain.

## The cost stack

Leverage adds costs an ordinary sector fund does not carry.

| Cost | Plain sector ETF | 3x daily fund |
|---|---|---|
| Expense ratio | 0.10 to 0.20% | 0.75 to 1.00% |
| Financing on the borrowed exposure | None | 1 to 3%+ a year, higher when rates are high |
| Turnover and tax | Low | High, from daily resets |
| Rough total annual drag | 0.10 to 0.30% | 2 to 5%+ |

When short-term rates are high, the borrowing cost embedded in the fund's swaps climbs. In a 7 to 8 percent sector, a 3 to 5 percent cost stack eats a large part of the gross return before decay even enters the picture.

## The disciplined stance

For most long-term investors the answer on 3x daily funds is to leave them alone. They are built, regulated, and disclosed as single-day trading tools, not as holdings for a retirement account. If you do experiment with one in this simulator, treat it as a short-horizon trade measured in days, size it so a 70 to 80 percent loss would not derail the plan (for most people that means a few percent of the account at most), and decide your exit before you enter. The simulator is the right place to watch decay chew through a leveraged position over a few weeks, so you learn the lesson with play money.
