# Justified P/B

## Part 1: Definition

Justified price-to-book measures the price-to-book ratio a company's fundamentals warrant. Price-to-book compares a company's market value to its book value, the owners' stake recorded on the balance sheet. The justified version asks what that ratio should be, given how much the company earns on its equity, how fast it grows, and how much risk its shareholders bear. A company that earns far more than its shareholders require deserves to trade well above book value. A company that earns less deserves to trade below it. The comparative view shows this warranted ratio side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can set each company's fair multiple against the price the market actually charges.

The side-by-side view carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing, so their fundamentals can be judged on the same terms. Justified price-to-book turns three drivers into one number that the market multiple can be tested against. When a company's actual price-to-book sits above its justified figure, the market may be paying more than the fundamentals support. When the actual sits below the justified figure, the market may be overlooking a business that earns its keep.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Justified P/B above actual P/B | The fundamentals support a higher multiple than the market charges. A possible bargain. |
| Justified P/B near actual P/B | The market price and the fundamentals roughly agree on what the equity is worth. |
| Justified P/B below actual P/B | The fundamentals support a lower multiple than the market charges. The price may have run ahead. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g)`

ROE is return on equity, the profit earned for each dollar of equity. The letter g is the company's expected growth rate, and Ke is the cost of equity, the return shareholders require. The numerator is the return on equity above the growth rate, and the denominator is the cost of equity above the growth rate. Dividing the first by the second gives the price-to-book the fundamentals warrant. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies against their actual price-to-book.

The formula rests on a simple idea. When return on equity exactly equals the cost of equity, the justified ratio is one, so the company should trade at book value, because it earns exactly what its owners require. When return on equity exceeds the cost of equity, the ratio rises above one, and the company deserves a premium to book. When return on equity falls short, the ratio drops below one, and the company deserves a discount. Growth amplifies the result, lifting the warranted multiple for a company that earns above its hurdle and deepening the discount for one that earns below it. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Justified price-to-book gives the reader a fair multiple to set against the market multiple, built from the drivers that should determine value. The actual price-to-book on its own says only what the market charges, not whether that charge is right. The justified figure supplies the missing benchmark, because it states what the company's own return on equity, growth, and risk imply the multiple should be. Comparing the two across an industry shows which companies look cheap or dear once their fundamentals are taken into account.

The justified figure matters because it ties the price-to-book directly to the spread between return on equity and the cost of equity, which is the core of value creation for shareholders. A company can trade at a low price-to-book and still be expensive if it earns little on its equity, and at a high price-to-book and still be cheap if it earns far above its hurdle. The justified multiple captures that, so it turns a raw price tag into a value judgment that can be ranked across the industry.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces drive the gap between two companies. The first is return on equity, since a higher return above the hurdle lifts the warranted multiple. The second is the cost of equity, since a higher hurdle lowers it, because riskier equity deserves a smaller premium to book. The third is growth, which magnifies the spread in either direction, raising the justified multiple for a company that earns above its hurdle and lowering it for one that earns below.

A difference can also come from the inputs rather than the business. The cost of equity rests on a beta and a risk premium that can be measured several ways, so a different hurdle changes the justified figure. The growth rate is an estimate, and a high assumed growth lifts the warranted multiple sharply when return on equity already beats the hurdle. Return on equity itself can be flattered by buybacks or write-downs that shrink equity, which inflates the justified multiple without any real improvement in the business.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare the justified price-to-book to each company's own actual price-to-book, not the justified figures against each other. A higher justified multiple does not mean a better deal, because it only signals fair value when set against the market price. The useful comparison is the gap between justified and actual for each company, which shows where the market is paying less or more than the fundamentals warrant. Within one industry the formula is applied the same way, so these gaps can be ranked fairly across the companies.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Justified far above actual | The fundamentals support a higher multiple than the market charges. Check that the return spread is durable. |
| Justified close to actual | The market and the fundamentals agree. No clear edge from this metric. |
| Justified below actual | The market charges more than the fundamentals warrant. The price assumes improvement that has to come. |

Always check whether the return spread that lifts a high justified multiple is durable. A wide spread that competition is likely to erode will not support the warranted multiple for long, so a large gap to actual may close from the justified side rather than the price.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's justified price-to-book over several years, so the reader can watch it move alongside the actual multiple. A company whose justified figure rises is widening the spread between its return on equity and its cost of equity, which is the engine that deserves a premium to book. A company whose justified figure drifts toward one is losing that spread, which means its fundamentals support less of a premium each year. Watching the justified figure against the actual shows whether a discount is opening or closing.

The most useful pattern is a justified multiple that climbs steadily while the actual multiple lags, because that gap is a discount built on improving fundamentals. A company whose justified figure keeps sliding toward or below one is being squeezed, and a high actual multiple may not hold. Comparing how each company's justified and actual multiples have trended shows which valuations rest on a durable return spread and which on a fading one.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Actual P/B

**Why read the two together.** Justified price-to-book states the multiple the fundamentals warrant, and actual price-to-book states the multiple the market charges. The justified figure is meaningless without the actual one to test it against, because the whole point is the gap between them. Reading them together turns a fair-value estimate into a verdict on whether the market price is too high or too low.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose justified multiple sits well above its actual multiple may be undervalued, because the market charges less than the fundamentals support. A company whose justified multiple sits below its actual multiple may be overvalued, because the price assumes more than the business delivers. Comparing the gaps across the industry shows which companies the fundamentals call cheap and which they call dear.

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is the main driver of the justified multiple, because a higher return above the hurdle warrants a higher price-to-book. The return on equity column shows the engine, and the justified figure shows the multiple that engine supports. Reading them together shows whether a high warranted multiple rests on a genuinely strong return on equity.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high return on equity and a high justified multiple has a valuation grounded in real profitability. A company with a high justified multiple but only an average return on equity owes its warranted premium to a generous growth assumption or a low hurdle, which is more fragile. The company whose justified multiple rests on durable return on equity has the most reliable fair value.

### Cost Of Equity

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity sits in the denominator of the justified multiple, so a higher hurdle lowers the price-to-book the fundamentals warrant. The cost of equity column shows the risk shareholders bear, and the justified figure shows how that risk feeds into fair value. Reading them together shows how much of a high warranted multiple comes from a low hurdle rather than a strong return.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a low cost of equity can justify a higher price-to-book, because its profits are discounted at a gentler rate. A company with a high cost of equity must earn a far higher return on equity to justify the same multiple. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies deserve a richer warranted multiple because their shareholders accept less risk.

## Part 8: Limitations

Justified price-to-book is a model figure, and it depends on a return on equity, a growth rate, and a cost of equity that are all estimates. A change in the growth assumption alone can swing the warranted multiple sharply, especially when return on equity already beats the hurdle, because growth magnifies the spread. The formula also assumes stable growth, so it fits a steady company far better than one whose returns or growth are in flux. When the growth rate approaches the cost of equity, the denominator shrinks and the figure becomes unstable.

The justified multiple inherits the flaws of its inputs. Return on equity can be flattered by buybacks or write-downs that shrink equity, and it breaks down entirely when equity is negative. The cost of equity rests on a contested beta and premium. The figure also reflects current fundamentals, not future ones, and a wide return spread today may narrow as competition arrives. A large gap between justified and actual is a reason to study the durability of the return spread, not a promise that the price will move to meet the warranted multiple.
