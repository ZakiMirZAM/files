# DuPont Tax Burden

## Part 1: Definition

DuPont tax burden measures how much of a company's pre-tax profit it keeps after tax. Pre-tax income is the profit a company earns before income tax is taken out. Net income is what remains after tax. The tax burden is net income divided by pre-tax income, so it is the share of each pre-tax dollar that survives the tax bill. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company hands over the least to tax. A company with a tax burden of 0.80 keeps 80 cents of every pre-tax dollar. A direct competitor at 0.65 keeps only 65 cents.

The tax burden is one of the five levers in the DuPont breakdown of return on equity. Return on equity is yearly profit per dollar of owner money, and the DuPont method splits it into five parts that multiply together: tax burden, interest burden, operating profitability, how hard the assets work, and how much debt the company uses. The tax burden is the tax-retention lever. It captures the last step in turning operating profit into the profit owners keep. A company that pays less tax keeps more of what it earns, which lifts its return on equity through this lever alone.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Highest in the industry | Keeps the most after tax, from a low rate, tax credits, or favorable geography. Check if it lasts. |
| Middle of the pack | A typical effective tax rate for the industry. No special advantage or burden. |
| Lowest in the industry | Pays the most to tax, from a higher rate or a one-time tax charge in the year. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Tax Burden = Net Income ÷ Pre-tax Income`

Net income is the profit left after every cost, including tax. Pre-tax income is the profit before income tax. The result is a figure between zero and one, and a company keeping more after tax sits closer to one. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company faces the same tax backdrop at that time.

This lever is the final factor in the five-part DuPont chain. The chain multiplies the tax burden by the interest burden, the operating margin, the asset turnover, and the financial leverage to rebuild the full return on equity. The tax burden is where pre-tax profit becomes the after-tax profit that belongs to owners. A higher tax burden lifts return on equity, but only because the company surrenders less to tax, not because the underlying business is stronger.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

DuPont tax burden isolates the part of return on equity that comes from tax rather than from running the business. Two companies in one industry can post the same return on equity while one earns it through a strong operation and the other through a low tax bill. Splitting out the tax burden shows which is which. It answers a precise question. How much of each company's profit advantage is simply paying less tax.

The lever matters because a tax advantage is often less durable and less repeatable than an operating one. A low rate can come from a temporary credit, a favorable jurisdiction, or a one-time item, any of which can reverse. A company whose high return on equity leans heavily on a light tax burden is more exposed than a peer that earns the same return from operations. Comparing this lever across an industry tells the reader whose profitability rests on the business and whose rests partly on the tax code.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the statutory rate where a company earns its profit, since a firm based in or selling into a low-tax country keeps more. The second is tax credits and incentives, such as those for research or investment, which lower the effective rate. The third is the mix of profit across countries, because a company that books more profit in low-tax places keeps a larger share overall.

A difference can also be a one-time effect rather than a lasting one. A one-off tax charge can push a company's tax burden down for a single year, making its return on equity look weaker than the business deserves. A one-time tax benefit does the reverse. A company carrying past losses can pay little tax for a while, lifting its tax burden temporarily until those losses are used up. These swings make a single year's lever an unreliable guide on its own.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Tax burdens vary by sector and geography, so a number that is normal for a domestic utility can differ from one for a global manufacturer. Within one industry the companies often share similar tax exposure, which makes the lever more comparable, and a real outlier stands out as a genuine advantage or burden.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A real tax advantage, or a one-time benefit. Check whether the low rate is structural or temporary. |
| Cluster of similar burdens | The companies face similar tax conditions, so this lever explains little of the gap in return on equity. |
| One company far below the rest | A higher rate or a one-time tax charge. Confirm before treating it as a permanent weakness. |

Always check whether a high tax burden is structural before crediting it to a company's return on equity. A low rate that rests on a credit due to expire, or on a single good year, will not support the return for long.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's tax burden over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that keeps a high tax burden year after year usually enjoys a real and lasting tax position. A burden that jumps around from year to year is a sign of one-time items rather than a steady advantage, and it should be smoothed in the reader's mind before crediting it to the return on equity.

The most useful pattern is a lever that drifts steadily in one direction. A tax burden rising over time can mean expiring credits or a shift toward higher-tax markets, which will drag on return on equity. A burden falling over time can mean a growing tax advantage. Because tax rules change for whole industries at once, the reader should separate an industry-wide shift that moves everyone from a change that affects one company alone.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** DuPont tax burden is one of the five levers that multiply together to form return on equity, so it explains part of why one company's return outranks another's. Return on equity is the headline result, and the tax burden shows how much of that result comes from keeping profit out of the tax bill rather than from the business itself. Putting the two side by side shows whether a company's return advantage is operational or merely fiscal.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high return on equity and an ordinary tax burden earns its lead from the business, which is the more durable result. A company whose return on equity ranks high mainly because its tax burden is high is leaning on the tax code, which can reverse. The reader can use this pairing to find the company whose strong return survives even after the tax advantage is stripped out.

### DuPont Interest Burden

**Why read the two together.** Tax burden and interest burden are the two financial levers in the DuPont chain, sitting next to each other between operating profit and net income. The interest burden captures what debt costs take from operating profit, and the tax burden captures what tax takes from what remains. Reading them together shows how much of the distance between a company's operating profit and its bottom line is financing and how much is tax.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high score on both levers loses little of its operating profit to interest or tax, so its return on equity rests mostly on the business. A company that scores well on tax burden but poorly on interest burden is keeping its tax bill low while heavy debt eats its profit. Comparing the two levers across the industry tells the reader where each company's profit leaks away before it reaches owners.

### DuPont EBIT Margin

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin is the operating lever in the DuPont chain, and tax burden is the lever that acts last on what operations produce. The margin shows how profitable the core business is, and the tax burden shows how much of that profit the company keeps after tax. Putting the two together separates a real operating advantage from a tax-driven one.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a strong EBIT margin and an ordinary tax burden is a genuinely profitable operator. A company with a weak EBIT margin but a high tax burden is propping up a mediocre business with a light tax bill, which is a fragile basis for a good return on equity. The reader can use this pairing to find the company whose return is built on operating strength rather than on tax.

## Part 8: Limitations

DuPont tax burden can rank a company high for a reason that will not last, because a low tax bill from a one-time benefit or an expiring credit flatters the lever for a single year. A high tax burden in one year can also reflect a one-time tax charge rather than a genuine disadvantage. Reading the lever from a single snapshot can therefore mislead, and it should be judged across several years before it is trusted.

The lever says nothing about the quality of the underlying business. A company can hold a high tax burden while running a weak operation, so a top rank on this single lever is not a verdict on the company. The tax burden also depends on accounting and tax-reporting choices that differ across firms, which can blur the comparison. The comparative view shows which company keeps the most after tax, not which is the best business or the cheapest stock, so a high rank invites research rather than ending it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company keeps the most of its pre-tax profit after tax, and is the low effective rate structural or a one-time benefit?
2. How much of the leading company's return on equity comes from this tax lever rather than from operating strength?
3. Has the company's tax burden stayed steady over several years, or does it swing with one-time tax items?
4. Does any company show a high tax burden only because of past losses that shelter it from tax, and how long will that shelter last?
5. Is a low tax burden the result of a single year's tax charge, or a genuine and lasting disadvantage?
6. Are the companies clustered on this lever, which would mean tax explains little of the gap in return on equity, or spread apart, which would mean tax is a real differentiator?
7. Which company would keep the steadiest return if a tax credit it relies on were to expire?
8. Does the company with the best tax burden also rank well on the operating levers, or is its return propped up mainly by a light tax bill?
