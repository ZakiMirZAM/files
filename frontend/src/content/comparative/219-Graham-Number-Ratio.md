# Graham Number Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The Graham number ratio measures how a company's share price compares with a conservative fair-value floor built from its earnings and its book value. The Graham number is that floor, named after Benjamin Graham, an early value investor who set strict limits on what a careful buyer should pay. It combines two anchors: earnings per share, the yearly profit for each share, and book value per share, the owners' accounting stake for each share. The ratio divides the share price by the Graham number, so a figure below 1.0 means the price sits under the floor and a figure above 1.0 means it sits above. The comparative view places this ratio side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which companies trade below their conservative floor and which trade well above it. A company at 0.8 sits below the floor. A company at 2.0 trades at twice it.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies share the same kind of earnings and assets. When firms run similar businesses, the gap between price and this floor can be compared directly. A ratio below 1.0 marks a company that is cheap by Graham's strict standard, while a high ratio marks one the market prices far above its conservative value.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 1.0 | Trades under the conservative floor, a candidate for value by Graham's strict standard. |
| Near 1.0 | Priced close to the floor. Neither clearly cheap nor clearly stretched on this measure. |
| Well above 1.0 | Priced far above the floor, usually a company the market values for growth or quality. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Graham Number Ratio = Share Price ÷ √(22.5 × EPS × Book Value Per Share)`

The Graham number is the square root of 22.5 multiplied by earnings per share and by book value per share. The 22.5 comes from Graham's rule that a defensive buyer should not pay more than about 15 times earnings and about 1.5 times book value, and 15 multiplied by 1.5 equals 22.5. Earnings per share is yearly profit divided by share count, and book value per share is the owners' stake divided by share count. The ratio then divides the actual share price by that floor. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from lowest to highest. Ranking at one shared moment keeps the comparison fair, because every company is measured against the same recent figures.

The number only works when both earnings and book value are positive, since a square root needs a positive input. A company with a loss or with negative book value cannot produce a meaningful Graham number, so the ratio is blank or misleading for it. This makes the measure most useful for steady, asset-backed, profitable companies of the kind Graham favored.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

The Graham number ratio gives the reader one strict, conservative yardstick for comparing prices across an industry. Rather than asking which company is most exciting, it asks which company trades cheaply against a floor built from hard accounting figures. Earnings and book value are concrete, so the floor is harder to argue with than a forecast. Comparing the ratio across the industry shows which companies the market prices below their conservative value and which it prices far above.

A company trading below 1.0 while its peers trade above is cheap by a demanding standard, and that gap is worth investigating. The measure leans toward companies with real assets and steady profits, which is exactly the kind of business that suits this floor. A wide spread between the cheapest and dearest company in the same industry is a signal worth examining, because the market is pricing similar businesses very differently against the same conservative anchor. The ratio is a screen for value, not a full verdict, but it points to where caution-minded buyers should look first.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the market's view of growth, since a company expected to grow faster trades further above its floor. The second is the quality of the business, because the market pays more above book value for a company with strong, durable returns. The third is how asset-heavy the company is, as a company with large book value per share has a higher floor and so a lower ratio at the same price.

A difference can also come from the inputs rather than the price. A company with a one-time gain shows inflated earnings per share, which raises its floor and pushes its ratio down for a year that will not repeat. A company that has taken a write-down shows reduced book value, which lowers its floor and lifts its ratio. A company that holds few physical assets, common in service or technology businesses, has a low book value and so almost always shows a high ratio, even when it is a fine business.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. The Graham number suits asset-heavy, steady businesses far better than light, fast-growing ones, so a ratio that flags value in one industry is normal in another. Comparing companies that share the same asset intensity and earnings pattern keeps the floor meaningful. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies sit on the same kind of balance sheet.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company below 1.0, peers above | A standout value candidate by Graham's standard, worth a close look at why it is cheap. |
| Cluster of ratios above 1.0 | A market that values the whole industry above its conservative floor, common for growth-minded sectors. |
| One company far above the rest | A company priced for strong growth or quality. The floor explains little of its price. |

Always check whether a low ratio comes from inflated earnings or a high book value rather than a low price. A company that looks cheap only because a one-time gain lifted its floor is not the bargain it appears to be.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's Graham number ratio over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that has slipped below 1.0 after years above it may have become a genuine bargain or may be falling for a real reason. A company whose ratio keeps climbing is winning the market's confidence, usually because its earnings or its perceived quality are rising.

The most useful pattern is how a company's ratio moves against its own past and against the industry. A company trading below its usual ratio and below its peers is worth examining, since the market may be too gloomy about it. A company whose ratio has drifted far above the floor for years is one the market no longer values on conservative grounds at all. Comparing the paths shows which companies have fallen toward value and which have left the floor far behind.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Actual P/B

**Why read the two together.** The Graham number ratio blends earnings and book value into one floor, while actual price to book compares price against book value alone. Book value is half of what builds the Graham floor, so reading the two together shows how much of a company's standing on this measure comes from its assets. A company cheap on both is cheap against its book and against the blended floor.

**What the combination tells you.** When a company sits below 1.0 on the Graham ratio and low on price to book, its cheapness is grounded firmly in its assets, the most conservative kind of value. When the Graham ratio is low but price to book is high, the low ratio is being driven by strong earnings rather than cheap assets. Comparing the two across the industry shows which apparent bargains are backed by real, low-priced assets and which lean on an earnings figure that could change.

### Price To Earnings

**Why read the two together.** Price to earnings prices a company against its profit alone, while the Graham number ratio blends profit with book value into a conservative floor. Earnings are the other half of the Graham floor, so reading the two together shows how much of a company's value rests on its profit. A company cheap on both is cheap on earnings and on the blended floor.

**What the combination tells you.** When a company is cheap on both measures, its low price is backed by both profit and assets, the most convincing kind of value. When the Graham ratio is low but price to earnings is high, the floor is being held up by a large book value rather than by cheap earnings. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies are cheap on the strength of their profits and which only look cheap because their assets are heavy.

## Part 8: Limitations

The Graham number ratio cannot be used for companies with a loss or negative book value, because the floor needs both figures to be positive. It systematically marks asset-light businesses, such as many service and technology companies, as expensive, since they hold little book value and so carry a high floor by design. A one-time gain or a write-down can swing a company's earnings or book value for a single year and distort the floor in a way the price did not cause. These traps make the measure unreliable outside the steady, asset-backed companies it was built for.

The floor is a rule of thumb from a cautious era, not a true measure of worth. A company trading below it can keep falling, and a company well above it can be a fine investment. The ratio shows whether the market prices a company below a strict conservative standard, not whether the company is a good business or a safe one. The comparative view shows which company is cheapest against the Graham floor, not which is the best to own, so a low ranking is a starting point for research rather than a verdict.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which companies trade below 1.0, and which of them look genuinely cheap rather than cheap because of a distorted earnings or book figure?
2. For a company below the floor, is the cheapness driven by low-priced assets or by an earnings figure that could change?
3. Does any company's low ratio come from a one-time gain that inflated its floor for a year that will not repeat?
4. Which companies cannot be measured here because they have a loss or negative book value, and how should they be judged instead?
5. Is the whole industry asset-light, making high ratios normal and a low ratio especially notable?
6. Are the companies clustered together against the floor, or is one priced far below the rest for reasons worth investigating?
7. How does each company's current ratio compare with its own multi-year history, and which has fallen furthest toward value?
8. Among the companies below the floor, which also looks cheap on both price to earnings and price to book, marking the most grounded value in the group?
