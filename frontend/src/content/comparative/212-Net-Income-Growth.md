# Net Income Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Net income growth measures how fast a company's yearly profit is rising or falling. Net income is the profit left after every cost, including interest and tax. Growth is the change in that profit from one year to the next, shown as a percentage. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company is expanding its profit fastest and which is stalling or shrinking. A company with net income growth of 20 percent earned 20 percent more profit this year than last. A direct competitor at minus 5 percent earned 5 percent less.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry face the same customers, the same demand, and the same costs, so the company growing profit fastest is usually winning share, cutting costs better, or pricing more powerfully than the rest. A higher growth rate points to a company pulling ahead. A lower or negative one points to a company falling behind or hitting a rough patch.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The fastest-growing profit, from rising share, better costs, or stronger pricing, unless a weak prior year flatters it. |
| Middle of the pack | Profit moving in line with the industry. The company is keeping pace, not pulling ahead. |
| Bottom of the industry | Stalling or shrinking profit, from lost share, rising costs, or a one-time hit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Net Income Growth = (This Year's Net Income − Last Year's Net Income) ÷ Last Year's Net Income`

This year's net income and last year's net income both come from the income statement. The difference, divided by last year's figure, gives the growth rate. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company's latest year is measured the same way.

The growth rate depends heavily on the starting point. A company recovering from a weak prior year can show a huge growth rate that overstates real progress, because the base it grew from was unusually low. A company growing from a strong prior year shows a more modest rate even when the dollar gain is large. The starting figure shapes the percentage, so the base year matters as much as the latest one.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Net income growth is the clearest read on which company in an industry is gaining ground. A company that grows profit faster than its peers is usually doing something the others are not, such as winning customers, controlling costs, or raising prices the market will bear. Ranking companies on profit growth shows which businesses are climbing and which are treading water inside the same competitive arena.

The metric is most useful inside one industry, where every company faces the same demand and the same pressures. A company that grows profit faster than the rest year after year is steadily strengthening its position. A wide gap between the fastest and slowest grower in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the leader is capturing gains the others cannot. The ranking points to which company is winning the competition for profit.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is sales growth. A company selling more, whether by winning share or raising prices, usually grows profit faster. The second is cost control. A company holding costs steady while sales rise lets more of each new sales dollar reach profit. The third is operating leverage, the way a company with high fixed costs sees profit swing sharply when sales move, up or down.

A difference can also be a base effect or a one-time event rather than a real trend. A company recovering from a loss or a weak year shows a large growth rate built on a low starting point, which exaggerates the gain. A one-time charge in the prior year makes this year's growth look strong, and a one-time gain in the prior year makes it look weak. A company can post high growth one year and negative growth the next simply because of these swings, so a single year's rank can mislead.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Normal growth rates differ across industries, so 10 percent can be fast in a mature industry and slow in a young one. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because every company rides the same demand and the same conditions, and the same growth rate counts as strong or weak for all of them.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A likely share gainer or cost cutter, unless a weak prior year inflates the rate. Check the base year. |
| Cluster of similar numbers | An industry where companies are growing together, often riding the same demand rather than competing for it. |
| One company far below the rest | A company losing ground, or one whose prior year held a one-time gain that makes this year look weak. |

Always check the base year of a fast grower before crediting it as the winner. A high growth rate built on a depressed prior year is a rebound, not a sustained advantage, so the dollar level of profit matters alongside the percentage.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's net income growth over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that grows profit faster than its peers for many years in a row has a durable advantage, because consistent growth is hard to fake. A single strong year proves far less, because one year can come from a rebound, a one-time gain, or a temporary boom.

The most useful pattern is steady, repeated growth rather than one spectacular year. A company that posts solid growth every year is more convincing than one that swings between large gains and losses. A company climbing the ranking steadily is often gaining real share, while a company that tops the list once and falls back was likely riding a one-off. The history separates lasting winners from lucky years.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Revenue

**Why read the two together.** Net income growth shows how fast profit is rising, and revenue shows whether sales are rising to support it. Profit growth that comes from rising sales is healthier than profit growth squeezed from cost cuts alone. Reading the two together shows whether each company's profit growth rests on a growing business or on one-time savings. The pairing reveals the source of the growth, not just its size.

**What the combination tells you.** A company growing both profit and revenue is expanding in the healthiest way, because more sales are driving the gains. A company growing profit while revenue is flat is leaning on cost cuts, which can run out. The reader can use the pairing to separate companies winning new business from companies merely trimming expenses, and to favor the former for lasting growth.

### Net Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Net income ratio is the profit margin, the share of each sales dollar kept as profit, and net income growth shows how fast that profit is rising. Reading them together shows whether a fast-growing company is also a profitable one. Growth at a healthy margin is worth far more than growth at a thin one. The pairing checks the quality of the profit behind the growth.

**What the combination tells you.** A company growing profit quickly while holding a strong margin is the most attractive combination, because each new sale is both growing and profitable. A company growing profit fast but at a shrinking margin is buying growth by sacrificing profitability, which is fragile. The reader can use the pairing to favor companies whose growth comes without giving away the margin that makes profit worth having.

### Price To Earnings

**Why read the two together.** Net income growth shows how fast a company's profit is rising, and price to earnings shows what the market is paying for that profit. A high price is only justified when growth backs it up. Reading the two together shows whether each company's price tag matches its actual growth. The pairing turns a growth ranking into a value judgment.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with fast growth and a moderate multiple is the most appealing, because the reader buys real growth without paying a premium for it. A company with slow growth and a high multiple is priced for a story the numbers do not support. The best find is often the fastest grower whose multiple has not yet caught up to its progress.

## Part 8: Limitations

Net income growth can rank a rebound above a steady performer, because a company recovering from a weak year shows a huge percentage built on a low base. The comparison also misleads when prior-year one-time gains or charges distort the starting point, making this year's growth look strong or weak for reasons that will not repeat. A single year of growth says little about whether the gain can last.

The number measures the change in profit, not the level. A company can grow profit fast and still be the smallest, least profitable business in the industry, so a top growth ranking is not a verdict on quality. The comparative view shows which company's profit is rising fastest this year, not which company is the best business or the cheapest to buy, so a high ranking is a starting point for research rather than a conclusion.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company is growing profit fastest in the industry, and is the growth built on rising sales, better costs, or a rebound from a weak year?
2. Does the fastest grower also show rising revenue, or is its profit growth coming from cost cuts that may run out?
3. Is any company's high growth rate inflated by a depressed prior year or a prior-year one-time charge?
4. Has the leader grown profit faster than its peers for several years, or did it top the ranking only once?
5. Which company is climbing the ranking steadily, and what in the business explains the gain?
6. Are the companies growing together, which would suggest shared demand, or spread apart, which would suggest one company is taking share from the others?
7. Does the fastest grower also hold a healthy margin, or is it buying growth by sacrificing profitability?
8. Does the fastest grower trade at a multiple that already prices in the growth, or has the market not yet caught up?
