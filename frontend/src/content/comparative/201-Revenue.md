# Revenue

## Part 1: Definition

Revenue is the total money a company collects from selling its products or services in a year, before any cost is taken out. Revenue is also called sales or the top line, because it sits at the top of the income statement. The comparative view shows revenue side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company sells the most and which sells the least. A company with revenue of 50 billion dollars sold five times as much as a competitor with revenue of 10 billion dollars.

The side-by-side comparison shows the size of each company and the share of the industry each one controls. The largest seller in an industry is often the one with the most customers, the widest distribution, or the strongest brand. Revenue alone does not say whether a company is profitable, because a company can sell a great deal and still lose money on every sale.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The largest seller, often the market leader with scale and reach. |
| Middle of the pack | A solid competitor with meaningful but not dominant share. |
| Bottom of the industry | A smaller player, a niche specialist, or a company still growing toward scale. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Revenue = Units Sold × Price Per Unit`

Revenue is the sum of every sale a company made during the year, recorded when the product or service was delivered. The comparative table takes each company's revenue at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared year is what makes the comparison fair, because it removes the question of which company simply reported over a longer or more recent period.

Two pieces drive the number. The first is how many units the company sold. The second is the price charged for each unit. A company can grow revenue by selling more units, by raising prices, or by both at once. Reading revenue next to the profit margins shows whether the sales were won at a healthy price or by discounting.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Revenue is the simplest measure of how big each company is within its industry. Size matters because a larger seller usually buys inputs more cheaply, spreads fixed costs over more units, and carries more weight with suppliers and customers. The biggest seller often sets the prices the rest of the industry must follow. Knowing who leads on revenue is the first step in mapping the competitive order of an industry.

Revenue also reveals market share, the slice of total industry sales each company holds. A company that holds a large and steady share is harder to dislodge than a small one. Comparing revenue across the group shows whether the industry is dominated by one or two giants or split among many similar rivals.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

The plainest reason one company sells more than another is that it serves more customers or charges higher prices. A company with a wider product range, more stores, or a stronger brand will usually report larger revenue. A company that has been in the market longer has often built a bigger base of repeat buyers.

A difference can also come from how the companies are built rather than how well they sell. One company may make and sell the whole product itself, while another outsources part of the work, which lowers the revenue it records. A recent acquisition can lift revenue overnight without any gain in underlying strength. Currency swings can change reported revenue for a company that sells abroad, even when unit sales hold steady.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Revenue across different industries says little, because a grocery chain and a software firm sell at completely different scales. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies sell similar things to similar customers.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A dominant leader that likely sets industry pricing and enjoys scale. |
| A cluster of similar revenues | A fragmented industry where no single company controls the market. |
| A small company growing fast | A challenger taking share, worth watching even though it is not yet the largest. |

Always read revenue next to the profit margins before judging a leader. The largest seller is not always the best business, because high sales won through heavy discounting can come with thin or negative profit.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's revenue over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that grows its revenue faster than the rest of the industry is taking market share. A company whose revenue is flat while rivals grow is losing ground, even if its sales are not falling.

The most useful pattern is a change in the order of the leaders. A company climbing past larger rivals is gaining strength and may be the better forward-looking pick. A long-time leader whose growth has stalled may be ceding its position. Steady growth across the whole group points to a healthy, expanding industry, while flat or falling revenue across the group points to a shrinking one.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Revenue measures how much each company sells, and the net income ratio measures how much profit it keeps from each sales dollar. Revenue alone rewards size, so the largest seller can look the strongest while earning little. Reading the two together shows whether the biggest seller is also turning those sales into real profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with high revenue and a high net income ratio is both large and efficient, the strongest position in the industry. A company with high revenue but a low net income ratio is winning sales at the cost of profit, often through discounting. The company with smaller revenue but a far higher margin may be the better business, because its sales are more profitable even though they are fewer.

### Net Income Growth

**Why read the two together.** Revenue shows current size, and net income growth shows how fast profit is rising. A large company can be stagnant while a smaller one grows quickly. Putting the two next to each other separates the established leaders from the rising challengers within the industry.

**What the combination tells you.** A large company with strong profit growth is extending its lead and is the safest leader in the group. A large company with no profit growth may be a mature business that smaller rivals are catching. A small company with fast profit growth is the one most likely to climb the revenue ranking in future years, which makes it a candidate worth early attention.

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Revenue is sales recorded on the income statement, while free cash flow is the actual cash left after the company pays to run and maintain itself. A company can report large revenue yet generate little cash if its business swallows that money. Reading the two together shows whether the largest seller also produces the most spendable cash.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that leads on both revenue and free cash flow is a large business that genuinely turns sales into cash, the most durable kind of leader. A company that leads on revenue but trails on free cash flow is selling a great deal without converting it to cash, a warning sign. The company that generates the most free cash flow per dollar of revenue is often the higher-quality business, even when another company sells more.

## Part 8: Limitations

Revenue measures size rather than quality, so the largest seller in an industry can be a weaker investment than a smaller and more profitable rival. A high ranking on revenue says nothing about whether the company earns a profit, holds little debt, or trades at a fair price. Revenue can be inflated for a year by an acquisition or by aggressive booking of sales, neither of which reflects lasting strength.

The number also depends on how each company is structured, so two companies of similar real size can report very different revenue. A company that records the full sale price looks larger than one that records only its own share of a deal. The comparative view shows which company is biggest, not which is best, so a high revenue ranking is a starting point for research rather than a verdict on the stock.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company leads the industry on revenue, and how large is its lead over the second-place company?
2. Is the largest seller also one of the more profitable companies, or does its size come with thin margins from discounting?
3. Which company is growing revenue fastest, and is it taking share from a specific larger rival?
4. Are the companies clustered close together in a fragmented industry, or spread far apart where one or two giants control it?
5. Has any company's revenue jumped from an acquisition rather than from selling more of its own products?
6. Does the revenue leader also convert its sales into the most free cash flow, or does a smaller company convert better?
7. Which company has gained the most ground in the ranking over the past several years, and what in the business explains the climb?
8. Does the largest company hold its lead through scale and brand, or is a faster-growing challenger closing the gap?
