# Actual P/B

## Part 1: Definition

Actual price-to-book measures how many dollars investors pay in the market for each dollar of a company's book equity. Book equity, also called book value, is what the owners are left with after every debt is subtracted from everything the company owns, as recorded on the balance sheet. The market part is the company's market capitalization, the total price the market puts on all its shares. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company the market prices richly against its own net worth and which it prices cheaply. A company with an actual price-to-book of 1 is priced at exactly its book value. A direct competitor at 3 is priced at three times its book value.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry record their assets under the same accounting rules, so their book values are built on a shared footing. This metric is the core relative-value lens for banks, because a bank's assets are mostly financial and carried close to their true worth, which makes book value a solid yardstick. The actual number is most useful when read against the justified price-to-book, the level the company's own profitability deserves.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Highest in the industry | The market pays the most per dollar of book value, expecting strong and lasting profit on equity. |
| Middle of the pack | Priced in line with the industry's typical net worth. The market sees no special promise or threat. |
| Lowest in the industry | The cheapest against book value, either a bargain or a business the market expects to earn poorly. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`P/B = Market Capitalization ÷ Book Value of Equity`

Market capitalization is the share price multiplied by the number of shares. Book value of equity is the owners' stake on the balance sheet, total assets minus total liabilities. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from lowest to highest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is priced against the same market on the same day.

A useful way to read a company's number is against its justified price-to-book, which rises when return on equity exceeds the cost of equity, the return owners demand for the risk. When a company earns more on its equity than owners require, the market rightly pays above book value. The actual number tells the reader what the market charges. The justified number tells the reader what the fundamentals deserve. The gap between them is the heart of the comparison.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Actual price-to-book is the most common way to judge which company in an asset-heavy industry is cheap and which is dear. Raw market capitalization says nothing about value, because the largest company usually carries the biggest market value. Price-to-book removes size from the picture and asks how much the market pays for each dollar of recorded net worth. That question is what ranks the companies by relative price against their balance sheets.

The ratio is the workhorse of relative valuation for banks and other lenders. Earnings for these firms can swing with one-time provisions, so a profit multiple can mislead, while book value stays steadier and more comparable. A company priced far above book value is one the market expects to keep earning a high return on equity. A company priced near or below book value is one the market doubts, or one other buyers have overlooked.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is return on equity, the profit earned on each dollar of book value. A company that earns more on its equity deserves a higher price-to-book, because each dollar of book value produces more profit. The second is risk. A steadier, safer company earns a higher price-to-book, because its profit is more certain. The third is expected growth, since a company growing its equity base profitably is worth more per dollar of book value today.

A difference can also be an accounting effect rather than a real one. Share buybacks shrink book equity and lift the ratio, so a company that has bought back many shares can look expensive against book value without being a better business. A write-down shrinks book equity and lifts the ratio too. Different rules for goodwill and intangible assets can swell or shrink book value across companies, so two firms with the same economics can show different ratios for reasons that are purely accounting.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Price-to-book varies widely across industries, because a capital-light software firm carries little book value while a bank carries a large one. A ratio of 2 can be cheap for one industry and dear for another, so cross-industry comparison is misleading. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies record their assets on the same terms.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | The market expects a lasting high return on equity. Confirm with the profitability metrics in this view. |
| Cluster of similar ratios | The market sees the companies as alike, with no standout winner or loser against book value. |
| One company far below the rest | A possible bargain, or a business the market expects to earn a weak return. Check return on equity before buying the discount. |

Always read the actual number against the justified price-to-book in this view. A company priced below its justified level may be a bargain, while a company priced above it may be running on optimism the profitability cannot support.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's price-to-book over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company the market has paid above book value for year after year usually earns a return on equity its peers cannot match. A company whose ratio is climbing is one the market is warming to. A company whose ratio is sinking toward or below book value is one the market is leaving behind.

The most useful pattern is a ratio that drifts far from where the company has historically traded against its peers. A company now far cheaper than its own past relative to the industry may be mispriced, or may be facing a real decline in profitability. A company now far dearer than its past may be priced for a return on equity it has to keep delivering. The history shows whether today's ranking is normal for these companies or a recent shift worth explaining.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Justified P/B

**Why read the two together.** Actual price-to-book shows what the market charges for each dollar of book value, and justified price-to-book shows what the fundamentals deserve. The justified level rises with return on equity and falls with risk, so it is the fair yardstick for the actual price. Putting the two side by side turns a raw price ranking into a value judgment, because it shows whether each company is priced above or below what its profitability warrants.

**What the combination tells you.** A company trading below its justified price-to-book is the most interesting find in the table, because the market is paying less than the business deserves. A company trading well above its justified level is the most exposed, because the price assumes a return on equity the business has to keep earning. Comparing the gap across companies tells the reader which firm offers the widest discount to its own fair value.

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity measures how much profit a company earns on each dollar of book value, and price-to-book measures what the market pays for that book value. A company that earns more on its equity deserves a higher price-to-book, so the two belong together. Reading them as a pair shows whether the most expensive companies are also the best earners, or whether the price has run ahead of the profitability.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high return on equity and a moderate price-to-book is the strongest combination, because the reader buys a top earner without paying a top price. A company with a high price-to-book but only an average return on equity is paying for a reputation the numbers do not support. The best pick is often the company whose return on equity ranks higher than its price-to-book, because the market has not yet fully rewarded the profitability.

### Excess Return (ROE − Ke)

**Why read the two together.** Excess return is how far a company's return on equity sits above its cost of equity, the return owners demand. A company earns more than book value in the market only when this excess return is positive, so it is the engine behind a high price-to-book. Putting the two next to each other shows whether a rich price rests on real excess profit or on hope.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a wide positive excess return and a moderate price-to-book is a strong earner the market has not fully priced. A company with a high price-to-book but a thin or negative excess return is priced beyond what its profitability justifies. The reader can use this pairing to find the company whose genuine excess profit is not yet reflected in its market price against book value.

## Part 8: Limitations

Actual price-to-book can rank a weaker business above a stronger one when accounting distorts book value. A company that has bought back many shares shows a smaller book equity and a higher ratio, which makes it look expensive without being a better business. A company that just took a large write-down shows the same effect. The ratio also breaks when a company has negative book equity, which can follow a string of losses or heavy buybacks, because dividing by a negative number gives a meaningless figure that distorts the ranking.

The number reflects today's price and the latest book value, not the future. A low ratio can mark a real and lasting fall in profitability rather than a bargain, so the cheapest company against book value is not automatically the best buy. The comparative view shows how the market prices each company against its net worth, not whether the market is correct, so a low ranking invites research rather than ending it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company is the cheapest in the industry against book value, and is the low price a bargain or a business the market expects to earn poorly?
2. Does each company's actual price-to-book sit above or below its justified price-to-book, and which company offers the widest discount to its own fair value?
3. Does the most expensive company against book value also earn the highest return on equity, or has the price run ahead of the profitability?
4. Does any company show a low ratio only because heavy buybacks or a recent write-down shrank its book equity?
5. Does any company show a very high ratio or negative book equity that makes its rank misleading?
6. How does each company's ratio compare with where it has traded against the industry in the past?
7. Are the companies clustered at similar ratios, which would suggest the market sees them as alike, or spread apart, which would suggest a clear winner and loser?
8. Which company pairs a low price-to-book with a wide positive excess return, marking strong profitability the market has underpriced?
