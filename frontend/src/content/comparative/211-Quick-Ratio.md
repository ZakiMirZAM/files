# Quick Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

Quick ratio measures whether a company can cover its short-term bills using only its most liquid assets, leaving out inventory. The liquid assets are cash, short-term investments, and money owed by customers, all of which can be turned into cash quickly. Inventory is left out because selling it takes time and is not guaranteed. The short-term bills are debts and payments due within a year. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company could meet its near-term obligations without relying on a sale of goods. A company with a quick ratio of 1.5 has 1.5 dollars of quick assets for every dollar of short-term bills. A direct competitor at 0.5 has only 50 cents on the same dollar.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry hold similar kinds of inventory and collect from customers on similar terms, so the cushion each keeps in truly liquid form reflects its own choices. A higher quick ratio points to dependable short-term safety. A lower one points to a company that would need to sell inventory or raise cash to meet a sudden bill.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| High in the industry | The most dependable short-term safety, held in cash and receivables rather than goods. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical liquid comfort for the industry. The company can meet bills without leaning hard on inventory. |
| Low in the industry | Thin liquid cushion, meaning the company depends on selling inventory to cover near-term bills. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities`

Current assets are everything expected to become cash within a year. Inventory is subtracted because it is the slowest current asset to turn into cash. Current liabilities are everything due within a year. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is measured on the same date.

Quick ratio is the stricter sibling of the current ratio, often called the acid test because it sets a tougher bar. The current ratio counts inventory among the resources available to pay bills, while the quick ratio refuses to, on the view that inventory cannot be relied on in a pinch. The two share the same denominator, so the only difference is whether inventory counts, which makes the gap between them a direct read on how much a company leans on its goods.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Quick ratio is the most demanding read on which company in an industry has the most dependable short-term safety. A company can look safe on the current ratio while most of its cushion sits in inventory that may be hard to sell. Stripping out inventory shows which companies could pay their near-term bills even if sales stalled. Ranking on the quick ratio separates real liquidity from liquidity that depends on moving goods.

The metric is most useful inside one industry, where companies carry inventory of similar character. An industry with goods that sell fast can lean on inventory more safely than one with goods that move slowly, so the quick ratio matters most where inventory is hardest to convert. A company that holds a strong quick ratio year after year keeps its safety in the most certain form. A company near the bottom is one whose short-term comfort would vanish if its inventory could not be sold.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is how much of each company's short-term resources sits in inventory rather than cash. A company that holds large inventory shows a much lower quick ratio than current ratio. The second is the cash and receivables balance. A company that collects from customers quickly and keeps cash on hand scores higher. The third is reliance on short-term debt, which enlarges current liabilities and lowers the ratio.

A difference can also reflect the business model rather than safety. A company that sells to customers on credit carries large receivables, which count as quick assets and lift the ratio, while a cash-only seller may carry less. A company that has just stocked up ahead of a busy season shows a temporarily low quick ratio because inventory is high and cash has been spent. The number can move for ordinary operating reasons that say little about real strength.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. The right level for a quick ratio depends on how fast an industry's inventory and receivables turn into cash, so a figure that is safe in one industry can be thin in another. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies hold similar assets and collect on similar terms, and the same level counts as safe or thin for all of them.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | The most dependable cushion, held in cash and receivables rather than goods. |
| Cluster of similar numbers | An industry where companies keep similar liquid safety and none stands out. |
| One company far below the rest | A company leaning on inventory to cover bills, which is the least certain form of safety. |

Always compare the quick ratio against the current ratio for the same company. A large gap between the two means the company's apparent safety rests on inventory, so the quick ratio is the more honest read on which company could actually pay its bills in a tight moment.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's quick ratio over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that holds a steady quick ratio over time keeps dependable safety as a habit. A single reading proves less, because the snapshot depends on the exact day the year closed and can swing with stocking cycles.

The most useful pattern is a quick ratio that drops while the current ratio holds steady, because that gap means a company is shifting its cushion into inventory and out of cash. A company whose quick ratio rises is building safety in its most certain form. The history shows whether a thin ranking today is a lasting trait or a temporary dip from stocking up, and whether a company's dependable safety is improving or eroding against its peers.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Current Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Current ratio counts inventory among short-term resources and quick ratio leaves it out, so the two differ only by inventory. Reading them as a pair isolates exactly how much of each company's cushion depends on selling goods. The gap between the two is the single best read on the quality of a company's liquidity. The pairing turns two similar numbers into a clear measure of how dependable the safety is.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose current and quick ratios sit close together keeps its cushion in cash and receivables, which is dependable. A company with a wide gap between the two relies on inventory it still has to sell, which is far less certain in a downturn. The reader should rank a company's true short-term safety by its quick ratio and read the gap to the current ratio as a warning sign.

### Net Debt To OCF

**Why read the two together.** Quick ratio shows dependable short-term safety on one day, while net debt to operating cash flow shows the weight of debt against the cash the business generates. Near-term liquidity and longer-term debt pressure are separate risks, and reading them together gives the fuller view. The pairing checks whether a company that can cover this quarter's bills is also sound over the years ahead.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a strong quick ratio and low net debt to operating cash flow is dependable on both the short and long view. A company with a comfortable quick ratio but heavy net debt may meet near-term bills while still carrying a load that strains it over time. The reader can use the pairing to separate companies that are merely liquid this quarter from companies that are durably sound.

### Debt Service Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Debt service coverage shows whether a company earns enough to cover its scheduled debt payments, and quick ratio shows whether it holds enough liquid assets to meet bills on short notice. Coverage from earnings and cushion from liquid assets are two ways to survive a tight stretch, so reading both shows the full picture of resilience. The pairing checks safety from both the income side and the balance-sheet side.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks well on both has strong protection, because it can pay debt from earnings and meet sudden bills from liquid assets. A company strong on one but weak on the other has a single point of weakness that a hard stretch could expose. The reader can use the pairing to find which company in the industry is best armored against a cash crunch and which is most fragile.

## Part 8: Limitations

Quick ratio can understate a company that holds fast-selling inventory, because it treats all inventory as unsellable when some industries convert goods to cash within days. The comparison also misleads when a strong company runs a thin quick ratio on purpose, since easy borrowing makes a small liquid cushion harmless for that firm. A high number can reflect large receivables that the company may struggle to collect rather than dependable cash.

The number is a single snapshot taken on one day, not a measure of cash flow through the year. A company that has just stocked up or just paid a large bill can show a misleading reading. The comparative view shows which company holds the most dependable short-term cushion on paper, not which company is the best business or the cheapest to buy, so a high ranking is one input rather than a conclusion.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company holds the most dependable short-term cushion in the industry, measured in cash and receivables rather than inventory?
2. How wide is the gap between each company's quick and current ratios, and which companies depend most on selling inventory for their safety?
3. Does the company with the lowest quick ratio face genuine strain, or is it running tight because it can borrow easily?
4. Has any company's quick ratio fallen while its current ratio held steady, signaling a shift of the cushion into inventory?
5. Do the quick assets rest on receivables the company may struggle to collect rather than on cash?
6. Are the companies clustered together, which would suggest similar liquid safety, or spread apart, which would suggest very different choices?
7. Does a company with a thin quick ratio also cover its debt payments poorly, leaving it exposed from two directions?
8. Is a low quick ratio a temporary effect of stocking up before a busy season, or a lasting trait of the business?
