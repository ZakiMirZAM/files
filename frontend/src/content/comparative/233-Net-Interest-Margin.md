# Net Interest Margin

## Part 1: Definition

Net interest margin measures how much a lender earns from interest, after its own funding cost, for each dollar of assets it holds. A lender is a company such as a bank that takes in money and lends it out. It earns interest on the loans and securities it holds, called earning assets, and it pays interest on the deposits and borrowing it funds them with. Net interest income is what is left after subtracting the interest paid from the interest earned. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large lender in one industry, so the reader can see which one captures the widest spread between what it earns and what it pays. A bank with a net interest margin of 3 percent keeps 3 cents of net interest a year for every dollar of assets.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Lenders in one industry face the same interest rates, the same borrowers, and the same competition for deposits. When those conditions are shared, a wider net interest margin points to a genuinely better lending franchise, one that funds itself cheaply or lends at higher rates without taking on reckless risk.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Widest in the industry | The strongest lending spread, often from cheap deposits or higher-yield lending. Check the risk of the loan book. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical spread for the industry. The lending franchise is neither advantaged nor weak. |
| Narrowest in the industry | A weaker spread, from costly funding, low-yield lending, or pressure from competition. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Net Interest Margin = Net Interest Income ÷ Average Earning Assets`

Net interest income is the interest a lender earns on its assets minus the interest it pays on its funding. Average earning assets is the typical balance of loans and securities held over the year. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from widest to narrowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every lender faces the same rate environment at that time.

The margin standardizes the spread so lenders of different sizes can be compared. A larger bank earns more total interest, but that says nothing about the quality of its spread. Dividing net interest income by earning assets strips out size and leaves a clean read on how wide a spread each lender captures on every dollar it puts to work.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Net interest margin is the cleanest single measure of which lender in an industry runs the better core franchise. A lender lives on the spread between what it earns on assets and what it pays on funding, so the margin sits at the center of its profit. Raw interest income rewards size, since the biggest bank usually earns the most interest. The margin removes size and asks how well each lender prices its loans and funds itself, which is what separates a strong franchise from a merely large one.

A lender that holds a wide net interest margin year after year, above the rest of its industry, usually owns something competitors cannot copy. Cheap and sticky deposits are the most common source, because a bank that funds itself for almost nothing keeps more of every dollar it earns on loans. A wide gap between the best and worst lender in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the leader is funding or lending on terms the others cannot match.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two lenders. The first is funding cost, the rate paid on deposits and borrowing. A lender rich in low-cost deposits keeps a wider spread than one that relies on expensive wholesale funding. The second is asset yield, the rate earned on loans and securities. A lender that lends to higher-rate borrowers earns more on each asset. The third is the mix of assets, since a book weighted toward higher-yield consumer loans earns more than one weighted toward low-yield government securities.

A difference can also reflect risk rather than skill. A lender can widen its margin by lending to riskier borrowers at higher rates, which lifts the number until those loans go bad. A wide margin built on risky lending is more fragile than the same margin built on cheap deposits. The rate environment also matters, because a sudden move in interest rates can widen or squeeze a lender's spread before it can reprice its loans and deposits.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only lenders inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Net interest margin is a metric for banks and other lenders, so it is meaningless for a company that does not live on a lending spread. Different kinds of lenders also run different margins, since a consumer lender earns a wider spread than a wholesale-funded mortgage lender. Within one lending industry the comparison is fair, because the companies share the same rates and the same competition.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One lender far above the rest | A likely funding or lending advantage, unless the wide spread comes from risky loans. Confirm with credit quality. |
| Cluster of similar margins | A competitive lending market where no lender has a real edge on spread. |
| A leader whose margin is shrinking | Competition for deposits is rising, or rates are squeezing the spread. |

Always check the credit quality behind a wide margin before crediting it as the best franchise. A wide spread earned by lending to risky borrowers can reverse fast when those loans default, while a wide spread built on cheap deposits is far more durable.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each lender's net interest margin over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A lender that stays at the top of its industry for many years has the most convincing claim to a durable funding or lending advantage. A single wide year proves far less, because one strong year can come from a temporary swing in interest rates.

The most useful pattern is a widening or narrowing gap between the leader and the rest. A leader holding its spread while others narrow is defending a real advantage, often cheap deposits. A leader whose margin is converging with the pack is losing the edge that set it apart. Because the whole industry's margin tends to move with interest rates, the reader should separate a rate-driven shift that lifts everyone from a structural change that helps or hurts one lender alone.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Net interest margin shows how wide a spread a lender earns on its assets, and return on equity shows how much profit it earns on owner money. The margin is a main driver of a lender's profit, so a wide spread should feed a strong return on equity. Putting the two side by side shows whether a lender's spread advantage actually reaches the bottom line or is eaten by costs and loan losses along the way.

**What the combination tells you.** A lender with both a wide margin and a high return on equity is converting its spread into real profit, which is the strongest result in the table. A lender with a wide margin but only an average return on equity is losing the spread to high costs or rising loan losses. The reader can use this pairing to find the lender whose franchise advantage survives all the way to shareholder profit.

### Debt Service Coverage

**Why read the two together.** A wide net interest margin can come from lending to riskier borrowers at higher rates, which raises the spread until those loans sour. Debt service coverage gauges how comfortably borrowers can meet their payments, which signals how safe a lender's loan book is. Reading the two together separates a margin earned from cheap funding from a margin earned by taking on credit risk.

**What the combination tells you.** A lender with a wide margin alongside healthy coverage in its loan book is earning its spread safely. A lender with a wide margin alongside weak coverage is reaching for yield, and its spread can collapse when borrowers default. Comparing the two across the industry tells the reader which leader earns its spread from a strong franchise and which earns it by stretching for risk.

### Financial Leverage (Equity Multiplier)

**Why read the two together.** Net interest margin is earned on a lender's assets, and financial leverage shows how many dollars of assets each dollar of equity supports. A lender amplifies a thin margin into a strong return on equity by holding many assets against little equity. Putting the two together shows how much of a lender's profit comes from the spread itself and how much comes from running a large balance sheet on a small equity base.

**What the combination tells you.** A lender with a decent margin and moderate leverage earns its profit from the spread on a safe balance sheet. A lender with a thin margin and very high leverage is relying on a stretched balance sheet to manufacture its return, which is more fragile in a downturn. Comparing the two across the industry tells the reader which lender's profit rests on a real spread and which rests on heavy leverage.

## Part 8: Limitations

Net interest margin can rank a riskier lender above a safer one when the wide spread comes from lending to weaker borrowers at higher rates. The number captures the spread but not the loan losses that can follow, so a wide margin today can turn into heavy write-offs tomorrow. The metric also ignores fee income, so a lender that earns much of its profit from fees rather than lending can look weak on margin while running a strong business overall.

The number reflects the past year's rates, not the future. A lender at the top today may be earning its spread from a rate environment that is about to turn against it. The metric also applies only to lenders, so it is meaningless for the non-lending companies that may share an industry. The comparative view shows which lender captured the widest spread, not which is the safest or the cheapest stock, so a wide margin is a starting point for research rather than a verdict.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which lender leads the industry on this metric, and is the wide spread built on cheap deposits or on lending to riskier borrowers?
2. Does the lender with the widest margin also lead on return on equity, or is the spread lost to costs and loan losses before it reaches profit?
3. How long has the leader held the widest margin? Is the gap with the rest of the industry widening or narrowing?
4. Does any lender show a wide margin alongside weak borrower coverage, which would point to a spread earned by taking credit risk?
5. Is a lender's wide margin driven by genuine franchise strength or by running a heavily leveraged balance sheet?
6. Are the lenders clustered together, which would suggest a competitive market with no edge, or spread apart, which would suggest one lender funds itself far more cheaply?
7. Which lender would best hold its spread if interest rates moved sharply, judging by its deposit base and asset mix?
8. Does the lender with the widest margin also rank well on credit quality, or does the spread come with a riskier loan book?
