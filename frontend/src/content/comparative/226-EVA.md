# EVA

## Part 1: Definition

EVA measures how much profit a company earns above the full cost of the money it uses. EVA stands for economic value added, the profit left after a company is charged for every dollar of capital it employs. The capital charge is the amount of invested capital multiplied by the cost of that capital, which is the yearly return lenders and owners expect. A company that earns more than this charge creates value. A company that earns less destroys value, even when its accounting profit looks healthy. The comparative view shows EVA in dollars side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which companies add real economic profit and which only cover their costs.

The side-by-side view carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry face the same customers, the same input costs, and the same competition. EVA goes one step beyond ordinary profit by subtracting a charge for capital, so a positive number means the company is beating the return its investors could earn elsewhere at the same risk. About half of public companies fail this test in a typical year, so a positive EVA is a real distinction.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| High positive EVA | The company earns well above the cost of its capital. A genuine value creator. |
| EVA near zero | The company just covers its capital cost. It runs in place and adds little. |
| Negative EVA | The company earns less than its capital costs and is destroying value, despite any reported profit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`EVA = NOPAT − (Invested Capital × WACC)`

NOPAT is net operating profit after taxes, the profit the business earns from operations after tax but before interest. Invested capital is the total money put into the business by lenders and owners. WACC is the weighted average cost of capital, the blended yearly return those investors require. The product of invested capital and WACC is the capital charge, the toll the company must clear before any profit counts as economic profit. The comparative table computes EVA for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest.

A plain way to read the number is three lines: sales, minus operating costs, minus the cost of capital. What remains is EVA. The capital charge is the part most profit measures forget. Two companies can report the same operating profit, yet the one that ties up far more capital earns a smaller EVA, because its capital charge is larger. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is measured against the same standard at the same time.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

EVA is the cleanest test of whether a company in an industry creates value at all. Ordinary profit rewards size and ignores the cost of capital, so a large company can report a big profit while still earning less than its investors require. EVA charges for the capital, so it separates companies that earn a true surplus from companies that merely look profitable. That question is what distinguishes a value creator from a value destroyer inside the same industry.

A company that holds a high positive EVA year after year, above the rest of its industry, is usually defending an advantage competitors cannot copy. EVA also reflects capital discipline directly, because a company that grows by pouring capital into low-return projects lifts its reported profit but lowers its EVA. A wide gap between the best and worst EVA in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the leader is earning a surplus the others cannot.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the spread between the return on capital and the cost of capital, since a wider spread produces more economic profit. The second is the amount of invested capital, since a large base earning a positive spread produces more EVA in dollars. The third is the cost of capital itself, since a riskier company faces a higher charge, which shrinks its EVA even when its operating profit is strong.

A difference can also come from how capital is measured rather than from the business. Past write-downs shrink invested capital, which lowers the capital charge and flatters EVA. Heavy reinvestment raises invested capital before the profit it funds arrives, which depresses EVA for a time. EVA is a dollar figure, so a large company can show a bigger EVA than a smaller rival simply because it is bigger, not because it is a better operator. The per-dollar version, EVA margin, removes that size effect.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare EVA inside one industry, which is exactly what this view does. EVA in dollars rewards size, so the largest company often shows the largest EVA even when a smaller rival earns a wider spread on each dollar of capital. The fair reading pairs the dollar figure with EVA margin and with the company's invested capital, so the reader can tell a large value creator from a merely large company.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A likely value creator, unless its lead comes only from its larger size. Check EVA margin to confirm. |
| Cluster near zero | A commodity-like industry where companies barely clear their cost of capital. |
| One or more negative | Companies destroying value, often in a heavy investment phase or facing a weak return spread. |

Always check whether a high EVA rests on a durable return spread or on a low cost of capital. A wide spread that competition is likely to erode will not support the EVA for long.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's EVA over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that holds a positive EVA for many years has the strongest claim to a durable advantage, because it keeps beating its cost of capital while rivals fade. A single strong year proves far less, because one good year can come from a temporary boom or a one-time gain.

The most useful pattern is a steadily rising EVA, which means the company is widening the spread between its returns and its capital cost or growing profitably on top of it. A company whose EVA drifts toward zero is losing that spread and adding less value each year. A company climbing from negative toward positive is improving, and that turn can matter as much as a leader extending its lead. Watching how the ranking shifts shows which value creation is durable and which is fading.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** The capital charge inside EVA is invested capital multiplied by WACC, so WACC sets the hurdle each company must clear. The WACC column shows that hurdle for every company, which exposes the bar built into the EVA calculation. Reading the two together shows whether a high EVA was earned over a demanding hurdle or handed to a company by a low one.

**What the combination tells you.** A high EVA earned despite a high WACC is the strongest result, because the company adds value over a steep bar. A high EVA paired with a low WACC may flatter a business that only looks good because its hurdle is gentle. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies create value over a demanding standard and which benefit mainly from a forgiving cost of capital.

### EVA Margin

**Why read the two together.** EVA in dollars rewards size, while EVA margin states the same economic profit per dollar of sales. The EVA margin column removes the size effect, so the reader can tell a large value creator from a company that simply books a big number because it is big. Putting the two side by side separates scale from quality.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on both EVA and EVA margin is a strong value creator at any size, because the surplus is large in dollars and rich per sale. A company high on EVA but only average on EVA margin owes its dollar lead to its size rather than its economics. The company with the best EVA margin is often the better operator even when a larger rival shows a bigger EVA.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is the return side of the EVA spread, and WACC is the cost side. EVA combines both into a dollar figure, while return on invested capital shows the percentage return on its own. Reading them together shows whether a high EVA comes from a high return on capital or merely from a large capital base.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on return on invested capital earns a wide spread over its cost of capital, which is the engine of a durable EVA. A company with a positive EVA but only an ordinary return on invested capital is leaning on size, and its lead is more fragile. The company with the best return on invested capital usually has the most reliable EVA, because the surplus comes from each dollar of capital rather than from the count of dollars.

## Part 8: Limitations

EVA in dollars rewards size, so it can rank a large average business above a smaller excellent one. The figure also depends on the measure of invested capital, which accounting can distort through past write-downs and buybacks, and on the cost of capital, where a small change in WACC can flip a company from positive to negative. A company that just took a large write-down can show a higher EVA for the wrong reason, because its capital base shrank.

EVA fits some industries poorly. Banks and property companies are hard to value with the economic profit model, so their figures may be missing or unreliable. The number also reflects the past year, not the future, and a wide spread today may narrow as competition arrives. A high EVA shows which company added the most value last year, not which stock is cheapest to buy, so a top ranking is a starting point for research rather than a verdict.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company earns the highest EVA, and is the lead built on a wide return spread or simply on a larger capital base?
2. Does the EVA leader also lead on EVA margin, or does its dollar advantage disappear once size is removed?
3. Which companies show a negative EVA, and are they destroying value or in a heavy investment phase that will pay off later?
4. Does the company earn its EVA over a demanding cost of capital, or does a low WACC flatter the result?
5. How has each company's EVA trended over several years, and is the leader extending its lead or being caught?
6. Are the companies clustered near zero, which would suggest a commodity industry, or spread apart, which would suggest one company has a real edge?
7. Does any company show a higher EVA only because a recent write-down shrank its invested capital?
8. Is the return spread that lifts the highest EVA durable, or is competition likely to erode it?
