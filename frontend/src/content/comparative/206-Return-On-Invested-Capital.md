# Return On Invested Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Return on invested capital measures how much operating profit a company earns for each dollar of capital put into the business, counting both owner money and borrowed money. Invested capital is the total of equity and debt that funds the company's operations. The metric is often shortened to ROIC. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company earns the best return on all the money it uses. A company with a return on invested capital of 20 percent earns 20 cents of after-tax operating profit a year for every dollar of capital. A competitor at 6 percent earns only 6 cents.

The side-by-side comparison strips away how each company is financed, because it counts equity and debt together. The metric is therefore the truest read on the efficiency of the underlying business. Within one industry the companies use capital in similar ways, so a higher return on invested capital usually points to a genuinely stronger business rather than to clever borrowing.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The most efficient business, often one protected by a durable advantage. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical capital efficiency for the industry, with no clear edge. |
| Bottom of the industry | A weaker business, a capital-heavy operator, or a company earning less than its capital costs. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`ROIC = Net Operating Profit After Tax ÷ Invested Capital`

Net operating profit after tax is the profit the core business earns after tax but before interest, so it ignores how the company is financed. Invested capital is the equity plus the debt that funds operations. Dividing one by the other gives the return. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared year is what makes the comparison fair, because it removes the question of which company reported more recently.

The profit measure used here removes the effects of financing decisions, so debt cannot flatter the number. This treatment is the key difference from return on equity, which can be lifted by borrowing. By counting all capital and using profit before interest, return on invested capital gets as close as possible to the true efficiency of the business.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Return on invested capital is the cleanest single measure of which company in an industry runs the best underlying business. Return on equity can be inflated by debt, which makes a heavily borrowed company look stronger than it is. Return on invested capital removes that distortion by counting debt and equity together, so a high number must be earned by the business itself rather than by financial engineering.

A company that earns a high return on invested capital year after year, above its industry and above what its capital costs, is usually protected by something rivals cannot copy. That durable advantage is often called a moat. The widest moats show up as the highest and most stable returns on capital. A wide gap between the best and worst company in the same industry is a strong signal, because the leader is earning a return the others cannot reach.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Two forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the profit the business earns from its operations, which reflects pricing power and cost control. The second is how much capital the company must tie up to earn that profit. A company that generates strong profit from a light base of assets earns a high return, while a capital-heavy company earns less on the same profit.

A difference can also come from the stage or structure of the business rather than from quality. A company part-way through a large expansion shows a low return because new capital has been raised before the profit it will fund arrives. A company that has written down old assets carries a smaller capital base, which can lift the return for reasons unrelated to performance. An older company with fully depreciated plants can show a higher return than a rival still paying for new ones.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Capital needs differ enormously across industries, so a high return in a capital-light industry and a moderate one in a heavy industry are not the same achievement. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies tie up capital in similar ways.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A likely moat, because the lead cannot come from borrowing the way a return-on-equity lead can. |
| A cluster of similar returns | A commodity-like industry where no company earns more than its capital costs. |
| A leader whose return is falling | A fading advantage, rising competition, or a heavy new investment that has not yet paid off. |

Always compare each company's return against its cost of capital, the rate it must earn to justify the money it uses. A company earning above its cost of capital is creating value, while one earning below it is destroying value even if the raw number looks acceptable.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's return on invested capital over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that holds a high return for many years has the most convincing claim to a durable moat. A single strong year proves far less, because one good year can come from a temporary boom or a recently shrunken capital base.

The most useful pattern is a widening or narrowing gap between the leader and the rest. A leader holding its return steady while rivals fade is defending a real advantage. A leader whose return is sliding toward the pack is losing the edge that justified its premium. A company whose return is climbing toward the leader's is often building an advantage and may be the better forward-looking pick.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity can be inflated by debt, while return on invested capital counts equity and debt together so borrowing cannot flatter it. The gap between the two ratios shows how much of a company's return on equity comes from leverage rather than from the business. Reading them together separates real operating skill from financial engineering.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on both earns its return from the business itself, which makes the result trustworthy. A company that ranks high on return on equity but only average on return on invested capital is leaning on debt to lift the headline number. The company with the best return on invested capital is usually the safest pick, even when another company shows a flashier return on equity.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is the return a company earns on its capital, and WACC is the blended cost of that capital, the hurdle the return must clear. A return only creates value when it sits above the cost. Reading the two together turns the raw return into a verdict on whether the company is building or destroying value.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose return on invested capital sits well above its WACC is creating value with every dollar it invests, the mark of a strong business. A company whose return sits below its WACC is destroying value even if the return looks respectable in isolation. Comparing the gap between return and WACC across the group ranks the companies by genuine value creation, not just by raw profitability.

### Sales To Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital combines two things, the profit margin and how hard the capital works. Sales to invested capital isolates the second part, showing how much revenue each dollar of capital generates. Reading the two together shows whether a company's return comes from fat margins or from working its capital hard.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high return on invested capital and high sales to invested capital earns its return by turning capital over quickly, a sign of an efficient, asset-light model. A company with a high return but low sales to invested capital earns it through wide margins instead. Comparing the two paths across the industry shows which kind of advantage each leader relies on and how vulnerable that advantage might be.

## Part 8: Limitations

Return on invested capital can be distorted by the age and accounting of a company's assets, so an older company with depreciated plants can outrank a rival still paying for new ones without being a better business. The measure also depends on how invested capital is defined, and small differences in that definition can shift the ranking. A company in a heavy investment phase can show a low return for a year that says nothing about its long-run quality.

The number reflects the past year, not the future. A high return can rest on an advantage that competitors are about to erode. The comparative view shows which company earns the best return on its capital, not which company is the cheapest to buy, so a high ranking is a starting point for research rather than a verdict on the stock. A high return must also be checked against the cost of capital before it can be called value creation.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company leads the industry on return on invested capital, and how wide is the gap to the rest?
2. Does the leader's return sit clearly above its cost of capital, or is it only earning back roughly what its capital costs?
3. How long has the leader held its high return? Is the advantage stable, widening, or fading toward the pack?
4. Does the company with the highest return on equity also lead on return on invested capital, or does its lead vanish once debt is removed?
5. Is any company's return depressed by a heavy investment phase that has not yet started paying off?
6. Does the leader earn its return through wide margins or through working its capital hard, judging by sales to invested capital?
7. Could the age or write-down history of a company's assets be flattering its return relative to rivals?
8. Which company's return is climbing toward the leader's, and what in the business explains the improvement?
