# Price To Earnings

## Part 1: Definition

Price to earnings measures how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of a company's yearly profit. The price part is the share price, the amount one share costs in the market. The earnings part is the profit per share, the company's net profit divided by the number of shares. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company carries the cheapest price tag and which carries the dearest. A company with a price to earnings of 12 costs 12 dollars for every dollar of yearly profit. A direct competitor at 30 costs 30 dollars for the same dollar of profit.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry share customers, costs, and competition, so the market judges them against each other. A company priced well above its peers is one the market expects to grow faster or to carry less risk. A company priced well below its peers is one the market doubts, or one that other buyers have overlooked.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Lowest in the industry | The cheapest stock, either a bargain or a business the market expects to weaken. |
| Middle of the pack | Priced in line with the industry. The market sees no special promise or threat. |
| Highest in the industry | The most expensive stock, priced for fast growth or judged the safest in the group. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`P/E = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share`

Share price is the current market price of one share. Earnings per share is the company's net income divided by its share count. The trailing version uses the profit from the most recent twelve months, which is what this view shows. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from lowest to highest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is priced against the same market.

The ratio standardizes price so companies of different sizes can be compared. A higher share price does not mean a more expensive stock, because a high price can sit on top of high profit. Dividing price by earnings strips out size and leaves a clean read on how richly the market values each dollar of profit.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Price to earnings is the most common way to judge which company in an industry is cheap and which is dear. Raw share price says nothing about value, because one company can trade at 200 dollars and another at 20 and the cheaper business can be the one priced at 200. Price to earnings removes the share price's arbitrary level and asks the real question. How much is the market paying for each dollar of profit. That question is what ranks the companies by relative price.

Comparing the multiple across one industry is the heart of relative valuation, the practice of pricing a company against similar companies rather than from scratch. The method is fast and easy to read, which is why equity research leans on it. The method also carries a warning. A low multiple is not automatically a bargain, because the market may be pricing in a real decline. Price to earnings tells the reader where each company stands, not whether the market is right.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is expected growth. A company the market expects to grow profit quickly earns a higher multiple, because buyers pay today for tomorrow's larger profit. The second is risk. A steadier, safer company earns a higher multiple, because its profit is more certain. The third is the profit figure itself. A company with a temporary dip in profit shows a high multiple that overstates how expensive it really is.

A difference can also be an accounting effect rather than a real one. A one-time gain lifts the latest profit and pushes the multiple down, making the stock look cheap for a reason that will not repeat. A one-time charge shrinks profit and pushes the multiple up. A company with very low profit in a weak year can show an enormous multiple that means little, because dividing by a tiny number produces a large and unstable result.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Multiples vary widely across industries, so a multiple of 15 can be cheap in one industry and dear in another. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies share the same growth and risk backdrop and the market prices them on the same terms.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far below the rest | A possible bargain, or a business the market expects to shrink. Check growth before buying the discount. |
| Cluster of similar multiples | The market sees the companies as alike, with no standout winner or loser. |
| One company far above the rest | Priced for fast growth or judged the safest. The price already assumes good news. |

Always check whether a low multiple comes with falling profit or a fading business. A cheap stock attached to a declining company is often a trap rather than a bargain, so the lowest multiple is a starting point for research, not a verdict.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's price to earnings over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that the market has paid up for year after year usually has a quality the others lack, such as durable growth or low risk. A company whose multiple is climbing is one the market is warming to. A company whose multiple is sinking is one the market is leaving behind.

The most useful pattern is a multiple that drifts far from where the company has historically traded against its peers. A company now far cheaper than its own past relative to the industry may be mispriced, or may be facing a real problem. A company now far dearer than its past may be priced for a growth story that has to come true. The history shows whether today's ranking is normal for these companies or a recent shift worth explaining.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Net Income Growth

**Why read the two together.** Price to earnings reflects what the market expects a company's profit to do, and net income growth shows what the profit has actually done. A high multiple is only justified when growth backs it up. Putting the two side by side shows whether the price each company carries is earned by real growth or paid on hope. The pairing turns a price ranking into a value judgment.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high multiple and high growth is priced fairly for its speed, not necessarily overpriced. A company with a high multiple and weak growth is the most exposed in the table, because the price assumes growth the business is not delivering. A company with a low multiple and solid growth is the most interesting find, because the market is paying little for real progress.

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Price to earnings measures how dear a company is, and return on equity measures how good the underlying business is. A great business deserves a higher price, so the two belong together. Reading them as a pair shows whether the most expensive companies are also the best operators, or whether the price has run ahead of the quality.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high return on equity and a moderate multiple is the strongest combination, because the reader buys a top operator without paying a top price. A company with a high multiple but only average return on equity is paying for a reputation the numbers do not support. The best pick is often the company whose quality ranks higher than its price, because the market has not yet fully rewarded the business.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital shows how much profit a company earns on all the money in the business, both equity and debt, so it cannot be flattered by borrowing. Price to earnings shows what the market charges for that profit. The two together separate companies that are expensive because they are excellent from companies that are merely expensive.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on return on invested capital and trades at a fair multiple is a quality business at a reasonable price. A company that trades at a high multiple while earning only an ordinary return on invested capital is priced beyond what the business justifies. The reader can use this pairing to find the company whose strong economics are not yet fully reflected in its price.

## Part 8: Limitations

Price to earnings can rank a declining business as the cheapest in the industry, which tempts the reader into a value trap. A low multiple often signals a real problem the market sees and the reader does not. The ratio also breaks when a company has very low or negative profit, because dividing by a tiny or negative number gives a huge or meaningless figure that distorts the ranking.

The number reflects the past year's profit and today's price, not the future. A multiple can look cheap only because the latest profit was lifted by a one-time gain that will not repeat. The comparative view shows how the market prices each company against its peers, not whether the market is correct, so a low ranking invites research rather than ending it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company is the cheapest in the industry on this metric, and is the low price a bargain or a business the market expects to decline?
2. Does the cheapest company also show steady or rising profit, or is the low multiple paired with falling earnings?
3. Which company is the most expensive, and is the high price backed by faster growth or lower risk than its peers?
4. Does any company show a very high or very low multiple only because its latest profit was distorted by a one-time gain or charge?
5. How does each company's multiple compare with where it has traded against the industry in the past?
6. Are the companies clustered at similar multiples, which would suggest the market sees them as alike, or spread apart, which would suggest a clear winner and loser in the market's eyes?
7. Does the cheapest stock also rank well on business quality, judging by return on equity and return on invested capital?
8. Is the most expensive company priced for growth it is actually delivering, or for growth that still has to arrive?
