# DuPont Interest Burden

## Part 1: Definition

DuPont interest burden measures how much of a company's operating profit survives after interest on its debt is paid. Operating income is the profit a company earns from its core business before interest and tax. Pre-tax income is what remains after interest is subtracted. The interest burden is pre-tax income divided by operating income, so it is the share of operating profit left once lenders are paid. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company loses the least of its operating profit to interest. A company with an interest burden of 0.90 keeps 90 cents of every dollar of operating profit after interest. A direct competitor at 0.60 keeps only 60 cents.

The interest burden is one of the five levers in the DuPont breakdown of return on equity. Return on equity is yearly profit per dollar of owner money, and the DuPont method splits it into five parts that multiply together: tax burden, interest burden, operating profitability, how hard the assets work, and how much debt the company uses. The interest burden is the interest-drag lever. It captures how much of a company's operating profit is consumed by the cost of its borrowing before tax is even considered.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Highest in the industry | Little operating profit lost to interest, usually from low debt or cheap borrowing. The strongest result. |
| Middle of the pack | A typical interest drag for the industry. Debt costs are neither a help nor a heavy burden. |
| Lowest in the industry | A heavy interest drag, from large debt, high rates, or a year of weak operating profit. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Interest Burden = Pre-tax Income ÷ Operating Income`

Operating income is the profit from the core business before interest and tax. Pre-tax income is the profit after interest but before tax. The result is a figure that approaches one when interest costs are small and falls well below one when they are large. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company faces the same rate environment at that time.

This lever is the second financial factor in the five-part DuPont chain. The chain multiplies the tax burden by the interest burden, the operating margin, the asset turnover, and the financial leverage to rebuild the full return on equity. The interest burden sits between the operating profit a company produces and the pre-tax profit it has left. A higher interest burden lifts return on equity because less profit leaks to lenders, while a low one drags the return down even when the business itself is sound.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

DuPont interest burden isolates the part of return on equity that is shaped by debt costs rather than by the business. Two companies in one industry can run equally strong operations yet end with very different returns on equity, because one carries heavy borrowing and loses much of its profit to interest. Splitting out the interest burden shows how much of the gap is financing. It answers a precise question. How much of each company's operating profit is being handed to lenders before owners see anything.

The lever matters because a heavy interest drag is a sign of risk, not just lower profit. A company that loses a large share of operating profit to interest has little cushion if profit falls or rates rise. The same debt that lifts return on equity through the leverage lever also drags it down through the interest lever, so the two must be read together. Comparing this lever across an industry tells the reader which companies keep their operating profit and which are quietly surrendering it to debt.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the amount of debt, since a company with little borrowing pays little interest and keeps almost all of its operating profit. The second is the interest rate it pays, because a company that borrows cheaply, often a stronger or safer firm, loses less than one paying a high rate. The third is the level of operating profit itself, because the same interest bill takes a larger share when operating profit is low.

A difference can also be a temporary effect rather than a structural one. A company part-way through paying down debt can show a low interest burden one year and a heavier one the next, or the reverse. A year of weak operating profit can push the interest burden down sharply, because a fixed interest bill swallows a bigger slice of a smaller profit. A one-time gain that lifts operating profit does the opposite. These swings make a single year's lever an unreliable guide on its own.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Debt loads differ widely across industries, so a heavy interest burden that is normal for a capital-intensive utility would be alarming for a capital-light software firm. Within one industry the companies face similar financing needs, which makes the lever more comparable, and a company that loses far more of its profit to interest than its peers stands out as carrying more risk.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | Little debt or very cheap borrowing. A safe and strong result, worth confirming with the leverage metrics. |
| Cluster of similar burdens | The companies carry similar debt loads, so this lever explains little of the gap in return on equity. |
| One company far below the rest | A heavy interest drag from large or costly debt. A warning sign for a downturn. |

Always read the interest burden together with the leverage lever in this view. A company can lift its return on equity by borrowing more, but the same debt shows up here as a heavier interest drag, so the two levers reveal whether the leverage is helping or hurting on balance.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's interest burden over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that keeps a high interest burden year after year carries light or cheap debt and runs little financing risk. A burden that falls over time signals rising debt or rising rates eating into profit, which weakens the return on equity even if the business is unchanged.

The most useful pattern is a lever that drifts steadily in one direction. An interest burden sliding lower year after year is a warning that debt is building or borrowing is getting dearer. A burden climbing toward one shows a company paying down debt and reclaiming its operating profit. Because interest rates move for whole industries at once, the reader should separate a rate-driven shift that squeezes everyone from a change in one company's own borrowing.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** DuPont interest burden is one of the five levers that multiply together to form return on equity, so it explains part of why one company's return differs from another's. Return on equity is the headline result, and the interest burden shows how much of the operating profit reaches the bottom line after debt costs. Putting the two side by side shows whether a company's return is being quietly drained by interest before owners benefit.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high return on equity and a high interest burden earns its return cleanly, with little lost to debt. A company whose return on equity looks acceptable despite a low interest burden is working hard on the operating side just to overcome its debt costs. The reader can use this pairing to find the company whose return is not propped up by leverage that the interest lever is silently undermining.

### Financial Leverage (Equity Multiplier)

**Why read the two together.** Financial leverage and interest burden are two sides of the same debt. Leverage shows how many dollars of assets each dollar of equity carries, which lifts return on equity, while the interest burden shows the cost of the debt that funds those assets, which lowers it. Reading the two together shows whether a company's borrowing is adding more to the return through leverage than it takes away through interest.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with high leverage but still a strong interest burden is borrowing cheaply and using debt well, so the leverage is a net help. A company with high leverage and a weak interest burden is paying dearly for its debt, so the borrowing may be hurting more than helping. Comparing the two levers across the industry tells the reader whose use of debt is productive and whose is a drag.

### Debt Service Coverage

**Why read the two together.** Interest burden shows the share of operating profit that survives interest, and debt service coverage shows how many times over a company's profit can cover its debt payments. Both measure the weight of debt on a company, from different angles. Reading them together gives a fuller picture of how much room a company has before its borrowing becomes a threat.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high interest burden and strong coverage carries its debt comfortably and would weather a downturn. A company with a low interest burden and thin coverage is stretched, and a fall in profit could leave it unable to pay. Comparing the two across the industry tells the reader which company's debt is safely managed and which company's debt is a risk to its return and its survival.

## Part 8: Limitations

DuPont interest burden can mislead in a single year, because a year of weak operating profit pushes the lever down sharply even when the company's debt has not changed. A one-time gain that lifts operating profit can push it up for the wrong reason. The lever should be read across several years before it is trusted, and it should always be paired with the leverage lever, since debt affects return on equity through both at once.

The lever says nothing about the strength of the core business. A company can keep a high interest burden simply by avoiding debt, even while running a weak operation. The lever also depends on how interest and operating items are classified, which can differ across firms and blur the comparison. The comparative view shows which company loses the least operating profit to interest, not which is the best business or the cheapest stock, so a high rank invites research rather than ending it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company loses the least of its operating profit to interest, and is the light drag from low debt or from cheap borrowing?
2. How much of each company's gap in return on equity is explained by interest costs rather than by the operating business?
3. Does any company show a low interest burden mainly because a weak year shrank its operating profit, rather than because of heavy debt?
4. Does a company's high leverage add more to its return through the leverage lever than it takes away through this interest lever?
5. Has the interest burden drifted lower over several years, signaling debt or rates building up against the company?
6. Are the companies clustered on this lever, which would mean debt costs explain little of the gap in return on equity, or spread apart, which would mean financing is a real differentiator?
7. Which company would best protect its profit if interest rates rose, judging by its current interest drag and debt level?
8. Does the company with the heaviest interest drag also show thin debt service coverage, marking real financing risk?
