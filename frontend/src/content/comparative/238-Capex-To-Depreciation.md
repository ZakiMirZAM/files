# Capex To Depreciation

## Part 1: Definition

Capex to depreciation measures how much a company spends on new long-term assets against how fast its existing assets are wearing out on paper. Capex, short for capital expenditures, is the cash a company spends on factories, equipment, and other long-lived assets. Depreciation is the yearly accounting charge that spreads the cost of those assets across their useful lives, standing in for the amount needed just to replace what is wearing out. A reading above 1 means the company is spending more than it takes to maintain its asset base, so it is growing. A reading below 1 means it is spending less than its assets are aging. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry use similar kinds of assets that wear out at similar rates, so the pace at which each replaces and expands those assets reflects how aggressively it is investing. A higher reading points to a company in a building phase. A lower one points to a company harvesting cash from assets it already owns.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The most aggressive investor, expanding capacity well beyond simple maintenance. |
| Middle of the pack | A company investing at a steady pace, growing its asset base modestly. |
| Bottom of the industry | A company spending below maintenance, either harvesting cash or underinvesting. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Capex To Depreciation = Capital Expenditures ÷ Depreciation`

Capital expenditures come from the cash flow statement and show the year's spending on long-term assets. Depreciation is the year's charge for the aging of existing assets. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is measured against the same point in time.

A reading near 1 suggests the company is spending roughly what it needs to keep its assets intact. A reading well above 1 suggests expansion, since spending exceeds the replacement level. Capital spending can be lumpy, with little spent for years and then a large outlay for a replacement, so a single year can mislead. Reading several years together shows whether capex and depreciation stay aligned or drift apart for a reason worth understanding.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Capex to depreciation is the cleanest single read on which company in an industry is investing for the future and which is living off its past. The two companies may report similar profit today, but the one spending well above maintenance is building the capacity that drives tomorrow's sales, while the one spending below maintenance is quietly shrinking its asset base. Ranking companies on this measure shows where the growth investment is going.

The metric matters most inside one industry, where the companies need similar assets and face similar replacement needs. A company that consistently spends above maintenance is committing to growth, which can pay off or can destroy value if the spending earns poor returns. A company that consistently spends below maintenance is either highly efficient or starving its assets. Comparing the pace across an industry tells the reader which companies are leaning into growth and which are pulling back.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Two forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the stage of investment. A company expanding capacity or entering new markets spends far above maintenance, while a mature company with full capacity spends near or below it. The second is the lumpiness of capital spending, since a single large project can lift one company's reading for a year while a quiet year between projects lowers another's.

A difference can also be an accounting effect rather than a real one. Depreciation depends on how a company values and ages its assets, so a firm with older, heavily depreciated assets shows a low depreciation charge that lifts the ratio even without extra spending. A recent acquisition can mismatch capex and depreciation, because the acquired assets enter at fresh values. For these reasons the reading should be read across several years before it is trusted as a sign of real investment intensity.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Capital intensity varies enormously across industries, so a reading of 2 can be ordinary growth in a heavy industry and aggressive in a light one. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies need similar assets and face similar maintenance demands.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | An aggressive investor expanding capacity, which rewards or punishes depending on the returns it earns. |
| Cluster of similar numbers | An industry where all firms reinvest at a similar steady pace set by the business model. |
| A company far below the rest | A company harvesting cash from existing assets, or one underinvesting and letting its base age. |

Always check whether a high reading is funding profitable growth before crediting it as a strength. Heavy spending that earns poor returns destroys value, so the pace of investment matters less than what the investment produces.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's capex to depreciation over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. Capital spending is lumpy, so a single high year can simply be a large project rather than a lasting commitment to growth. Watching the multi-year average smooths the lumps and shows the company's true investment pace.

The most useful pattern is a company whose reading stays consistently above 1, because sustained spending above maintenance signals a long commitment to expansion. A company whose reading drifts below 1 for several years may be harvesting cash or quietly underinvesting in a way that will limit future sales. The history tells the reader whether a high reading today is a passing project or a durable growth strategy.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation shows how heavily a company is investing relative to maintenance. Free cash flow shows the cash left after that investment is paid for. The two are linked directly, because capex is subtracted in the free cash flow calculation, so heavy investment lowers free cash flow today in the hope of higher cash flow later.

**What the combination tells you.** A company spending well above maintenance with still-strong free cash flow is investing from a position of strength, which is the healthiest combination. A company spending heavily while free cash flow turns thin or negative is stretching, and the payoff must arrive for the bet to work. The pairing shows the reader whether a company's growth investment is comfortably funded or pushing its cash to the limit.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation shows how much a company is investing. Return on invested capital shows what its existing capital already earns. Reading the two together answers the key question about heavy spending, which is whether the company earns good returns on the capital it deploys.

**What the combination tells you.** A company investing aggressively while earning a high return on invested capital is compounding value, because each new dollar of spending should earn the same strong return. A company investing aggressively while earning a low return is destroying value, since it is pouring money into a business that does not pay. The pairing separates growth that builds wealth from growth that burns it.

### Sales To Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation shows how fast a company adds to its capital base. Sales to invested capital shows how much revenue that capital base produces. The two together reveal whether heavy spending is translating into the extra sales it is supposed to fund.

**What the combination tells you.** A company spending above maintenance and lifting its sales per dollar of capital is investing productively, turning new assets into new revenue. A company spending heavily while its sales per dollar of capital stagnates is adding assets that are not yet earning their keep. The reader can use the pairing to judge whether an aggressive investor is building capacity that works.

## Part 8: Limitations

Capex to depreciation can mislead because capital spending is lumpy, so a single year can show a large project that does not reflect the company's normal pace. The reading also depends on the depreciation charge, which is an accounting estimate, so a company with old, heavily depreciated assets can show a high ratio without any real increase in spending. A recent acquisition can mismatch the two figures for reasons unrelated to investment intent.

The number says nothing about the quality of the investment. A high reading shows a company is spending, not that the spending will earn good returns, so heavy investment can be a sign of either ambition or waste. The comparative view shows which company is investing most aggressively relative to maintenance, not which company is investing wisely, so a high ranking is a starting point for research rather than a verdict.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company in the industry is spending most aggressively above maintenance, and is the spending a lasting strategy or a single large project?
2. Does the company investing most heavily also earn a strong return on invested capital, or is it pouring money into a business that does not pay?
3. Is any company's high reading caused by old, heavily depreciated assets that lower the depreciation charge rather than by real extra spending?
4. Which company is spending below maintenance, and is it harvesting cash from a strong base or quietly underinvesting?
5. Is the heavy investor still producing solid free cash flow, or has its spending pushed cash flow thin?
6. Are the companies clustered together, which would suggest a shared reinvestment pace, or spread apart, which would suggest different growth bets?
7. Has the leader's heavy spending shown up yet in rising sales per dollar of capital, or are the new assets not earning their keep?
8. Does the company investing most for the future also have the balance sheet to fund that investment without straining itself?
