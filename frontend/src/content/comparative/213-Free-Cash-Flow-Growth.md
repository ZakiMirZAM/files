# Free Cash Flow Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Free cash flow growth measures how fast the cash a company keeps after running and investing in the business is rising or falling. Free cash flow is the cash left from operations after the company pays for the equipment and facilities it needs, which is the spending called capital expenditure. Growth is the change in that free cash flow over time, shown as a percentage. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company is building its true cash generation fastest. A company with free cash flow growth of 15 percent is producing 15 percent more spare cash than before. A direct competitor at minus 10 percent is producing 10 percent less.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry need similar kinds of investment to operate, so the company whose spare cash is growing fastest is usually converting sales into cash more effectively or spending less to grow. A higher growth rate points to a company strengthening its cash engine. A lower or negative one points to a company whose cash is being eaten by costs, weak collections, or heavy investment.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The fastest-rising cash generation, from better cash conversion or lighter investment needs. |
| Middle of the pack | Cash generation moving in line with the industry. The company is keeping pace. |
| Bottom of the industry | Falling cash generation, from rising costs, heavy investment, or weakening collections. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure`

Operating cash flow is the cash the business produces from its day-to-day operations. Capital expenditure is the cash spent on equipment, facilities, and other long-lived assets. Free cash flow is the difference, and free cash flow growth is the change in that figure over the period. The comparative table computes the growth for each company and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. This view uses an average growth rate across several years, which smooths the sharp swings that a single year can carry.

Free cash flow swings more than profit, because capital expenditure can be lumpy. A company may spend heavily on a new plant one year and little the next, which sends free cash flow down sharply and then up again. Averaging the growth over several years is what makes the comparison fair, because it prevents one big investment year from dominating the ranking and reflects the company's steadier cash trend.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Free cash flow growth is the clearest read on which company in an industry is building real, spendable cash fastest. Profit can be lifted by accounting choices, but free cash flow is the cash actually left over, and rising free cash flow is hard to fake. Ranking companies on free cash flow growth shows which businesses are turning their operations into a growing stream of cash that can fund dividends, buybacks, or debt repayment.

The metric is most useful inside one industry, where companies face similar investment needs. A company that grows free cash flow faster than its peers year after year is improving the engine that ultimately funds shareholder returns. A wide gap between the fastest and slowest grower in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the leader is converting its business into cash more effectively than the rest. The ranking points to which company is strengthening its financial freedom.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is growth in operating cash flow, which rises when sales grow and the company collects from customers promptly. The second is the path of capital expenditure. A company easing its investment after a building phase frees up cash and lifts free cash flow growth. The third is working capital, the cash tied up in inventory and receivables, which can swing free cash flow up or down as it shrinks or grows.

A difference can also be a base effect or a timing effect rather than a real trend. A company recovering from a year of heavy investment shows a large growth rate built on a low starting point, which overstates progress. A company that just began a big expansion shows falling free cash flow that reflects investment for the future, not weakness. Free cash flow is lumpy by nature, so a single period's rank can mislead even with averaging.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Investment needs differ across industries, so a heavy industry naturally shows lumpier and lower free cash flow growth than a light one. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies face similar capital demands and the same growth rate counts as strong or weak for all of them.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A likely improvement in cash conversion or a step down in investment, unless a heavy prior year flatters the rate. |
| Cluster of similar numbers | An industry where companies invest and convert cash on similar terms, with no standout. |
| One company far below the rest | A company in a heavy investment phase, or one whose cash conversion is weakening. |

Always check whether falling free cash flow comes from heavy investment before judging a company as weak. Spending to build future capacity lowers free cash flow today but can lift it later, so a low ranking can hide a company investing for growth rather than one in decline.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's free cash flow growth over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that grows free cash flow faster than its peers across a full cycle has a durable cash engine, because consistent cash growth survives the lumpy years. A single strong year proves far less, because one light investment year can lift free cash flow without any real improvement.

The most useful pattern is steady cash growth across the ups and downs of investment, rather than one spectacular year. A company whose free cash flow rises through both heavy and light investment years is converting its business into cash reliably. A company climbing the ranking as a building phase ends is often about to enter a strong cash period. The history separates a durable cash generator from a company riding a quiet investment year.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Free Cash Flow

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow growth shows how fast the cash is rising, and free cash flow shows the level it is rising from. A high growth rate built on a tiny base means little, while steady growth on a large base is powerful. Reading the two together shows whether a fast-growing company is also a large cash generator. The pairing keeps the growth rate honest by anchoring it to the actual cash produced.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with high free cash flow growth and a large free cash flow base is the strongest in the table, because real cash is both large and growing. A company with high growth but a tiny base is producing little cash despite the impressive percentage. The reader should rank true cash strength by reading the growth rate alongside the dollar level it grows from.

### Operating Cash Flow Growth

**Why read the two together.** Operating cash flow growth shows how fast the cash from daily operations is rising, before investment is subtracted. Free cash flow growth shows what is left after investment. Reading the two together shows whether a company's spare cash is growing because the business is stronger or only because it cut back on investment. The pairing reveals the source of the free cash flow growth.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose free cash flow growth and operating cash flow growth rise together is building cash from a genuinely stronger business. A company whose free cash flow is growing only because operating cash flow is flat and investment fell is improving the headline without strengthening the engine. The reader can use the pairing to favor companies whose cash growth comes from real operating gains rather than from underinvestment.

### Capex To Depreciation

**Why read the two together.** Capex to depreciation shows whether a company is investing more than it is wearing out its assets or letting them run down. Free cash flow growth rises when investment falls, so this pairing checks whether rising free cash flow is healthy or borrowed from the future. Reading them together separates efficient cash generation from underinvestment that will eventually hurt. The pairing guards against rewarding a company that is starving its asset base.

**What the combination tells you.** A company growing free cash flow while still investing enough to maintain its assets is generating cash the right way. A company whose free cash flow is rising only because it has cut investment below what it needs to maintain its assets is mortgaging its future. The reader can use the pairing to tell durable cash growth from a short-term boost that comes at the cost of tomorrow's capacity.

## Part 8: Limitations

Free cash flow growth can rank a company that simply paused investment above one that is spending to grow, because cutting capital expenditure lifts free cash flow in the short term. The comparison also misleads on a low base, since a company recovering from a heavy investment year shows a huge percentage that overstates real progress. Free cash flow is lumpy by nature, so even an averaged growth rate can be shaped by the timing of large projects.

The number measures the change in cash, not the level or the reason behind it. A company can grow free cash flow fast by underinvesting, which weakens it over time, so a top growth ranking is not a verdict on quality. The comparative view shows whose spare cash is rising fastest, not which company is the best business or the cheapest to buy, so a high ranking is a starting point for research rather than a conclusion.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company is growing free cash flow fastest in the industry, and is the growth from stronger operations or from a step down in investment?
2. Does the fastest grower also generate a large amount of free cash flow, or is the high rate built on a tiny base?
3. Is any company's high growth rate inflated by a recovery from a heavy investment year?
4. Does a company's falling free cash flow reflect heavy investment for future growth rather than a weakening business?
5. Has the leader grown free cash flow faster than its peers across a full cycle, or only in one quiet investment year?
6. Are the companies growing together, which would suggest shared conditions, or spread apart, which would suggest one company converts cash far better?
7. Is a company's free cash flow growth coming at the cost of underinvestment that will hurt its assets later?
8. Does the fastest grower's operating cash flow growth confirm the gain, or is the free cash flow rising only because investment was cut?
