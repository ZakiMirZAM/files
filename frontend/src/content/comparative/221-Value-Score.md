# Value Score

## Part 1: Definition

Value score is a single number from 0 to 100 that ranks a company against the other large companies in its industry. It is not a price or a value in dollars. It is a screening rank, built by combining many of the leaderboard metrics into one figure so the reader can sort an industry from strongest to weakest at a glance. A higher score places a company higher in the industry. A company near 100 ranks at the top across the blended metrics, and a company near 0 ranks at the bottom.

The score answers a sorting question, not a pricing question. It does not say a company is worth a certain amount or trades at a certain discount. It says how a company stacks up against its peers once profitability, growth, safety, and cheapness are weighed together. The comparative view shows the score side by side for every company in one industry, with the highest score at the top of the leaderboard.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Near the top of the industry | Strong across the blended metrics. A good first place to look, not a finished verdict. |
| Middle of the pack | Mixed results, strong on some metrics and weak on others. Typical for the industry. |
| Near the bottom | Weak across most of the blended metrics, or weighed down by a few poor readings. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The score is built by ranking every company in the industry on each chosen metric, then blending those ranks. For each metric, the model places the companies in order and converts each company's position into a percentile, a figure from 0 to 100 that shows where it sits in the line. For metrics where lower is better, such as debt, the order is reversed so that the safer company still scores higher. The percentiles are then combined using fixed weights, and the weighted average becomes the company's value score.

The blend mixes several kinds of metric. Profitability and returns carry the most weight, cheapness and growth carry medium weight, and safety and leverage carry lighter weight. Because the score is built from ranks within the industry, it always spreads the companies across the range, and it only has meaning against the peers in the same industry. A score of 80 in one industry and a score of 80 in another are not the same thing, because each is measured against a different set of companies.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Value score exists to turn many separate metrics into one ordering. An industry leaderboard can show a dozen columns, and no company leads on all of them. The score weighs the columns together so the reader can rank the whole industry without judging each metric by hand. It is the fastest way to see which companies deserve a closer look and which can be set aside.

The metric is a screen, not a valuation. It does not estimate what a company is worth or whether the stock is cheap in dollars. It sorts companies by a blend of quality, growth, safety, and cheapness, and the top of that sort is where research should begin. Treating the score as a starting filter rather than a final answer is the right way to use it, because the single number hides the detail in the columns beneath it.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

A company's score rises when it ranks well on the heavily weighted metrics and falls when it ranks poorly on them. Because returns and profitability carry the most weight, a company that earns high returns on its capital tends to score well even if it is weaker elsewhere. Because the blend includes cheapness, a company priced at a discount can lift its score, and an expensive company can drag it down despite strong operations.

The gap between two companies often comes from a small number of metrics rather than all of them. One company may lead on growth and cheapness while trailing on safety, and the weights decide which pull wins. A company can also score poorly for a passing reason, such as a single weak year of growth, even though the rest of its profile is sound. The single number cannot show which metric drove the result, so the columns underneath always carry more detail than the score alone.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. The score is built from ranks within one industry, so it has no meaning across industries. A high score means a company ranks above its own peers, not that it beats companies in a different industry. Within one industry the comparison is fair because every company is ranked against the same set of rivals on the same metrics.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A broad leader across the blended metrics. Confirm by reading the columns that drove the lead. |
| A tight cluster of scores | The companies are evenly matched. The score alone will not separate them, so the detail metrics matter more. |
| A high score built on one strong column | A narrow lead that depends on a single metric. Check whether the strength is durable. |

Always open the columns behind a high score before trusting it. The score is a summary, and two companies with the same score can have very different strengths and weaknesses underneath.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the score the way a leaderboard moves over time. The score itself reflects the latest year, so the most useful comparison is how the latest scores spread across the industry and how each company's standing has shifted from earlier readings. A company that has climbed the ranking over several years is improving against its peers on the blended metrics. A company that has slipped is losing ground, even if its own numbers look steady, because the others improved faster.

Compare the latest ranking against the picture from past years to judge durability. A company that has held the top of the industry across several readings has a more convincing claim than one that jumped to the top in a single year. A company rising steadily through the ranking is often a better forward-looking choice than the current leader. The pattern to watch is whether the order of the industry is stable or whether the companies are changing places.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity is one of the most heavily weighted inputs to the value score, so a company that ranks well on returns tends to rank well overall. Reading the two together shows how much of a company's score comes from its profitability rather than from the other metrics. It opens up one of the largest pieces hidden inside the single number.

**What the combination tells you.** When a company leads on both the score and return on equity, its high rank is anchored in genuine profitability. When a company leads on the score but ranks only average on return on equity, the high rank is coming from cheapness, growth, or safety instead. Comparing the two across the industry shows whether the top of the leaderboard is led by the best operators or by the cheapest stocks.

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** Margin of safety is the cheapness input to the value score, and it carries meaningful weight in the blend. Reading the two together shows how much of a company's rank comes from being priced at a discount rather than from business quality. It separates the cheap part of the score from the quality part.

**What the combination tells you.** A high score paired with a wide margin of safety means the company is both well ranked and cheap, which is the strongest combination on the leaderboard. A high score paired with a narrow margin means the rank is built on quality and growth while the stock is already fully priced. Comparing both across the industry shows which top-ranked companies are bargains and which are excellent but expensive.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is another heavily weighted input to the score, and it measures returns without the distortion that debt can add. Reading it next to the score shows whether a company's high rank rests on real operating returns rather than on borrowing. It tests the quality behind the summary number.

**What the combination tells you.** When a company ranks high on both the score and return on invested capital, the rank is supported by returns the business earns on all of its capital. When the score is high but return on invested capital is only average, the rank may lean on cheapness or on debt-flattered figures. Comparing the two across the industry shows which leaders earn their place through durable returns.

## Part 8: Limitations

Value score is a ranking, not a valuation, and it can mislead if read as one. It never says what a company is worth or whether the whole industry is cheap. Because it is built from ranks within one industry, it always produces a leader even when every company is weak, so a high score in a poor industry is still a poor result in absolute terms. The single number also hides the columns beneath it, so two companies with the same score can be opposite in their strengths.

The weights are fixed, so the score reflects one fixed view of what matters, and it will not suit every reader or every industry. A company can be pushed down by a single weak metric that the reader does not care about, or lifted by a metric that does not fit the industry. The score is best used as a first filter that points to companies worth studying, not as the final word on which company to own.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company has the highest value score in the industry, and which columns underneath the score drove the lead?
2. Does the top-ranked company lead on the heavily weighted quality metrics, or does its rank come mainly from cheapness?
3. Are the scores tightly clustered, meaning the companies are evenly matched, or spread apart, meaning one company stands out?
4. Is a company's high score broad across many metrics, or narrow and dependent on a single strong column?
5. How has the ranking moved over several years? Which company is climbing and which is slipping against its peers?
6. Does the top score belong to the best business, the cheapest stock, or a company that balances both?
7. Is any company pushed down the ranking by one weak metric that may not matter for this industry?
8. Even the top-ranked company sits in one industry, so how attractive is the industry itself before its leader is chosen?
