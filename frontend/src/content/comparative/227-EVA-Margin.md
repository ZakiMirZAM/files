# EVA Margin

## Part 1: Definition

EVA margin measures how much economic profit a company earns for each dollar of sales. EVA stands for economic value added, the profit left after a company is charged for every dollar of capital it uses. The margin states that economic profit as a share of revenue, so a company with an EVA margin of 4 percent earns 4 cents of economic profit on every sales dollar. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company turns sales into value most effectively, regardless of how large each company is.

The side-by-side view carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing and the margin removes size from the picture. EVA in dollars rewards the largest company, but EVA margin states the result per dollar of sales, so a small company and a large one can be judged on the same footing. A positive EVA margin means the company earns a surplus on each sale after covering both operating costs and the cost of its capital. It consolidates operating efficiency and asset management into one net score.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| High positive EVA margin | The company earns a rich economic profit on each sale. The strongest operator in the industry. |
| EVA margin near zero | The company barely clears its capital cost on each sale. Typical of a commodity business. |
| Negative EVA margin | The company loses value on each sale once the cost of capital is counted. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`EVA Margin = EVA ÷ Revenue`

EVA is economic value added, the operating profit after tax minus a charge for the capital the business uses. Revenue is total sales for the year. Dividing the first by the second states the economic profit as a percentage of sales. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is measured against the same standard at the same time.

The margin standardizes economic profit so companies of different sizes can be compared. A large company can post a big EVA in dollars yet a thin EVA margin, which means its surplus is large only because its sales are large. A smaller company with a higher EVA margin earns more value on each dollar it sells. The margin captures the same value creation as EVA but strips out the effect of scale, so it reads as a measure of business quality rather than of size.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

EVA margin is the cleanest single measure of which company in an industry creates the most value per sale. EVA in dollars favors the biggest company, so the largest firm usually shows the largest EVA. EVA margin removes size from the picture and asks how much economic profit each company wrings from each sales dollar. That question separates a great operator from a merely large one, in the same way return on equity separates a great business from a large one.

The margin matters because it ties value creation to the income statement that operating managers actually run, while still charging for the balance sheet. A company can grow sales and report a wider ordinary margin yet show a falling EVA margin if it ties up too much capital to do it. Comparing the margin across an industry shows which company runs the most productive business model, blending pricing power, cost control, and lean use of capital into one number.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the operating margin, the profit kept on each sale before the capital charge. The second is capital intensity, how much capital the company ties up for each dollar of sales, because a leaner business carries a smaller capital charge per sale. The third is the cost of capital, since a riskier company is charged more for the capital it uses, which thins its EVA margin even when its operating margin is strong.

A difference can also come from the model rather than the business. Heavy reinvestment raises invested capital and the capital charge before the new sales arrive, which depresses the margin for a time. A recent write-down shrinks invested capital and lifts the margin without any real improvement. A company that leans on suppliers for interest-free credit needs less capital of its own, which lowers its capital charge and widens its EVA margin. These effects can move the margin without changing how well the company actually sells.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Capital intensity and operating margins vary widely across industries, so an EVA margin of 3 percent can be strong in a heavy industry and weak in a capital-light one. Within one industry the companies share the same economics, so the margin reads as a clean rank of business quality. The margin is the right tool for ranking, because it already removes the size effect that distorts the dollar figure.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | The most productive business model, blending strong margins and lean capital. Check that the edge is durable. |
| Cluster of similar margins | A commodity-like industry where no company runs a clearly better model. |
| A leader whose margin is shrinking | Competitors are catching up, or the leader is tying up more capital to hold its sales. |

Always check whether a high EVA margin comes with low capital intensity or with a low cost of capital. A margin earned by running lean is more durable than the same margin handed over by a gentle hurdle rate.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's EVA margin over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that holds a high EVA margin for many years runs a genuinely productive business model, because it keeps earning a surplus per sale while rivals fade. A single strong year proves far less, because one good year can come from a temporary boom or a one-time gain.

The most useful pattern is a steadily rising EVA margin, which means the company is widening its operating margin, running leaner on capital, or both. A company whose margin drifts toward zero is being squeezed, losing pricing power or tying up more capital to defend its sales. A company climbing the ranking steadily is often a better forward-looking pick than the current leader, because an improving business model tends to keep improving. Watching how the ranking shifts shows which advantages are durable and which are fading.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** EVA margin states economic profit per dollar of sales, while EVA states the same profit in dollars. The EVA column shows the absolute size of the surplus, and the margin shows its richness per sale. Reading them together separates how much value a company creates in total from how efficiently it creates value on each sale.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on both is a large and efficient value creator, because its surplus is big in dollars and rich per sale. A company high on EVA but low on EVA margin owes its dollar lead to its size, not its model. A small company with a high EVA margin is the more efficient operator and often the better forward-looking pick, because its model would create more value if it grew.

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The operating income ratio is the profit kept on each sale before any charge for capital, and EVA margin takes that operating margin and subtracts the capital charge per sale. The operating ratio shows the income statement, and the gap down to the EVA margin shows the weight of the balance sheet. Reading them together shows how much capital the company ties up to earn its operating profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high operating margin and a similarly high EVA margin runs a capital-light model, because little is lost to the capital charge. A company with a strong operating margin but a thin EVA margin ties up heavy capital, so its income statement flatters a business that the balance sheet drags down. The company whose EVA margin stays close to its operating margin is usually the leaner and stronger operator.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Return on invested capital is the return earned on each dollar of capital, and EVA margin is the surplus earned on each dollar of sales. Both rest on the same spread between returns and the cost of capital, viewed from different denominators. Reading them together shows whether a rich margin comes from a high return on capital or from a particular sales mix.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on both earns a wide spread and converts it efficiently into sales, which is the strongest result. A company with a high EVA margin but only an ordinary return on invested capital may be turning capital slowly, so its per-sale surplus rests on heavy capital rather than on a high return. Comparing the two shows which company's value creation is rooted in genuinely productive capital.

## Part 8: Limitations

EVA margin depends on the measure of invested capital and the cost of capital, both of which can distort the figure. A past write-down shrinks invested capital and lifts the margin without any real improvement, and a small change in the cost of capital can swing the margin from positive to negative. The margin can also be unstable when sales fall sharply in a weak year, because the capital charge stays while the denominator shrinks, which deepens a negative reading.

The margin fits some industries poorly, especially banks and property companies, where the economic profit model is not the right tool, and the figure may be missing for them. The number reflects the past year, not the future, and a rich margin can rest on a product cycle about to fade. The comparative view shows which company runs the most productive model, not which stock is cheapest to buy, so a high ranking is a reason to study durability rather than a verdict on the share.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company leads the industry on EVA margin, and is the lead built on a strong operating margin or on lean use of capital?
2. Does the EVA margin leader also rank well on EVA in dollars, or is it a smaller company whose model is efficient but not yet large?
3. Which companies show a negative EVA margin, meaning they lose value on each sale once the cost of capital is counted?
4. Does any company show a high margin only because a recent write-down shrank its invested capital?
5. How has each company's EVA margin trended over several years, and is the leader holding its edge or being caught?
6. Are the companies clustered near zero, which would suggest a commodity industry, or spread apart, which would suggest one company runs a better model?
7. Does the company earn its margin over a demanding cost of capital, or does a low hurdle flatter the result?
8. Which company is climbing the ranking fastest, and what in the business model explains the climb?
