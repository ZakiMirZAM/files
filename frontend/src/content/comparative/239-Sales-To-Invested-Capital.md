# Sales To Invested Capital

## Part 1: Definition

Sales to invested capital measures how much revenue a company produces from each dollar of capital invested in the business. Invested capital is the money actually put to work running the company, the equity plus the debt, set apart from spare cash and unrelated assets. The ratio shows how hard that working capital is pulling. A company with a sales to invested capital of 3 generates 3 dollars of revenue a year for every dollar of capital invested. A direct competitor at 1 generates only 1 dollar on the same capital. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company is most productive with the capital it commits.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry need similar capital to compete, so the revenue each draws from that capital reflects how efficiently it deploys money. A higher reading points to a capital-productive operation that needs little to generate a lot. A lower one points to a capital-hungry operation that ties up more money for the same sales.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The most capital-productive operator, turning a small capital base into large sales. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical capital productivity for the industry. The business is neither lean nor heavy. |
| Bottom of the industry | A capital-hungry operator, or one part-way through investing capital that has not yet produced sales. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Sales To Invested Capital = Revenue ÷ Invested Capital`

Revenue is the company's total sales for the year. Invested capital is the capital put to work in the business, generally equity plus debt, with spare cash and unrelated assets set aside. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is measured against the same point in time.

This ratio is a close cousin of asset turnover, which measures sales against all assets. Sales to invested capital narrows the denominator to the capital actively invested, which strips out idle cash and assets unrelated to operations. The narrower base makes it a cleaner read on how productively a company uses the money it has committed to the business, rather than a read distorted by a large cash pile.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Sales to invested capital is the cleanest single read on which company in an industry gets the most revenue from the least committed capital. Two companies can produce the same sales, but the one that needs less invested capital to do it is the more efficient deployer of money. That efficiency frees up capital and lifts returns, so ranking companies on this measure shows which operator stretches each dollar of capital furthest.

The metric matters most inside one industry, where the companies need similar capital to compete. A company that holds a high reading year after year is using its plants, working capital, and equipment more productively than the rest. A wide gap between the most and least capital-productive company in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the leaner company is doing something with its capital the others have not matched.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the business model. A company that rents capacity or outsources production commits less capital and shows a higher reading than a peer that owns everything. The second is how well the company manages working capital, since lean inventory and fast collections free up capital and lift the ratio. The third is the stage of investment, because a company that just deployed capital into a new project carries the full capital cost before the sales it will produce have arrived.

A difference can also be an accounting effect rather than a real one. A company that recently bought another firm carries the acquired capital at fresh, high values, which lowers its reading. A company with older assets carried at low book values shows a higher reading even if the two run equally well. A recent write-down shrinks invested capital and lifts the ratio for a reason unrelated to productivity, so a single year should be read with care.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Capital productivity varies enormously across industries, so a reading of 1 can be normal in a heavy industry and weak in a light one. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies need similar capital to compete and the same level counts as lean or heavy for all of them.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A likely capital-efficiency edge, unless it leans on rented capacity that keeps capital off its own books. |
| Cluster of similar numbers | An industry where the capital base is fixed by the business model and no company has a real edge. |
| A company far below the rest | A capital-hungry operator, or one mid-way through deploying capital whose sales have not yet arrived. |

Always check whether a low reading comes from recent investment before judging the company as inefficient. New capital drags the ratio down for a year or two before the sales it supports arrive, so a temporary low can hide a strong business in build-out.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's sales to invested capital over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that stays at the top of its industry for many years has a durable capital-productivity advantage. A single strong year proves less, because a quiet year for investment can lift the ratio without any real improvement in how the company runs.

The most useful pattern is a company whose reading is climbing steadily, because rising sales per dollar of capital often means the company is filling capacity it already built. A company whose reading is falling may be adding capital faster than sales, which can be healthy investment or wasteful spending. The history tells the reader whether a low ranking today is a permanent trait or a passing phase of building.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Asset Turnover

**Why read the two together.** Asset turnover measures sales against all assets, while sales to invested capital measures sales against the capital actively invested in the business. The two answer the same question from slightly different angles, so reading both confirms whether a company's efficiency edge is real. The pairing guards against a turnover figure distorted by large cash piles or unusual assets.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on both has a genuine, well-grounded efficiency advantage that holds up however the base is measured. A company that ranks high on one but low on the other has a figure shaped by its balance-sheet mix rather than by true productivity. The reader should trust an efficiency claim most when both measures agree.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** Sales to invested capital shows how much revenue the invested capital produces. Return on invested capital shows how much profit that same capital earns. One measures volume per dollar of capital, the other measures profit per dollar of capital, so reading them together shows whether productive capital is also profitable capital.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on both turns its capital into sales and those sales into strong returns, which is the healthiest combination. A company high on sales per dollar of capital but low on return is generating volume without profit, so its efficiency is not paying off. The pairing separates companies that are merely busy with their capital from companies that are genuinely rewarded for it.

### Net Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Net income ratio is the profit margin, the share of each sales dollar kept as profit. Sales to invested capital is how many sales dollars the committed capital produces. A company can win either by keeping more per sale or by making more sales per dollar of capital, so putting them side by side shows which path each company has chosen.

**What the combination tells you.** A company can rank low on margin yet still earn strong returns if its sales per dollar of capital are high, because volume makes up for thin profit per sale. A company with high margin and low capital productivity earns its returns the opposite way, through pricing rather than volume. The most powerful business ranks well on both, and a company weak on both is the one to question.

## Part 8: Limitations

Sales to invested capital can rank a company as inefficient when it is simply newer, because fresh capital carries full value before it produces sales. The comparison also misleads when companies use different business models, since a firm that rents capacity shows a high reading that reflects its choices rather than superior management. A recent acquisition or write-down can move the ranking for accounting reasons unrelated to how the business runs.

The number says nothing about profit. A company can produce huge sales per dollar of capital and still lose money if its margins are thin, so a top ranking is not a verdict on quality. The comparative view shows which company is most productive with its capital, not which company is most profitable or cheapest to buy, so a high ranking is one input among several.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company leads the industry on this metric, and is the lead built on genuine productivity or on a business model that keeps capital off its own books?
2. Does the most capital-productive company also earn a strong return on invested capital, or does it win on volume without profit?
3. Is any company's low ranking explained by recent investment whose new capital has not yet produced sales?
4. Has the leader held its top position for several years, or is the ranking unstable from year to year?
5. Which company is climbing the ranking steadily, and is rising productivity a sign it is filling capacity it already built?
6. Are the companies clustered together, which would suggest the capital base is fixed by the industry, or spread apart, which would suggest one company runs much leaner?
7. Does a company's high reading come partly from old assets carried at low book values rather than from real efficiency?
8. Does the most capital-productive company also keep healthy margins, or does it generate sales without keeping much profit per sale?
