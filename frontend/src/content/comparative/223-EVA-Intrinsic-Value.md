# EVA Intrinsic Value

## Part 1: Definition

EVA intrinsic value is an estimate of what one share of a company is worth, based on the profit the business earns above the cost of the money it uses. EVA stands for economic value added, which is the profit left after a company is charged for every dollar of capital it employs. A business that earns more than its capital costs creates value, and a business that earns less destroys it. This method values a company as its invested capital plus the present worth of all the economic profit it is expected to add. The comparative view shows this fair value per share side by side for every large company in one industry.

The estimate is a model's view of value, not a market price. It rests on a simple idea, that a company is worth what it has invested plus the extra value it earns on top of its cost of capital. A company that consistently earns above its capital cost will have an EVA intrinsic value well above its invested capital. A company that only covers its capital cost adds nothing, so its estimated value sits close to the capital itself.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| High EVA value vs its price | The model sees a business that adds value cheaply priced. A possible bargain. |
| EVA value near its price | The market and the model roughly agree on what the share is worth. |
| Low EVA value vs its price | The model sees little value added, or a price that has run ahead of it. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`EVA Intrinsic Value = Invested Capital + Present Value of Future Economic Profit`

Economic profit for a year is the company's after-tax operating profit minus a charge for the capital it uses. The charge is the amount of invested capital multiplied by the required rate of return on that capital. The model projects this economic profit forward in two stages. In the first stage it forecasts the profit year by year over an explicit period, discounting each year back to today. In the second stage it captures the years beyond, treating the economic profit as level and discounting that stream back as well. The present worth of both stages is added to the invested capital and divided by the share count.

The required rate of return is the company's cost of capital, the blended yearly return its lenders and owners expect. The growth behind the profit is fit from the company's own history and capped, so the estimate does not swing on a single year. The terminal stage assumes new investment adds no further economic profit, a cautious choice that keeps the estimate grounded. Because every company is valued with the same two-stage method at the same moment, the estimates can be ranked fairly against each other and against price. The method is described here in plain words, and the settings are kept conservative for comparability.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

EVA intrinsic value gives the reader a value to set against price, built on the question of whether a business earns more than its capital costs. That question is the cleanest test of whether a company creates value at all. A company can grow and report profit while still destroying value if it earns less than the cost of the money it uses. This estimate captures that, and placed next to price it shows which companies create value cheaply and which are priced beyond the value they add.

The estimate matters because it ties value directly to the spread between returns and the cost of capital, which is the core of what makes a business worth more than the cash put into it. A simple earnings figure ignores that capital has a cost. The economic profit approach charges for it, so the estimate rewards companies that earn a true surplus. Comparing the estimate across an industry shows which company turns its capital into the most value for each dollar of price.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces drive the gap between two companies. The first is the spread between the return on capital and the cost of capital, since a wider spread means more economic profit and a higher estimate. The second is the amount of invested capital, since a larger base of capital earning a positive spread produces more value in dollars. The third is the cost of capital itself, since a riskier company is charged more for its capital, which lowers its economic profit and its estimated value.

The estimate can also differ for reasons rooted in the model rather than the business. A company with a steady history of earning above its capital cost gives the two-stage forecast a firm footing, while a company whose returns hover near its cost of capital adds almost no value and lands near its invested capital. A company in heavy reinvestment may show a thin spread now that the model treats cautiously. The cap on growth and the level terminal stage keep any single company from looking far better than its peers on one strong year.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare the EVA intrinsic value to each company's own price, not the dollar figures against each other. A larger estimate in dollars does not mean a better deal, because invested capital, share counts, and prices all differ. The useful comparison is the size of each company's estimate relative to its price. Within one industry the two-stage method is applied the same way, so the value-to-price gaps can be ranked fairly across the companies.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| EVA value far above price | The model sees value added at a discount. Check that the return spread is durable. |
| EVA value close to price | The market and model agree. No clear edge from this metric. |
| EVA value near invested capital | The company earns little above its capital cost, so it adds little value. |

Always check whether the return spread that lifts a high estimate is durable. A wide spread that competition is likely to erode will not support the value for long.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's EVA intrinsic value over several years, so the reader can watch the estimate move alongside the price. A company whose estimate rises is widening the spread between its returns and its capital cost, which is the engine of value creation. A company whose estimate drifts toward its invested capital is losing that spread, which means it is adding less value each year. Watching the estimate against the price shows whether a discount is opening or closing.

The most useful pattern is an estimate that climbs steadily while the price lags, because that gap is a discount built on a business that keeps earning above its capital cost. A company whose estimate keeps sliding toward its invested capital is being squeezed, and a high current reading may not hold. Comparing how each company's estimate has trended against its price shows which valuations rest on a durable return spread and which on a fading one.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DCF Intrinsic Value

**Why read the two together.** EVA intrinsic value and DCF intrinsic value are two methods aimed at the same fair value per share. One discounts the profit earned above the cost of capital, and the other discounts the free cash flow itself. Reading them together checks one estimate against an independent second estimate built on different inputs.

**What the combination tells you.** When the two methods land near each other, the fair value is more reliable, because two routes agree on the answer. When they diverge sharply, the value rests on an assumption that one method is especially sensitive to, and the figure deserves caution. Comparing both across the industry shows which companies have valuations that hold up under more than one method and which depend on a single approach.

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** EVA intrinsic value is built on the stream of economic profit, and the EVA column shows that economic profit for the latest year directly. Reading them together connects the valuation to the single year of profit that feeds it, so the reader can see whether the estimate rests on profit the company is actually earning now.

**What the combination tells you.** A high EVA intrinsic value backed by strong current economic profit is well supported, because the value rests on results already in hand. A high estimate paired with weak current economic profit leans on a forecast of future improvement that may not come. Comparing the two across the industry shows which valuations are grounded in present value creation and which depend on a hopeful path.

### WACC

**Why read the two together.** The capital charge inside economic profit uses the cost of capital, and a higher cost lowers the economic profit and the estimate. The WACC column shows that cost of capital for each company, so the reader can see the hurdle each business must clear to add value. It exposes the input that sets the bar for the whole calculation.

**What the combination tells you.** A high EVA intrinsic value earned despite a high cost of capital is the strongest result, because the business adds value over a steep hurdle. A high estimate paired with a low cost of capital may flatter a business that only looks good because its hurdle is low. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies create value over a demanding bar and which benefit mainly from a forgiving one.

## Part 8: Limitations

EVA intrinsic value is an estimate, and it depends on assumptions about future returns, growth, and the cost of capital. A change in the cost of capital alone can move the estimate sharply, because that figure sets the capital charge in every year. The method also leans on the measure of invested capital, which accounting can distort through past write-downs and buybacks. The estimate fits some industries poorly, especially banks and property companies, where the economic profit model is not the right tool, and the figure may be missing for them.

The estimate also assumes the company can keep earning above its capital cost, which competition tends to erode over time. A wide return spread today may narrow, and the model's value would narrow with it. Ranking the estimates shows which stocks the model judges undervalued, not which are sure to rise. A high reading is a reason to study the durability of the company's return spread, not a promise that the share will reach the estimated value.
