# DuPont EBIT Margin

## Part 1: Definition

DuPont EBIT margin measures how much operating profit a company earns from each dollar of sales. Operating income, also called EBIT, is the profit a company makes from its core business before interest and tax are taken out. Revenue is the company's total sales. The EBIT margin is operating income divided by revenue, so it is the share of each sales dollar the company keeps as operating profit. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company runs the most profitable core operation. A company with an EBIT margin of 25 percent keeps 25 cents of operating profit from every sales dollar. A direct competitor at 10 percent keeps only 10 cents.

The EBIT margin is one of the five levers in the DuPont breakdown of return on equity. Return on equity is yearly profit per dollar of owner money, and the DuPont method splits it into five parts that multiply together: tax burden, interest burden, operating profitability, how hard the assets work, and how much debt the company uses. The EBIT margin is the operating-profitability lever. It captures how efficiently the company turns sales into core profit, before financing and tax enter the picture, and it is the lever that most directly reflects the quality of the business itself.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Highest in the industry | The most profitable core operation, often from pricing power or tight cost control. The strongest lever. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical operating economics for the industry. The business is neither advantaged nor weak. |
| Lowest in the industry | A weaker operation, from low prices, high costs, or a heavy investment phase. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`EBIT Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue`

Operating income is the profit from the core business before interest and tax. Revenue is total sales. The result is a percentage, and a more profitable operation shows a higher one. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company is measured on the same terms at the same time.

This lever is the operating factor in the five-part DuPont chain. The chain multiplies the tax burden by the interest burden, the operating margin, the asset turnover, and the financial leverage to rebuild the full return on equity. The EBIT margin sits at the heart of the chain, because it reflects the business itself rather than its financing or its tax position. A higher EBIT margin lifts return on equity directly, and it is the lever that most cleanly separates a strong operator from a weak one.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

DuPont EBIT margin isolates the part of return on equity that comes from running the core business well. Two companies in one industry can post the same return on equity while one earns it from a genuinely profitable operation and the other from heavy debt or a light tax bill. Splitting out the EBIT margin shows which company's return rests on the business. It answers a precise question. How much operating profit does each company squeeze from every dollar of sales.

The lever matters because operating profitability is the most durable and repeatable source of a strong return. A high EBIT margin held year after year usually points to a real advantage, such as pricing power, a strong brand, or a lower cost base than rivals. That kind of advantage is often called a moat, something competitors cannot easily copy. A wide gap between the best and worst operator in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the leader is producing more profit from the same sales than the others can.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is pricing power, the ability to charge more without losing customers, which lifts the margin directly. The second is the cost base, since a company that produces or operates more cheaply keeps more of each sales dollar. The third is the mix of products or services, because a company weighted toward higher-margin lines earns a wider operating margin than one selling mostly low-margin goods.

A difference can also be temporary rather than structural. A company part-way through a large expansion can show a low EBIT margin because new costs have arrived before the sales they will support. A one-time charge can depress the margin for a single year, and a one-time gain can flatter it. A company in a cyclical downturn can show a thin margin that will widen again when demand recovers. These swings make a single year's margin an imperfect guide on its own.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Operating margins vary widely across industries, so a margin that is excellent for a grocer would be poor for a software firm. A high margin in a naturally high-margin industry and a moderate one in a thin-margin industry are not the same achievement, so cross-industry comparison is misleading. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies share the same cost structure and the same pricing environment.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A likely operating advantage, such as pricing power or a lower cost base. A real moat signal. |
| Cluster of similar margins | A commodity-like industry where no company has a real edge in operating profitability. |
| A leader whose margin is shrinking | Competitors are catching up, or pricing power is fading. |

Always check whether a high EBIT margin is structural before crediting it as a lasting advantage. A wide margin in a single boom year, or one flattered by a one-time gain, will not support the return on equity the way a steady margin built on pricing power can.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's EBIT margin over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that holds the top operating margin in its industry for many years has the most convincing claim to a durable advantage. A single strong year proves far less, because one good year can come from a temporary boom or a one-time gain rather than a real edge.

The most useful pattern is a widening or narrowing gap between the leader and the rest. A leader holding its margin while rivals slip is defending real pricing power or a cost advantage. A leader whose margin is converging with the pack is losing the edge that set it apart. Because operating margins move with the business cycle, the reader should separate a cyclical swing that lifts or squeezes the whole industry from a structural change that helps or hurts one company alone.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Return On Equity

**Why read the two together.** DuPont EBIT margin is one of the five levers that multiply together to form return on equity, and it is the one that reflects the business itself. Return on equity is the headline result, and the EBIT margin shows how much of that result comes from a genuinely profitable operation rather than from leverage or tax. Putting the two side by side shows whether a company's return is earned by the core business or manufactured by financing.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high return on equity and a high EBIT margin earns its lead from operating strength, which is the most durable result. A company whose return on equity ranks high while its EBIT margin is only average is leaning on debt or tax to reach its return. The reader can use this pairing to find the company whose return rests on a strong operation rather than on financial engineering.

### Asset Turnover

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin and asset turnover are two operating levers that often trade off against each other. The margin shows how much profit a company keeps from each sale, and asset turnover shows how much sales it generates from its assets. A company can earn a strong return through a high margin on slow-moving assets, or through a thin margin on fast-moving ones. Reading the two together shows which route each company takes.

**What the combination tells you.** A company strong on both levers is a rare and powerful operator, earning a wide margin while still working its assets hard. A company with a high margin but low turnover competes on premium pricing, while one with a low margin but high turnover competes on volume and efficiency. Comparing the two across the industry tells the reader the strategy behind each company's operating profitability and which approach is winning.

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** EBIT margin and the operating income ratio measure operating profitability from the same family of figures, so reading them together confirms the picture and exposes any quirk in how a company reports its operating profit. The operating income ratio is the same idea framed on the leaderboard, and lining it up with the DuPont lever checks that a company's operating strength is consistent across the view. Agreement between the two builds confidence in the read.

**What the combination tells you.** When a company ranks high on both, its operating profitability is real and consistent, and its contribution to return on equity is trustworthy. When the two disagree, the gap usually points to one-time items or a classification difference that the reader should investigate before crediting the margin. The pairing helps the reader separate a clean operating advantage from an accounting artifact.

## Part 8: Limitations

DuPont EBIT margin can mislead in a single year, because a one-time charge can depress it and a one-time gain can flatter it, neither reflecting the true operating strength. A company in a heavy investment phase can show a thin margin that understates a business about to scale, while a company in a cyclical peak can show a wide margin that will not last. The lever should be read across several years before it is trusted as a guide to the business.

The lever captures operating profitability alone, so a high rank on it does not make a company the best overall pick. A company can run a wide EBIT margin while undermining its return through poor asset use, heavy debt, or a high tax bill. The margin also depends on how operating costs are classified, which differs across firms and can blur the comparison. The comparative view shows which company runs the most profitable operation, not which is the cheapest stock, so a high rank invites research rather than ending it.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company runs the widest operating margin in the industry, and does the lead come from pricing power or from a lower cost base?
2. How much of the leading company's return on equity comes from this operating lever rather than from debt or tax?
3. How long has the leader held the top margin? Is the gap with the rest of the industry widening or narrowing?
4. Does any company show a low margin only because it is in a heavy investment phase, with sales still to catch up to its new costs?
5. Is a company's wide margin a steady feature or the product of a single boom year or a one-time gain?
6. Are the companies clustered on this lever, which would suggest a commodity-like business with no edge, or spread apart, which would suggest one operator has real pricing power?
7. Does the company with the widest margin also work its assets hard, or does it earn its return through premium pricing on slow assets?
8. Does the company with the best operating margin also rank well on return on equity, or is a strong operation being wasted by weak asset use, heavy debt, or high tax?
