# FCF Growth Used

## Part 1: Definition

FCF growth used is the yearly free cash flow growth rate the valuation model assumed for a company. Free cash flow is the cash a business has left after it pays its running costs and reinvests in itself. The growth rate is how fast the model expects that cash flow to rise each year over the projection period. This rate is the most important assumption inside the valuation, because it shapes every future year of cash flow the model adds up. The comparative view shows the growth rate the model used side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see how aggressive or cautious each company's valuation was.

The rate is an input to the valuation, not a result the company reported. It is the model's chosen expectation, fit from the company's own past and then held within limits. A high growth rate used means the model expected the company's cash flow to climb quickly, which lifts its estimated value. A low or negative rate means the model expected flat or shrinking cash flow, which holds the estimate down. Reading the rate shows what each valuation was built on.

| Where a company stands | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| High growth rate used | The model expected fast cash flow growth, which lifts the estimated value. Check that the history supports it. |
| Moderate growth rate used | A measured expectation, in line with the company's steady record. |
| Low or negative rate used | The model expected flat or shrinking cash flow, which holds the estimated value down. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The growth rate is fit from the company's own free cash flow history. The model looks at how the company's free cash flow per share changed from year to year across the available record, averages those yearly changes, and uses that average as the growth rate for the projection. The figure is then capped, held within a floor below zero and a ceiling above it, so an unusual year cannot push the rate to an extreme. The capped rate is the FCF growth used shown for that company.

The cap is what keeps the comparison fair. A company with one explosive year would otherwise carry a runaway growth rate that inflated its whole valuation, and a company with one collapse would carry a rate that wiped its value out. By holding every company's rate within the same limits, the model keeps no single company's valuation from resting on a freak year. The rate then feeds the projection, where it lifts each future year of cash flow before the cash is discounted back. The method is described here in plain words, and the limits are kept conservative for comparability.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

FCF growth used lets the reader see the engine behind each valuation rather than trusting the estimate blind. Two companies can show similar fair values, but if one rests on a fast growth assumption and the other on a slow one, they are not equally safe estimates. This column exposes that difference. It shows which valuations lean on optimism and which stand on cautious ground, which is essential before trusting any of the value rankings.

The rate matters because it is the single input that moves the valuation most. A higher growth rate lifts every projected year, so a small change in the rate can produce a large change in the estimated value. Comparing the rates across an industry shows whether the leaderboard's cheapest companies earned their discounts through real value or through hopeful growth assumptions. It is the check that keeps the value rankings honest.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

The growth rate differs mainly because of each company's own free cash flow history. A company whose cash flow has climbed steadily over the years is fit with a higher rate, and a company whose cash flow has been flat or falling is fit with a lower one. A company with a choppy record produces an averaged rate that may sit between its best and worst years and represent neither well. The fit reflects the past, so a company changing direction may carry a rate that no longer fits.

The cap also shapes the differences between companies. A company whose true history would imply a very high or very low rate is pulled back to the limit, so two companies with very different records can end up at the same capped rate. This narrows the spread at the extremes and keeps the comparison even. A company sitting right at the cap is one whose history pushed beyond the allowed range, which is itself worth noticing.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare the growth rate used as a check on each company's valuation, not as a goal in itself. A higher rate is not better. It means the valuation depends more on growth that has not yet happened. The useful comparison is whether each company's rate is supported by its history and whether the value ranking it produced still holds under a cautious rate. Within one industry the rate is fit and capped the same way, so the assumptions can be compared fairly.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| A cheap company on a low growth rate | The strongest case. The discount holds even on cautious assumptions. |
| A cheap company on a high growth rate | A fragile case. The discount depends on growth that may not arrive. |
| A company sitting at the cap | Its history pushed beyond the allowed range. Read the raw record before trusting the value. |

Always read the growth rate next to the company's value ranking. A wide discount built on a high growth rate is far less convincing than the same discount built on a modest one.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

Read the growth rate as a window on how stable each company's cash flow has been. The rate reflects the company's record, so the most useful comparison is how the rates spread across the industry and how each company's cash flow path has behaved over time. A company whose cash flow has grown steadily supports its rate with a clean record, while a company whose cash flow has lurched up and down produces a rate that rests on shaky ground. Comparing the rates shows which valuations stand on firm history.

Set the latest rate against the company's own past readings to judge whether its growth story is steady or shifting. A company whose cash flow has slowed in recent years may still carry a rate lifted by older, faster years, which would flatter its valuation. A company whose cash flow has turned upward may carry a rate held down by a weak past. The pattern to watch is whether the rate matches the company's recent direction or lags behind it, because a lagging rate makes the valuation less reliable.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### DCF Intrinsic Value

**Why read the two together.** FCF growth used is the assumption that drives the DCF intrinsic value, and a higher rate produces a higher estimate. The growth column shows the input, and the intrinsic value column shows the output. Reading them together shows how much of a company's estimated value depends on the growth the model assumed.

**What the combination tells you.** A high intrinsic value backed by a modest growth rate is sturdy, because the value holds even on cautious assumptions. A high intrinsic value backed by an aggressive growth rate is fragile, because the estimate falls sharply if the growth does not come. Comparing the two across the industry shows which valuations rest on firm ground and which lean on optimistic inputs.

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** The margin of safety depends on the intrinsic value, and that value depends on the growth rate used. So the discount a company shows is partly a product of how aggressive its growth assumption was. Reading the two together shows whether a wide discount is real or simply the result of a hopeful growth input.

**What the combination tells you.** A wide margin of safety built on a low growth rate is the strongest result, because the discount survives even with cautious assumptions. A wide margin built on a high growth rate may vanish if the company grows more slowly than assumed. Comparing the two across the industry separates discounts that would hold up from discounts that depend on optimism.

### Free Cash Flow Growth

**Why read the two together.** Free cash flow growth is the company's actual historical growth, while FCF growth used is the rate the model settled on after fitting and capping that history. Reading them together shows how close the model's assumption is to the company's real record. A large gap between the two reveals where the cap or the averaging changed the picture.

**What the combination tells you.** When the rate used is close to the company's actual growth, the valuation reflects the real business. When the rate used is far below the actual growth, the cap has held the valuation back, and the company may be worth more than the estimate shows. When the rate used is far above a recent slowdown, the valuation may be too generous. Comparing the two across the industry shows where the model's assumptions stray from reality.

## Part 8: Limitations

FCF growth used is fit from the past, and the past is an imperfect guide to the future. A company changing direction can carry a rate that no longer fits, and a company with a short or choppy history produces a rate that represents none of its years well. The cap, while it keeps the comparison fair, also flattens real differences, so two companies with very different records can share the same capped rate. The figure is missing or unreliable for companies whose cash flow is negative or erratic, where no sensible rate can be fit.

The rate is an assumption, not a forecast the company endorses, and it drives a valuation that is only as good as the assumption itself. A reasonable-looking rate can still prove wrong, and the value built on it will be wrong with it. Comparing the rates shows which valuations are aggressive and which are cautious, not which growth will actually occur. A low rate makes a valuation safer to trust, not more likely to be right.
