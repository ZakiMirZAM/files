# Debt Service Coverage

## Part 1: Definition

Debt service coverage measures how many times over a company's cash from operations can cover the debt payments it owes. Debt service is the cash a company must hand to lenders in a period, made up of interest plus the portion of debt principal coming due. Coverage divides the cash the business generates by that required payment, so a figure of 2.0 means the company produces twice the cash it needs to meet its lenders. The comparative view places this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company most comfortably carries its debt and which is closest to the edge. A company at 3.0 has ample cash to pay lenders. A company at 1.1 barely covers what it owes.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies face the same business swings. When demand falls for the whole industry, every company's cash drops, but the one with thin coverage runs out of room first. Coverage shows which company could keep paying lenders through a hard stretch and which could not.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The most comfortable debt load, with cash to spare even in a weak year. |
| Middle of the pack | Adequate coverage for the industry. The company can meet its lenders in normal times. |
| Bottom of the industry | The thinnest margin, where a drop in cash could leave the company unable to pay. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Debt Service Coverage = Operating Cash Flow ÷ (Interest + Current Debt Due)`

The numerator is the cash the business generates, often operating cash flow or operating earnings before non-cash costs. The denominator is the cash owed to lenders in the period, combining interest and the principal coming due. A figure of 1.0 means the cash exactly covers the payment with nothing left, and anything below 1.0 means the company must find cash elsewhere to pay its lenders. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment keeps the comparison fair, because every company is measured over the same recent period.

A related and simpler measure is interest coverage, which compares cash only against interest and ignores the principal coming due. Debt service coverage is stricter because it includes the principal repayment, so a company can look fine on interest alone yet be tight once the full payment is counted. Reading both gives a fuller picture of how heavy the near-term burden really is.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Debt service coverage is the cleanest single measure of which company in an industry can safely carry its debt. Two companies can borrow the same amount, but the one generating more cash relative to its payments is far safer, because the cash to pay lenders is what keeps a company out of trouble. The ratio answers the question that the size of the debt alone cannot: can the company actually pay what it owes when it comes due.

The most basic thing a lender wants to know is whether the company can cover its payments from the business itself. Coverage answers that directly. A company that holds strong coverage year after year can borrow to fund growth without the fragility that sinks weaker rivals. A wide gap between the strongest and weakest coverage in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the weak company is one bad year away from a problem the strong one would shrug off.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is how much the company has borrowed, since a larger debt load means larger payments to cover. The second is the cash the business generates, because a company with strong, steady operating cash covers the same debt far more easily than one with thin cash. The third is the cost and timing of the debt, as a company paying high interest or facing a large repayment soon has a heavier near-term burden than one with cheap, long-dated debt.

A difference can also be a timing effect rather than a real one. A company with a large chunk of principal coming due this year shows weak coverage now, even if its cash easily covers a normal year. A company whose operating cash was boosted by a one-time inflow shows strong coverage that will not repeat. A company part-way through a heavy investment phase may show pressured cash today that recovers once the spending ends.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. What counts as safe coverage depends heavily on the industry. A stable business with predictable cash can run with thinner coverage than a cyclical one whose cash swings hard, so a figure that is comfortable in one industry is dangerous in another. Within one industry the comparison is fair because the companies share the same cash patterns and the same vulnerability to downturns.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | The safest borrower, with the most room to absorb a bad year. |
| Cluster of similar coverage | An industry where companies carry debt in roughly equal comfort. |
| One company far below the rest | The most fragile borrower. Check its upcoming repayments and the steadiness of its cash. |

Always read the weakest coverage alongside how steady the company's cash is. Thin coverage in a stable business is less alarming than the same coverage in one whose cash swings with the cycle.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's debt service coverage over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that holds strong coverage across cycles has shown it can carry its debt in good times and bad. A company whose coverage falls year after year is heading toward trouble, whether from rising debt or weakening cash.

The most useful pattern is whether coverage holds up through the industry's weak years. A company that stays comfortably above its payments even in a downturn has a durable balance sheet. A company whose coverage drops near 1.0 in a soft year is the one to watch, because the next downturn could push it below the line. A company steadily rebuilding its coverage is repaying debt or growing its cash, both signs of strengthening.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity shows how much a company has borrowed, while debt service coverage shows whether it can comfortably pay what it owes. A heavy debt load is only dangerous when the cash to service it is thin. Reading the two together separates a bold but safe borrower from a genuinely stretched one.

**What the combination tells you.** A high debt to equity paired with strong coverage means the company borrows heavily but handles the payments with ease, which is far less worrying than the debt figure alone suggests. A high debt to equity paired with weak coverage is the real danger, because the company owes a lot and struggles to pay it. Comparing the two across the industry shows which heavy borrower would survive a hard year and which would not.

### Operating Cash Flow Growth

**Why read the two together.** Debt service coverage rests on the cash the business generates, and operating cash flow growth shows whether that cash is rising or falling. A company with adequate coverage today but shrinking cash is heading toward trouble, while one with growing cash is getting safer. Reading the two together shows the direction coverage is likely to take.

**What the combination tells you.** When a company has solid coverage and growing operating cash, its ability to pay lenders is strengthening, the best position in the table. When coverage is adequate but cash is shrinking, the cushion is eroding even if today's figure looks fine. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies are building safety into their balance sheets and which are quietly losing it.

## Part 8: Limitations

Debt service coverage can swing for reasons that have little to do with a company's lasting strength. A large principal repayment falling in one year can drag the figure down even for a company with healthy cash, while a year with no big repayment can make a weaker company look safe. A one-time inflow can inflate the cash and the coverage for a year that will not repeat. The ratio reflects the recent period, not the full schedule of payments stretching ahead.

The number also depends on how cash and debt service are defined, and different definitions can shift the figure. Interest-only coverage flatters a company that has large principal repayments looming. The comparative view shows who covered their payments most comfortably in the recent past, not who faces the heaviest burden in years to come, so a strong ranking is a prompt to check the repayment schedule rather than a guarantee of safety.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company covers its debt payments most comfortably, and which sits closest to the edge?
2. Does the weakest-covered company face a large principal repayment soon, or is the thin coverage a steady feature of its finances?
3. For each company, how would coverage hold up if industry cash fell in a downturn?
4. Does any company's strong coverage rest on a one-time cash inflow that will not repeat?
5. Which heavy borrower in the debt-to-equity ranking still covers its payments easily, and which does not?
6. Is each company's coverage rising or falling over several years, and what in the business explains the direction?
7. Are the companies clustered together in coverage, or is one company far more fragile than the rest?
8. Which company combines a manageable debt load, steady cash, and strong coverage, marking it the safest balance sheet in the group?
