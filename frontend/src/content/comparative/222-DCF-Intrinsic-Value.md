# DCF Intrinsic Value

## Part 1: Definition

DCF intrinsic value is an estimate of what one share of a company is worth, based on the cash the business is expected to produce in the future. DCF stands for discounted cash flow, a method that adds up a company's future cash and adjusts it for the fact that money arriving later is worth less than money today. The result is a fair value per share, shown in dollars. The comparative view shows this estimate side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see what each company is judged to be worth and compare that against its price.

The estimate is a model's view of value, not a market price. The market price is what buyers and sellers agree on today. The intrinsic value is what the future cash flow suggests a share should be worth. The two are usually different, and the gap between them is where opportunity or risk lies. A company with a high intrinsic value relative to its price may be cheap, and a company with a low one relative to its price may be dear.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| High intrinsic value vs its price | The model sees the share as worth more than the market pays. A possible bargain. |
| Intrinsic value near its price | The market and the model roughly agree on what the share is worth. |
| Low intrinsic value vs its price | The model sees the share as worth less than the market pays. A possible overvaluation. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Intrinsic Value = Present Value of Projected Cash Flows + Present Value of Terminal Value`

The model starts from the company's free cash flow, the cash left after the business pays its running costs and reinvests in itself. It projects that cash flow forward for several years using a growth rate fit from the company's own history. Each future year's cash flow is discounted back to today, meaning it is shrunk to reflect the wait and the risk, using a required rate of return. A terminal value captures the years beyond the projection, and it too is discounted back. The pieces are added together and divided by the share count to give a value per share.

The discount rate is the company's required rate of return, the yearly return an investor should demand for the risk taken. The growth rate is fit from history and then capped, so a single fast year cannot inflate the estimate, and a sharp decline cannot collapse it. Because every company is valued with the same method at the same moment, the estimates can be ranked fairly against each other and against each company's price. The method is described here in plain terms, and the exact settings are kept conservative so the comparison stays even across companies.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

DCF intrinsic value gives the reader a value to set against price, which is the heart of judging whether a stock is cheap. A leaderboard of prices alone says nothing about worth, because a high price can be justified and a low price can still be too high. The intrinsic value supplies the missing half. Placed next to price, it shows which companies the model sees as undervalued and which it sees as overvalued.

The estimate matters because it grounds cheapness in the business rather than in a multiple. A simple ratio compares price to one year's earnings or cash, while the intrinsic value reflects many years of expected cash flow and the risk attached to it. Comparing the estimate across an industry shows which company offers the most value for its price, and it feeds directly into the margin of safety that ranks the discounts.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces drive the gap between two companies. The first is the level of free cash flow, since a company that already produces more cash starts from a higher base. The second is the assumed growth rate, since faster expected growth lifts every future year and raises the estimate. The third is the required rate of return, since a riskier company is discounted harder, which lowers its estimated value even at the same cash flow.

The estimate can also differ for reasons that are about the model rather than the business. A company with a long, steady history gives the growth rate a firm footing, while a company with a choppy history gives it a shaky one. A company part-way through heavy reinvestment may show low current cash flow that understates its future. The cap on the growth rate keeps any single company from looking far better than its peers on one hopeful year, which keeps the comparison even.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare the intrinsic value to each company's own price, not the dollar figures against each other. A larger intrinsic value in dollars does not mean a better deal, because share counts and prices differ. The useful comparison is the size of each company's value relative to its price, which is what the margin of safety captures. Within one industry the method is applied the same way, so the value-to-price gaps can be ranked fairly.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Intrinsic value far above price | The model sees a discount. Check whether the growth assumption is realistic. |
| Intrinsic value close to price | The market and model agree. No clear edge from this metric. |
| Intrinsic value below price | The model sees the stock as expensive. The market expects more than the model assumes. |

Always check the growth assumption behind a high estimate. An intrinsic value that towers over price often rests on a hopeful growth rate, and a more cautious rate would shrink the gap.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's intrinsic value over several years, so the reader can watch the estimate move alongside the price. A company whose intrinsic value rises steadily is building expected cash flow that the model believes in. A company whose estimate swings sharply year to year has unstable cash flow, which makes its current value less reliable. Watching the estimate against the price shows whether a discount is opening or closing over time.

The most useful pattern is an intrinsic value that grows steadily while the price lags behind, because that gap is a widening discount built on real improvement. A company whose estimate keeps falling is losing the cash flow that justified its value, and a high current reading may not last. Comparing how steadily each company's estimate has tracked its price tells the reader which valuations to trust and which to treat with caution.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** DCF intrinsic value is the estimate of worth, and margin of safety is the discount of price below that worth. The two are halves of the same idea, one giving the value and the other giving the gap to price. Reading them together turns a dollar estimate into a ranking of how cheap each company is.

**What the combination tells you.** A high intrinsic value matters only when the price sits well below it, which is exactly what the margin of safety shows. When both point the same way, the company is valued highly by the model and priced cheaply by the market. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies offer real discounts and which carry a high estimate that the price has already caught up to.

### FCF Growth Used

**Why read the two together.** The intrinsic value rests on the assumed free cash flow growth rate, and a higher rate produces a higher estimate. The FCF growth used column shows that assumption directly, so the reader can see how much of the value depends on expected growth. It exposes the single input that moves the estimate most.

**What the combination tells you.** A high intrinsic value built on a modest growth assumption is sturdy, because the value holds even if the company grows slowly. A high intrinsic value built on an aggressive growth assumption is fragile, because the estimate falls sharply if the growth does not arrive. Comparing the two across the industry shows which valuations rest on cautious inputs and which depend on optimism.

### EVA Intrinsic Value

**Why read the two together.** DCF intrinsic value and EVA intrinsic value are two different methods aimed at the same target, a fair value per share. One discounts future cash flow, and the other discounts the profit earned above the cost of capital. Reading them together checks one estimate against an independent second estimate.

**What the combination tells you.** When the two methods land near each other, the fair value estimate is more reliable, because two routes agree. When they diverge sharply, the value rests on a fragile assumption that one method is sensitive to, and the reader should treat the figure with caution. Comparing both across the industry shows which companies have valuations that hold up under more than one method.

## Part 8: Limitations

DCF intrinsic value is an estimate, and it is only as good as its assumptions about future cash flow, growth, and risk. Small changes to the growth rate or the discount rate can move the estimate a great deal, so a company can rank high on value mainly because the inputs favored it. The estimate fits some industries poorly, especially banks, property companies, and money-losing firms, where the cash flow model does not capture the business well, and the figure may be missing or unreliable for them.

The estimate also reflects a model's view, not a guarantee. The future cash flow may never arrive, and the company may face changes the model cannot see. Ranking the estimates shows which stocks the model judges undervalued, not which are certain to rise. A high reading is a reason to study the company's cash flow and assumptions, not a promise that the share will reach the estimated value.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company's intrinsic value sits furthest above its price, and is the gap driven by strong cash flow or by an optimistic growth assumption?
2. Does the company with the highest estimate also show a steady cash flow history, or is its value built on a choppy record?
3. Is the high estimate supported by a cautious growth rate, or does it depend on an aggressive one?
4. Does the second valuation method, EVA intrinsic value, land near the DCF estimate, or do the two methods disagree?
5. How steadily has the company's intrinsic value tracked its price over several years, and is the discount widening or closing?
6. Are the value-to-price gaps clustered across the industry, or does one company stand out as far cheaper than its worth?
7. Which company's intrinsic value sits below its price, and what future is the market paying for that the model does not assume?
8. Does the estimate fit this industry well, or is the cash flow model a poor match for how these companies make money?
