# Return On Equity

## Part 1: Definition

Return on equity measures how much yearly profit a company earns for each dollar of shareholder money. Shareholder money, called equity, is what the owners are left with after all debts are subtracted from everything the company owns. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company turns owner money into profit most effectively. A company with a return on equity of 25 percent earns 25 cents of profit a year for every dollar of equity. A direct competitor at 8 percent earns only 8 cents on the same dollar.

The side-by-side comparison carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing. Firms in one industry face the same customers, the same input costs, and the same competition. When those conditions are shared, a higher return on equity points to a genuinely better operator rather than an easier business.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The strongest operator, or the one using the most debt. Check leverage before crediting skill. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical economics for the industry. The business is neither advantaged nor weak. |
| Bottom of the industry | A weaker operator, a company in a heavy investment phase, or one with a recent loss. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity`

Net income is the profit left after every cost, including interest and tax. Shareholders' equity is the owners' stake on the balance sheet. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because it removes the question of which company simply reported more recently.

A useful way to read a company's number is the three-part split from value-investing practice: return on equity equals profit margin, multiplied by how hard the assets work, multiplied by how much debt the company uses. Two companies can reach the same return on equity by very different routes. One earns it through high margins. Another borrows heavily to lift a mediocre margin.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Return on equity is the cleanest single measure of which company in an industry is the better business. Profit on its own rewards size, so the largest company usually shows the biggest profit. Return on equity removes size from the picture and asks how well each company uses the money entrusted to it. That question is what separates a great operator from a merely large one.

A company that holds a high return on equity year after year, above the rest of its industry, is usually defending something competitors cannot copy. That durable advantage is often called a moat. A wide gap between the best and worst company in the same industry is a signal worth investigating, because the leader is doing something the others cannot.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the profit margin, the share of each sales dollar kept as profit. The second is asset efficiency, how much sales the company produces from its assets. The third is debt. A company can lift its return on equity by borrowing more, which raises the number without making the underlying business any better.

A difference can also be an accounting effect rather than a real one. Share buybacks shrink equity and lift the ratio. A recent write-down shrinks equity and lifts it too. A company part-way through a large expansion can show a low number simply because new equity has been raised but the profit it will fund has not yet arrived.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. A high return on equity in a capital-light industry and a moderate one in a heavy industry are not the same achievement, so cross-industry comparison is misleading. Within one industry the comparison is fair because the companies share the same economics.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | A likely moat, unless the lead comes from heavy debt. Confirm with the leverage metrics in this view. |
| Cluster of similar numbers | A commodity-like industry where no company has a real edge. |
| A leader whose lead is shrinking | Competitors are catching up, or the leader's advantage is fading. |

Always check the leverage of the top-ranked company before crediting it as the best business. A high number built on borrowing is more fragile than the same number built on margins.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's return on equity over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that stays at the top of its industry for many years has the most convincing claim to a durable advantage. A single strong year proves far less, because one good year can come from a temporary boom or a one-time gain.

The most useful pattern is a widening or narrowing gap between the leader and the rest. A leader pulling further ahead is strengthening its position. A leader being caught is losing the edge that justified its premium. A company climbing the ranking steadily is often the better forward-looking pick than the current leader.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### ROIC

**Why read the two together.** Return on equity can be inflated by debt, so a high number does not always mean a strong business. Return on invested capital measures profit against all the money in the business, both equity and debt, so debt cannot flatter it. Reading the two together separates real operating skill from financial engineering.

**What the combination tells you.** When a company ranks high on both, the strong return is earned by the business itself and is trustworthy. When a company ranks high on return on equity but only average on return on invested capital, the lead comes from borrowing rather than skill. The company with the best return on invested capital is usually the safest pick even when another company shows a flashier return on equity.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity shows how much the company borrows for each dollar of owner money. The leverage part of the return on equity calculation comes directly from this borrowing. Putting the two ratios next to each other shows whether a top ranking was earned or borrowed.

**What the combination tells you.** A high return on equity paired with low debt is the strongest result in the table, because the profit is real and the balance sheet is safe. A high return on equity paired with high debt is a warning, because a downturn can erase the profit and leave the debt behind. Comparing the leverage of the top companies tells the reader which leader would survive a hard year and which would not.

## Part 8: Limitations

Return on equity can rank a weaker business above a stronger one when the weaker business simply borrows more. The comparison also breaks when a company has negative equity, which can happen after large buybacks or losses, because the ratio then gives a meaningless or misleading figure. A company that just took a large write-down can jump up the ranking for the wrong reason.

The number reflects the past year, not the future. A company at the top today may be earning its return from a product cycle that is about to fade. The comparative view shows which company has performed best, not which company is cheapest to buy, so a high ranking is a starting point for research rather than a verdict on the stock.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company leads the industry on this metric, and is the lead built on high margins or on heavy borrowing?
2. Does the top-ranked company also lead on return on invested capital, or does the lead disappear once debt is removed?
3. How long has the leader held the top position? Is the lead widening or narrowing against the rest of the industry?
4. Does any company show a very high number alongside negative or shrinking equity from buybacks or losses?
5. Which company is climbing the ranking fastest, and what in the business explains the climb?
6. Are the companies clustered together, which would suggest no real advantage, or spread apart, which would suggest one company has an edge?
7. Which company would keep the strongest return in a bad year, judging by how little debt it relies on?
8. Does the current ranking match the cheapest stock, or is the best business also the most expensive in the group?
