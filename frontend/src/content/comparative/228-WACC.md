# WACC

## Part 1: Definition

WACC measures the blended yearly return a company must earn to satisfy everyone who funds it. WACC stands for weighted average cost of capital, the cost of a company's debt and equity combined, weighted by how much of each it uses. Lenders expect interest, and shareholders expect a return on their stake, so the company's overall cost of money is a mix of the two. WACC is the hurdle rate the business must clear, because any project that earns less than WACC leaves investors worse off than other options at the same risk. The comparative view shows this hurdle side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company faces the steepest bar and which faces the gentlest.

The side-by-side view carries meaning because the companies do roughly the same thing yet can face different costs of capital. Firms in one industry share customers and competition, but they differ in how risky their cash flows are and how much debt they carry. A higher WACC means the market judges a company riskier, so its returns must clear a higher bar to create value. A lower WACC means the market sees safer cash flows, which lowers the bar.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Lowest WACC in the industry | The market sees the safest cash flows, so this company clears the lowest bar to create value. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical risk for the industry, with a hurdle in line with peers. |
| Highest WACC in the industry | The market sees the riskiest cash flows or the heaviest reliance on costly capital. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`WACC = (E/V × Cost of Equity) + (D/V × Cost of Debt × (1 − Tax Rate))`

E is the market value of equity, D is the value of debt, and V is the two added together. The cost of equity is the return shareholders require, and the cost of debt is the interest rate on borrowing, lowered by the tax saving because interest is tax deductible. Each cost is weighted by its share of total funding, then the two are added. The comparative table computes WACC for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from lowest to highest.

The cost of equity is usually built from the risk-free rate plus a premium for the company's risk, while the cost of debt reflects the rate the company pays to borrow. A company that uses more debt leans on the cheaper source, which can lower WACC, but only up to the point where the added risk raises both costs. Ranking at one shared moment is what makes the comparison fair, because every company's hurdle is measured against the same market conditions at the same time.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

WACC is the bar that decides whether a company's returns create value. A company can earn a high return on its capital and still destroy value if that return falls short of its WACC. Comparing WACC across an industry shows which companies must clear a high hurdle and which enjoy a low one, which is essential context for reading every return and valuation in the view. A return that looks strong against a low WACC may look weak against a high one.

The hurdle matters because value creation is the spread between returns and WACC, not the return alone. Two companies can earn the same return on capital, yet the one with the lower WACC creates more value, because it keeps a wider spread. WACC also sets the discount rate in valuation, so a lower WACC lifts the present value of a company's future cash flows. Comparing WACC across the industry shows which companies the market treats as safe and how much that safety is worth.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Three forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is business risk, how sensitive the company's profit is to the business cycle, since cyclical companies face a higher cost of equity. The second is the capital structure, the mix of debt and equity, because debt is cheaper than equity but more debt eventually raises the risk of both. The third is the cost of debt itself, which depends on how much the company borrows and how safely it can repay.

A difference can also come from how the cost of equity is measured rather than from the business. The cost of equity rests on a beta, a measure of how much the share moves with the market, and a higher beta lifts the cost of equity and the WACC. The market value weights also shift the number, so a company whose share price has risen carries more equity weight and may show a higher WACC. A company with a large tax shield from deductible interest carries a lower after-tax cost of debt, which trims its WACC.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. WACC varies across industries because some businesses face far more cyclical demand than others, so a WACC that is high in a stable industry can be normal in a volatile one. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies share the same demand backdrop and the differences reflect each company's own risk and funding choices.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far below the rest | The market sees the safest cash flows, which gives this company the lowest hurdle and a valuation edge. |
| Cluster of similar rates | The companies carry similar risk and funding, so the hurdle is not what separates them. |
| One company far above the rest | The market sees the most risk, or the company leans on costly equity, which raises its bar to create value. |

Always read WACC next to each company's return on capital, because the hurdle means nothing on its own. A low WACC only helps if the company earns a return above it, and a high WACC is only a problem if returns fall short of it.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's WACC over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company whose WACC stays low and steady has earned the market's lasting trust in its cash flows, which is a quiet advantage over rivals that pay more for capital. A WACC that drifts up signals rising risk, either from the business or from heavier reliance on costly capital.

Much of the movement in WACC over time comes from the wider market rather than the company, because the risk-free rate and the price of risk rise and fall for everyone at once. The useful comparison separates that shared shift from a company-specific change. When one company's WACC climbs while its peers hold steady, the change is its own, often from added debt or rising business risk. When all the companies move together, the cause is the market, and the ranking among them is what still matters.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Cost Of Equity

**Why read the two together.** The cost of equity is the equity piece of WACC, and for companies that lean on equity it is the larger piece. The cost of equity column shows the return shareholders require, while WACC blends that with the cost of debt. Reading them together shows how much of a company's hurdle comes from its owners' demands and how much from the cheaper debt it carries.

**What the combination tells you.** A company whose WACC sits well below its cost of equity is using debt to lower its overall hurdle, which helps value as long as the debt stays safe. A company whose WACC sits close to its cost of equity relies mainly on equity, so its hurdle is set by shareholder risk. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies lower their bar through borrowing and which carry the full weight of equity risk.

### Debt To Equity

**Why read the two together.** Debt to equity shows how much a company borrows for each dollar of owner money, and that mix is what WACC weights together. More debt pulls WACC toward the cheaper cost of debt, but it also raises the risk that lifts both costs. Reading the two together shows whether a low WACC is earned by safe cash flows or bought with heavy leverage.

**What the combination tells you.** A low WACC paired with low debt is the strongest result, because the hurdle is low and the balance sheet is safe. A low WACC paired with high debt is more fragile, because a downturn can raise the cost of both debt and equity and push the hurdle up just when the company can least afford it. Comparing leverage across the low-WACC companies tells the reader which low hurdle would survive a hard year.

### EVA

**Why read the two together.** EVA is the profit a company earns above its capital charge, and WACC is the rate that sets that charge. The WACC column shows the bar, and the EVA column shows whether the company cleared it in dollars. Reading them together connects the hurdle to the result, so the reader can see who beats a steep bar and who only clears a gentle one.

**What the combination tells you.** A positive EVA earned over a high WACC is the strongest result, because the company adds value despite a demanding hurdle. A positive EVA earned over a low WACC may flatter a business that only looks good because its bar is low. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies create value over a tough standard and which depend on a forgiving cost of capital.

## Part 8: Limitations

WACC is an estimate, not a fact, and it rests on inputs that are hard to pin down. The cost of equity depends on a beta and a risk premium that can be measured several ways, so two reasonable analysts can produce different WACCs for the same company. The market value weights shift with the share price, so a rally can change a company's WACC without any change in its business. A small change in WACC can swing a valuation sharply, which makes the ranking sensitive to assumptions.

WACC also reflects current conditions, so a low reading driven by low market interest rates can rise when rates do, lifting every company's hurdle at once. The number tells the reader the bar, not whether the company clears it, so a low WACC is not a verdict on quality. WACC fits some industries poorly, especially banks, where debt is raw material rather than financing, so the figure may be missing or misleading for them.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company faces the lowest WACC, and does the low hurdle come from safe cash flows or from heavy use of debt?
2. Does the company with the lowest WACC also earn a return on capital above it, or is the low bar wasted on weak returns?
3. Which company faces the highest WACC, and is the high hurdle driven by cyclical business risk or by costly equity?
4. How much of each company's WACC comes from its cost of equity, and how much from cheaper debt?
5. Has any company's WACC moved against the industry trend, which would point to a company-specific change in risk or leverage?
6. Are the companies clustered at similar rates, which would suggest the hurdle is not what separates them, or spread apart, which would suggest real differences in risk?
7. Would a low-WACC company hold its low hurdle in a downturn, judging by how much debt it relies on?
8. Does the lowest-WACC company also create the most value above its hurdle, judging by EVA and the spread over its return on capital?
