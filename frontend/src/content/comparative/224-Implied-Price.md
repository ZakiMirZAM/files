# Implied Price

## Part 1: Definition

Implied price is the share price the valuation model points to, read against the price the market sets today. The model estimates what one share is worth from the company's expected cash flow, and that estimate is the fair value the model implies. On this view the implied price is the market price each company's fair value is measured against, so the reader can see, company by company, whether the market pays more or less than the model's value. The comparative view shows the price alongside the fair value for every large company in one industry.

The implied price and the fair value are two readings of the same share. The fair value comes from the model and reflects future cash flow. The implied price comes from the market and reflects what buyers and sellers agree on now. When the fair value sits above the price, the model sees a discount, and when it sits below, the model sees the stock as dear. The comparison of the two is the bridge between what a company is judged to be worth and what it costs.

| Where a company stands | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Price well below fair value | The market pays less than the model's value. A possible bargain. |
| Price near fair value | The market and the model roughly agree on the share. |
| Price above fair value | The market pays more than the model's value. A possible overvaluation. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

The implied price is the company's current market price per share, the figure the valuation is compared against. When a live market quote is available, the model uses it directly. When no live quote is available, the model falls back to the company's market value divided by its share count, which gives a price as of the last filing. This price is then set against the fair value per share that the model produces from discounted cash flow.

The fair value side is built the same way for every company, by projecting free cash flow, discounting it at a required rate of return, and adding a value for the years beyond the projection. The growth rate behind the projection is fit from history and capped, so the fair value does not swing on a single year. Because both the price and the fair value are formed the same way at the same moment across the industry, the gap between them can be ranked fairly. The method is described here in plain words, and the settings are kept conservative for comparability.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

Implied price gives the reader the market's number to set against the model's number, and the comparison of the two is where cheapness shows. A fair value on its own says what a company is worth, but it says nothing about whether the stock is a good buy until it is read against the price. The implied price supplies that price. Together they show which companies the market prices below their value and which it prices above.

The comparison matters because price and value drift apart for long stretches, and that drift is the source of opportunity and risk. A company can be excellent and still be a poor buy if its price has run far past its value. A company can be ordinary and still be a fair buy if its price has fallen well below its value. Comparing the price against the fair value across an industry shows which companies the market may be mispricing and which it has judged correctly.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

The price differs between two companies for the same reasons any market price differs. It reflects what buyers and sellers expect, how confident they are, and how the broad market feels about the company on the day. A company the market trusts trades at a higher price relative to its value, and a company the market doubts trades at a lower one. The price moves with news and sentiment, often faster and more sharply than the fair value behind it.

The gap between price and fair value is what carries the meaning, and that gap differs because the two readings respond to different things. The fair value moves slowly, as expected cash flow and growth shift. The price moves quickly, as the mood of the market shifts. A wide gap can mean the market sees a problem the model misses, or that the market has overlooked value the model captures. The price alone does not say which is true.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare each company's price against its own fair value, not the prices against each other. A higher price in dollars says nothing about value, because share counts and fair values differ. The useful comparison is how far each company's price sits from its fair value, which is the gap the margin of safety measures. Within one industry the price and the fair value are formed the same way, so those gaps can be ranked fairly.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Price far below fair value | The model sees a discount. Check whether the fair value rests on a realistic growth assumption. |
| Price close to fair value | The market and model agree. No clear edge from this metric. |
| Price above fair value | The market expects more than the model assumes. Find out what the market is paying for. |

Always read the gap together with the quality of the fair value behind it. A price far below fair value means little if the fair value itself rests on hopeful assumptions.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's price against its fair value over several years, so the reader can watch the gap open and close. A price that falls below a steady fair value is a discount appearing, and a price that climbs above it is a premium building. Watching the two lines move shows whether the market is drifting away from the model's value or back toward it. A gap that the market keeps refusing to close is a different signal from one that has just appeared.

The most useful pattern is a price that lags a rising fair value, because that is a discount widening on a business that is improving. A price that races ahead of a flat fair value is a premium that may not last. Comparing how each company's price has tracked its fair value tells the reader which gaps reflect real mispricing and which reflect a market that disagrees with the model for a reason. A persistent gap deserves more suspicion than a fresh one.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Margin Of Safety

**Why read the two together.** Implied price is the market price, and margin of safety turns the gap between that price and fair value into a percentage discount. The price gives the raw comparison, and the margin gives the ranked size of it. Reading them together moves from two dollar figures to a clear ranking of how cheap each company is.

**What the combination tells you.** A price well below fair value matters most when the resulting margin of safety is wide, which is what makes the discount large enough to protect against error. When both point the same way, the company is cheap by both the raw comparison and the ranked one. Comparing them across the industry shows which price gaps are big enough to act on and which are too small to matter.

### DCF Intrinsic Value

**Why read the two together.** Implied price is the market's number, and DCF intrinsic value is the model's fair value that the price is measured against. The two are the price and the value at the heart of the comparison. Reading them together shows the full bridge from what a company costs to what the model says it is worth.

**What the combination tells you.** When the fair value sits well above the price, the model sees value the market has not paid for, and the gap is the opportunity. When the fair value sits below the price, the market is paying for a future the model does not assume. Comparing the two across the industry shows which companies are priced below their worth and which have prices that have outrun it.

### Graham Number Ratio

**Why read the two together.** Implied price compares the market price to a cash flow model's fair value, while the Graham number ratio compares the price to a conservative fair-value floor built from earnings and book value. The two test the price against two very different value benchmarks. Reading them together checks the price against more than one yardstick.

**What the combination tells you.** When the price sits below both the model's fair value and the Graham floor, two independent benchmarks agree the stock is cheap, which is a strong signal. When the price is below one benchmark but above the other, the cheapness depends on which method is trusted. Comparing both across the industry shows which companies look cheap by several measures and which look cheap by only one.

## Part 8: Limitations

Implied price is only useful when read against a fair value, and that fair value is an estimate that can be wrong. A wide gap between price and fair value may reflect a flaw in the model rather than a real bargain, since the model can miss what the market sees. When no live quote is available, the price falls back to the last filing date, which can be stale and can distort the gap. The comparison is weak in industries where the cash flow model fits poorly, because the fair value itself is unreliable there.

The market price is not always the better judge or the worse one, and the gap alone does not say which side is right. A price below fair value can stay there for years if the market sees a problem the model omits. Ranking the gaps shows where price and value diverge, not where the divergence will close. A wide gap is a reason to investigate why the market disagrees with the model, not proof that the market is wrong.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company's price sits furthest below its fair value, and is the gap large enough to matter once the margin of safety is read?
2. Does the fair value behind a wide gap rest on a realistic growth assumption, or on a hopeful one?
3. Does a second benchmark, such as the Graham number, also place the price below fair value, or only the cash flow model?
4. How long has the price stayed below fair value? Has the market refused to close the gap for years?
5. Is the price a live market quote, or a stale figure from the last filing date that may distort the gap?
6. Are the price-to-value gaps clustered across the industry, or does one company stand far apart from its fair value?
7. Which company's price sits above its fair value, and what future is the market paying for that the model does not assume?
8. Where the market and the model disagree most, is the model missing something the market can see?
