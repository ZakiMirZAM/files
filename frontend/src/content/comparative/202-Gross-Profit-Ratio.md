# Gross Profit Ratio

## Part 1: Definition

The gross profit ratio is the share of each sales dollar a company keeps after paying the direct cost of making its product. The direct cost is called cost of goods sold. The direct cost covers the materials and labor that go straight into the product, but not selling or overhead costs. The ratio is also called gross margin. The comparative view shows this number side by side for every large company in one industry, so the reader can see which company keeps the most from each dollar of sales before other expenses. A company with a gross profit ratio of 60 percent keeps 60 cents of every sales dollar after direct costs. A competitor at 30 percent keeps only 30 cents.

The side-by-side comparison points to which company has the most pricing power. Pricing power is the ability to charge more than rivals without losing customers. Within one industry the companies make similar products and face similar input costs. A higher gross margin then usually means the company can charge a premium or buy its inputs more cheaply.

| Where a company ranks | What the rank usually means |
|---|---|
| Top of the industry | The strongest pricing power or the lowest input costs, often a sign of a premium brand or scale. |
| Middle of the pack | Typical pricing and costs for the industry, with no clear edge. |
| Bottom of the industry | A price-taker, a higher-cost producer, or a company competing mainly on low prices. |

## Part 2: How it is calculated

`Gross Profit Ratio = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue`

Revenue is total sales for the year. Cost of goods sold is the direct cost of producing what was sold. Subtracting one from the other gives gross profit, and dividing that by revenue gives the ratio. The comparative table computes this for each company at its latest reported year and ranks the companies from highest to lowest. Ranking at one shared year is what makes the comparison fair, because it removes the question of which company reported more recently.

The ratio measures management's skill in buying and selling at favorable prices. A high gross margin can come from charging more, from sourcing inputs more cheaply, or from a product mix tilted toward higher-margin lines. Reading the gross margin next to the operating margin shows how much of this early advantage survives once selling and overhead costs are paid.

## Part 3: Why this metric is used to compare companies

The gross profit ratio is the cleanest early read on which company in an industry holds pricing power. Companies in one industry buy similar materials and pay similar wages, so the cost side is largely shared. When costs are shared, the company that keeps the most gross profit is usually the one customers are willing to pay more for, or the one that has driven its production costs below the rest.

A durable gap in gross margin often points to a real advantage that rivals cannot easily copy. The advantage might be a trusted brand, a patent, or a cost edge from large scale. A company that holds a high gross margin year after year, above its industry, is usually defending something competitors lack. A wide gap between the best and worst company is a signal worth investigating, because the leader is doing something the others cannot.

## Part 4: What makes one company's number differ from another's

Two forces explain most of the gap between two companies. The first is the price each company can charge. A premium brand or a patented product commands a higher price, which lifts the gross margin. The second is the cost of production. A company with cheaper inputs, lower labor costs, or greater scale produces the same product for less and keeps more of each sale.

A difference can also come from how each company defines its costs rather than from a real edge. One company may include shipping or warehouse costs in cost of goods sold, while another reports them lower down, which makes the first look less profitable at the gross level. Product mix matters too. A company that sells more of its high-margin lines will show a higher overall gross margin even if each individual product is no more profitable than a rival's.

## Part 5: How to compare companies on this metric

Compare only companies inside the same industry, which is exactly what this view does. Gross margins differ enormously across industries, so a software firm and a grocery chain cannot be judged against each other. A high gross margin in a capital-light industry and a moderate one in a thin-margin industry are not the same achievement. Within one industry the comparison is fair, because the companies share similar products and costs.

| Comparison pattern | How to read it |
|---|---|
| One company far above the rest | Likely a premium brand or a real cost advantage. Confirm it survives in the operating margin. |
| A cluster of similar margins | A commodity-like industry where no company can charge a premium. |
| A leader whose margin is shrinking | Pricing power is fading, or input costs are rising faster than the company can pass them on. |

Always check whether a high gross margin carries through to the operating margin. A company can keep a strong gross margin but spend it all on marketing or overhead, leaving little real profit.

## Part 6: Reading the historical trend

The comparative chart shows each company's gross margin over several years, so the reader can watch the ranking move rather than judging it from one snapshot. A company that holds the top gross margin for many years has the most convincing claim to lasting pricing power. A single strong year proves far less, because one good year can come from a temporary spike in prices or a brief drop in input costs.

The most useful pattern is a widening or narrowing gap between the leader and the rest. A leader pulling further ahead is strengthening its pricing power. A leader being caught is losing the premium that set it apart. A company whose gross margin is sliding while rivals hold steady is often facing rising costs or fading demand it cannot pass on to customers.

## Part 7: Metrics to read alongside it

### Operating Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The gross profit ratio measures profit after direct production costs only, while the operating income ratio measures profit after the selling and overhead costs too. A company can keep a high gross margin and then spend it all on marketing and administration. Reading the two together shows how much of the early pricing advantage actually reaches operating profit.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that leads on both gross and operating margin turns its pricing power into real operating profit, the strongest result in the group. A company with a high gross margin but a weak operating margin is spending its advantage on overhead rather than keeping it. The company that converts gross margin into operating margin most fully is usually the better-run business, even when another company prices higher.

### Net Income Ratio

**Why read the two together.** The gross profit ratio sits at the top of the income statement, and the net income ratio sits at the bottom, after every cost including interest and tax. Comparing them shows how much of a company's early advantage survives the full set of expenses. A wide drop from one to the other points to heavy costs below the gross line.

**What the combination tells you.** A company that ranks high on both is profitable at every level, which is the most durable position. A company that ranks high on gross margin but low on net margin is losing its advantage to interest, tax, or overhead further down. Comparing the full path from gross to net margin tells the reader which leader keeps its edge all the way to the bottom line.

### Return On Invested Capital

**Why read the two together.** The gross profit ratio shows pricing power, and return on invested capital shows how well that power turns into a return on all the money in the business. A high gross margin is only valuable if the company does not need to pour in huge amounts of capital to earn it. Reading the two together connects pricing strength to capital efficiency.

**What the combination tells you.** A company with a high gross margin and a high return on invested capital both prices well and uses capital efficiently, the mark of a genuine moat. A company with a high gross margin but a low return on invested capital must spend heavily on assets to support its sales, which dilutes the advantage. The company strong on both is usually the best business in the industry, because its pricing power is not eaten up by capital needs.

## Part 8: Limitations

The gross profit ratio captures only the direct cost of production. A company can lead on gross margin yet trail on real profit once overhead, interest, and tax are counted. The ratio is also sensitive to accounting choices, because companies differ in what they place in cost of goods sold, which can make one company look more efficient than it truly is. A favorable product mix in a single year can flatter the number without any gain in underlying pricing power.

The number reflects the past year, not the future. A high gross margin can rest on a product cycle or a pricing advantage that is about to fade as rivals respond. The comparative view shows which company keeps the most from each sales dollar before other costs, not which company is the best overall investment, so a high ranking is a starting point for research rather than a verdict on the stock.

## Part 9: Precise qualitative questions

1. Which company leads the industry on gross margin, and is the lead built on premium pricing or on lower production costs?
2. Does the top-ranked company keep its lead at the operating and net margin levels, or does the advantage disappear lower down?
3. How long has the leader held the top gross margin? Is the gap to the rest widening or narrowing?
4. Are the companies clustered together in a commodity industry with no pricing power, or spread apart where one company can charge a premium?
5. Has any company's gross margin shifted because of a change in product mix rather than a real change in pricing power?
6. Which company is improving its gross margin fastest, and is it raising prices or cutting costs to do so?
7. Does the company with the highest gross margin also earn a high return on invested capital, or does it need heavy assets to support its sales?
8. Could differences in what each company counts as cost of goods sold be exaggerating the gap between the leaders?
