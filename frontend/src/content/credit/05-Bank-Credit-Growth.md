# Bank Credit Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Bank Credit Growth measures how fast the total credit held by all US commercial banks is expanding, shown as the year-over-year percentage change in total bank credit. Total bank credit is the broadest measure of what banks put on their balance sheets, adding together all loans (business, commercial real estate, and consumer) and the securities banks hold, such as Treasuries and mortgage-backed bonds. The FRED series id is TOTBKCR, reported weekly, and it can be viewed as a level (the dollar amount outstanding, recently near 18 trillion dollars, approximate) or as a year-over-year growth rate (recently in the low single digits, approximate). The growth rate is the more useful view because the level of credit rises over time in any growing economy, so the rate of change is what signals a turn. The metric reveals how much new credit the banking system is creating, which is one of the main engines that drives the economy through the credit cycle.

The figure is constructed by totaling the loans and securities banks carry as assets and comparing that total to the level one year earlier. Loans are included because they fund company operations, property purchases, and household spending. Securities are included because when banks buy bonds they are also extending credit, just to governments and other issuers rather than to direct borrowers. The combined total is the aggregate credit that the banking system pushes into the economy, and because bank lending creates money, credit growth and the growth of the broader money supply move closely together. The metric is included as a credit indicator because the supply of credit is a primary force in the credit cycle, and the rate of that supply matters more than its level. Arnold Ziegel's account of the 2008 crisis shows why the rate is the signal to watch. Easy credit late in a boom funds the very assets that are inflating, and the liberal lending that pushed credit to record highs was the fuel for the housing bubble. A high and rapidly rising level of credit is therefore not proof of health by itself, and a sharp roll-over in the growth rate from a high base has preceded past recessions, because the assets funded by that easy credit are the ones that later default.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Bank Credit Growth matters for sector investing because aggregate credit creation is one of the main drivers of the credit cycle, and the credit cycle sets which sectors lead. When credit is growing strongly, businesses and households can borrow to spend and invest, which fuels expansion and supports cyclical sectors and above all Financials, which earn on the lending. When credit growth rolls over, the fuel for spending and asset purchases fades, which signals a slowdown that favors defensive sectors. The metric matters most for Financials, because creating credit is the core business of banks, so credit growth drives their revenue directly. It matters for Real Estate, because property is bought largely with borrowed money, so the flow of aggregate credit supports property values. Ziegel's framing gives the metric a specific late-cycle warning. Companies and households finance their assets with a combination of credit and equity, and when credit is abundant they can carry more debt against the same cash flow, which works only as long as the underlying cash flow holds. Late in a boom credit often climbs to record highs and grows rapidly because lending is easy, and that easy credit funds the assets that later default when the cycle turns. A sharp roll-over in the growth rate from a high level is one of the clearest signals that the credit cycle is turning down, which is the point to rotate from cyclical toward defensive sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in credit growth being driven by borrower demand, reflecting confidence to spend and invest, or by banks changing their willingness to lend, reflecting credit conditions? A demand-driven change signals shifting confidence, while a supply-driven change signals shifting bank willingness, and the two carry different implications.

2. Is the growth coming from loans or from securities on bank balance sheets, since the two behave differently across the cycle? A rise in securities holdings can reflect banks parking cash in safe bonds rather than extending credit to the real economy, which is a weaker signal than a rise in loans.

3. Is credit funding productive activity such as business investment and home purchases, or speculative activity such as bidding up assets that are already inflating? Productive credit supports a durable expansion, while speculative credit late in a boom raises the risk of a bubble, in line with Ziegel's account of the 2008 crisis.

4. Is the level of credit at a record high and still growing rapidly, which can signal late-cycle excess, or is it growing from a normal base in line with the economy? A high and rapidly rising level is not proof of trouble by itself, but it warrants closer attention to whether the underlying cash flows can support the added debt.

5. Is loan quality holding up, or are delinquencies and defaults beginning to rise even as the credit total keeps growing? Rising defaults beneath a still-growing total can be an early warning that the credit cycle is about to turn down, because the assets funded by the prior easy credit are the ones that default first.

6. Is the roll-over in credit growth coming from a high base after a rapid expansion, or from an already moderate pace? A sharp roll-over from a high base has preceded past recessions and is a stronger warning than a slowdown from an already measured pace.

7. Is borrowing shifting away from banks toward non-bank lenders such as private credit funds, which would make bank credit understate the true level of lending in the economy? When credit migrates to the shadow banking system, the bank series alone misses part of the picture.

8. Is the credit growth consistent with what lending standards and credit spreads are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure may be misleading? Confirmation across the supply signal, the price signal, and the volume signal strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution.

9. Are interest rates affecting borrowing decisions, since higher rates raise the cost of credit and can slow growth even when the underlying demand for activity is healthy? Rising rates can suppress credit growth for reasons unrelated to confidence, which affects how the slowdown should be interpreted.

10. Is the change in credit growth large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the credit cycle, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary factors? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants a sector rotation, since credit growth can fluctuate for temporary reasons such as one-off refinancing waves.
