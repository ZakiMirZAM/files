# Financial Stress Index (St. Louis Fed)

## Part 1: Definition

The Financial Stress Index measures the overall level of strain in the financial system, boiled down into a single number. It is built by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis from 18 separate market series, including interest-rate spreads, yield differences, and measures of market volatility, blended together so that their common movement captures system-wide stress. The FRED series is STLFSI4, published weekly, and it is expressed in standard deviations from normal, where zero represents average financial conditions. A reading above zero means stress is above its historical average, and a reading below zero means conditions are calmer than average. In recent weeks it has sat modestly below zero (an approximate figure used here for illustration), signaling calmer-than-average conditions. The metric reveals, in one glance, whether the machinery of credit and funding is running smoothly or is under pressure.

The figure is constructed by combining the 18 underlying series into a single index that isolates the stress they share in common, so that a broad move across spreads and volatility registers as a rise in the index while an idiosyncratic move in one series does not. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because it captures the exact condition that Ziegel places at the center of financial crises: the moment when the suppliers of liquidity fear they will run out of cash. Ziegel states that companies and banks fail for one of three reasons, and the third, the fear among suppliers of liquidity, is the one that turns a manageable problem into a collapse. He notes that when liquidity in markets dries up, market risk becomes credit risk, because a security that cannot be sold must be held to maturity and its owner is suddenly exposed to the credit of the issuer. The stress index is the market-wide reading of that dynamic. A reading above zero and rising means spreads are widening and volatility is climbing together, which is the environment where confidence cracks and solvency problems that could have been survived turn into failures because funding disappears. A reading below zero means the opposite, a calm system where credit and funding flow freely and risk-taking is rewarded.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Financial Stress Index matters for sector investing because it is the fastest and broadest read on whether the financial system is calm or in crisis, and that condition overrides almost every other signal for the credit-sensitive sectors. Financials are affected most, because banks and other financial firms are the plumbing of the system, so rising stress hits their funding, their asset values, and their earnings all at once. Real Estate is affected next, because property depends on credit that freezes when stress spikes and financing costs jump. The metric captures Ziegel's deepest lesson, that the difference between a solvency problem and a fatal one is confidence and liquidity, and that even large, highly rated firms like Bear Stearns and Lehman failed not because they slowly lost money but because the suppliers of liquidity fled once fear took hold. A reading below zero signals the calm, risk-on environment where cyclical sectors and Financials lead, because credit is cheap, funding is easy, and the market is willing to take risk. A reading above zero and rising signals the opposite, a flight to safety where defensive sectors outperform and the priority shifts from earning returns to avoiding the sectors most exposed to a funding freeze. Because the index moves fast and captures the whole system at once, it is the metric that most cleanly signals when to abandon a risk-on posture and rotate to defense.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

|---|---|---|
| High-Yield Credit Spread | The stress index is a broad composite of many series, while the high-yield spread is the sharpest single read on default risk in the riskiest borrowers, so reading them together confirms whether rising stress is being driven by credit fear. Ziegel notes that when liquidity dries up market risk becomes credit risk, so the two move together in a genuine crisis. This combines a system-wide composite with a focused credit read. | When the index is calm AND the high-yield spread is narrow, both broad and credit-specific stress are low, which supports Financials and cyclicals. Draining reserves can be the cause of the fear the index registers. This combines the market's reaction with the underlying supply of liquidity. | When the index is calm AND reserves are ample, liquidity is abundant and markets are relaxed, which supports Financials and cyclicals. When the index is rising AND reserves are draining, a liquidity squeeze is feeding system stress, which confirms a funding scare and favors defensive sectors. |
| Net Percent of Banks Tightening C&I Standards | The stress index is a fast, market-based read, while the lending survey is a slower, behavior-based read on how banks are responding, so reading them together shows whether market stress has begun to change bank behavior. This combines a market signal with a banking-behavior signal. | When the index is calm AND banks are loosening standards, stress is low and credit is flowing, which supports Financials, Real Estate, and cyclicals. ## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the index rising because of a broad move across many of its 18 components or because of an outsized move in one or two, since a broad-based rise signals genuine system stress while a narrow one may be a localized problem? The index is designed to capture common stress, so the breadth of the move matters for interpretation.

2. Is a rising index reflecting a liquidity scare among the suppliers of funding, since Ziegel identifies that fear as the condition that turns survivable problems into failures? A stress rise driven by funding fear is more dangerous than one driven by ordinary volatility.

3. Is market risk beginning to turn into credit risk, since Ziegel notes that when liquidity dries up assets that were tradable must be held to maturity and their owners become exposed to issuer credit? A stress spike that makes assets hard to sell converts a market problem into a credit problem.

4. Is the level of stress being confirmed by credit spreads and bank lending behavior, or is the index moving on its own in a way that suggests a false alarm? Confirmation across the credit indicators strengthens the signal, while an isolated move in the index warrants caution.

5. Is the stress a fast-moving spike likely to reverse or the start of a sustained crisis, since the index can jump on a single event and then subside? Distinguishing a passing scare from a genuine turn is essential before rotating a portfolio to defense.

6. Is the calm in a below-zero reading genuine resilience or complacency late in a boom, since Ziegel warns that easy conditions late in a cycle can mask building risk? A very calm index can precede a crisis when leverage and concentrations have quietly built up.

7. Is the rise in stress reaching the banking system's funding and the real economy, or is it confined to financial markets so far? A stress spike matters most when it begins to change bank lending and hit employment and spending.

8. Are policymakers likely to intervene to calm the stress, since central-bank liquidity support has repeatedly capped stress spikes, as when the Federal Reserve backstopped funding in past crises? The prospect of intervention changes how a stress reading should be interpreted for positioning.

9. Is the stress concentrated in one part of the system, such as a single asset class or set of institutions, since Ziegel identifies concentration as the risk most likely to destroy a portfolio or a firm? Concentrated stress can spread through the system if the exposed institutions are large or interconnected.

10. Is the change in the index large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in financial conditions, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by a single event? Because the index moves quickly, only a sustained and meaningful shift warrants a decisive sector rotation.
