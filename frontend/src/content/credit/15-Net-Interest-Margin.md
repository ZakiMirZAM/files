# Net Interest Margin, US Banks

## Part 1: Definition

Net Interest Margin measures the core spread that a bank earns on its lending, expressed as a percentage of the assets that earn interest. It is the interest a bank collects on its loans and securities minus the interest it pays out on deposits and other borrowing, divided by its earning assets. The FRED series is USNIM, published quarterly, and it has run near 3 percent in recent quarters (an approximate figure used here for illustration). A margin of about 3 percent means that for every 100 dollars of loans and securities a bank holds, it keeps roughly 3 dollars of interest income after paying for its funding. This is the bank's version of a paycheck, and it is the single revenue line that most defines how a traditional bank makes money. The margin reveals how profitable the basic business of taking in deposits and making loans is at any point in the interest-rate and competitive cycle.

The figure is constructed by taking the difference between the interest a bank earns and the interest it pays, then dividing by the assets that generate the interest income, so it isolates the profitability of the lending spread from fee income and other sources. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because that spread is the engine of bank earnings and the first line of defense against loan losses. Ziegel's framing makes the stakes clear. A bank earns only a thin margin on each dollar of assets, which is exactly why it must run on heavy leverage to produce an acceptable return on equity, as the DuPont logic shows that return on equity is margin times asset turnover times leverage. The spread has to do two jobs before any profit reaches the owners. First it must absorb the expected losses that Ziegel says are covered by reserves out of earnings, and only what is left over funds the return on the equity cushion that stands behind the whole balance sheet. When the margin compresses, the cushion of revenue that must soak up loan losses shrinks, so the same rate of defaults bites harder and the return that attracts capital to the sector erodes. A wide, stable margin is a sign of a healthy lending business, while a squeezed margin is a warning that banks have less room to absorb the credit losses of a downturn.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Net Interest Margin matters for sector investing because it is the core profitability of banking, and the health of banks drives both the credit cycle and the earnings of the Financials sector directly. Financials are affected most, because the spread is the main revenue source for traditional banks, so a widening margin lifts bank earnings while a compressing margin squeezes them regardless of how much banks lend. Real Estate is affected next, because a bank earning a healthy spread is a bank that can keep extending the mortgage and commercial-property credit the sector depends on, while a bank whose margin is squeezed has less incentive and less capacity to take lending risk. The metric connects to Ziegel's lesson that the spread must first cover expected losses before it rewards the owners, so the margin is the buffer between loan losses and bank capital. A wide margin lets a bank absorb a normal cycle of defaults and still earn its cost of capital, which supports a durable expansion led by Financials. A compressed margin means the same wave of losses cuts closer to capital, which weakens the banking system and argues for rotating toward defensive sectors whose earnings do not depend on the lending spread. Reading the margin alongside the direction of interest rates and the shape of the yield curve sharpens the call, since banks borrow short and lend long.

|---|---|---|
| Net Charge-Off Rate, All Loans | The margin is the revenue cushion that must absorb loan losses, while the charge-off rate is the loss actually landing, so reading them together shows whether the spread is wide enough for the losses arriving. This combines the size of the buffer with the size of the loss. | When the margin is wide AND charge-offs are low, banks earn a comfortable spread over their losses, which supports Financials and credit-sensitive sectors. | 10Y Treasury | Banks borrow short and lend long, so the level of long-term rates relative to short-term funding costs drives the spread, which means the 10-year yield and the shape of the curve help explain why the margin is widening or compressing. This combines the outcome (the margin) with a leading cause (the curve). | When the margin is healthy AND the yield curve is steepening, the spread is likely to widen further, which supports Financials. ## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in the margin coming from the asset side (rates earned on loans and securities) or the funding side (rates paid on deposits and borrowing), since the two carry different implications for how durable the change is? A margin widened by rising loan rates behaves differently from one narrowed by rising deposit costs.

2. Is the margin wide enough to cover the loan losses that the credit cycle is likely to bring, since Ziegel's logic is that the spread must first absorb expected losses before it rewards the owners? A margin that looks healthy in calm times can be inadequate once defaults rise.

3. Is the shape of the yield curve supporting or squeezing the margin, given that banks borrow short and lend long, so a flat or inverted curve compresses the spread even when lending is healthy? The curve is often the reason a margin moves, so it must be read alongside the margin itself.

4. Is competition for deposits forcing banks to pay up for funding, since intense deposit competition squeezes the margin from the funding side even when loan rates are stable? Rising funding costs can erode the spread independent of what banks earn on their assets.

5. Is a wide margin being earned by taking on riskier loans at higher rates, since a spread that looks attractive may simply be compensation for credit risk that will show up later as losses? A high margin built on risky lending can be a warning rather than a sign of strength.

6. Is the margin being supported by fee income and other revenue that the spread measure excludes, since a bank with strong non-interest income can withstand a thinner lending spread? The margin alone can understate the earnings power of a diversified bank.

7. Is the aggregate margin hiding wide differences between banks, since a healthy average can mask individual institutions whose spreads are already squeezed? The system can look profitable while the weakest banks are losing money on their lending.

8. Is the margin compression a temporary effect of the rate cycle or a structural decline driven by lasting competition and disintermediation? A cyclical squeeze reverses with the curve, while a structural one keeps pressure on bank earnings.

9. Is the change in the margin consistent with what loan growth, deposit growth, and loan losses are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure is misleading? Confirmation across the banking indicators strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution.

10. Is the change in the margin large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in bank profitability, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by the rate cycle? Only a sustained and meaningful shift in the spread warrants a sector rotation, since the margin can move for temporary reasons.
