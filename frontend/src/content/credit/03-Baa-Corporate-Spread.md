# Baa Corporate Spread over the 10-Year Treasury

## Part 1: Definition

The Baa Corporate Spread measures the extra yield that investors demand to hold long-maturity bonds issued by companies at the lowest rung of investment grade instead of the 10-year US Treasury, expressed in percentage points. The series shown is Moody's Seasoned Baa Corporate Bond Yield minus the 10-Year Treasury Constant Maturity yield, reported daily on FRED as BAA10Y. In a calm market in mid-2026 it sat near 1.8 percentage points (an illustrative recent value), meaning borrowers rated Baa paid roughly 1.8 points more than the government for long-term money. Baa (equivalent to BBB from Standard and Poors) is the last tier before a bond falls out of investment grade into high yield, so this spread prices the risk of the weakest borrowers that most conservative investors are still allowed to hold. Because Moody's has published Baa corporate bond yields since 1919, this is the longest continuous credit-spread history available, which makes it less a snapshot of today than a durable read on how much compensation the market pays for lower-investment-grade risk across entire cycles.

The figure is constructed by taking Moody's index of seasoned, long-maturity Baa-rated corporate bonds, reading its average yield, and subtracting the yield on the 10-year Treasury. Unlike the option-adjusted spreads on the high-yield and investment-grade indexes, this is a simple yield difference rather than a precisely modeled spread, so it is a little cruder, but that simplicity is what lets it stretch back over a century. It is included as a credit indicator because it turns Arnold Ziegel's risk rating into a price with the longest possible memory. Ziegel explains that a risk rating or debt rating is the market's estimate of the probability of default and the loss if default occurs, and Baa sits at the borderline of investment grade, the point where a downgrade would push a bond into junk and force many pension funds and insurers to sell. Reading this spread across cycles lets an investor see whether today's compensation for that borderline risk is historically generous or stingy. A century of history shows distinct regimes: long stretches of narrow spreads when confidence and easy credit prevail, and violent widenings in the Depression, the 1970s, 2008, and 2020 when default fear returned. Because the level can be compared to a hundred years of readings, the Baa spread answers a question the shorter series cannot, which is whether the market is being paid enough for lower-investment-grade risk relative to how it has been paid across the full sweep of credit history.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The Baa Corporate Spread matters for sector investing because its long history lets an investor judge not just the direction of credit stress but the regime the market is in, which shapes how aggressive or defensive a sector stance should be. A spread that is low by its own recent standards may still be low or high relative to a century of readings, and that longer frame tells you whether the market is being paid enough for lower-investment-grade risk. When the spread is narrow relative to history, capital is cheap for even the weakest investment-grade borrowers, which supports credit-sensitive and cyclical sectors that carry heavy debt. When it widens toward levels seen in past crises, the market is repricing the borderline tier for real default risk, which favors defensive sectors. The metric matters with particular force for Financials, because banks and insurers hold Baa-rated bonds and lend to Baa-rated borrowers, so a widening spread both marks down their portfolios and warns of the fallen-angel downgrades that force selling. It matters for Real Estate and Utilities, which are large, debt-heavy issuers clustered in the Baa tier and dependent on continuous refinancing, so their cost of the second way out rises directly with the spread. The regime lens gives the metric its edge, because the same absolute level means something very different in a low-spread era than in a high-spread one, and Ziegel's warning about easy credit late in a boom is precisely a warning that a historically narrow Baa spread can signal complacency about default risk rather than genuine safety. That makes the Baa spread a valuable input for setting the overall cyclical-versus-defensive posture across the credit cycle.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is today's spread narrow or wide relative to a century of Baa readings, not just its own recent range? The long history is the whole value of this series, because a level that looks normal against the past few years may be historically low and signal complacency about default risk.

2. Is the current level consistent with a low-spread confidence regime or a high-spread stress regime, and which way is the regime shifting? Ziegel's warning about easy credit late in a boom means a historically narrow spread can precede a turn rather than confirm safety.

3. Is the spread widening because Baa borrowers are genuinely deteriorating, or because the 10-year Treasury yield is moving and dragging the simple yield difference with it? Since this is a plain yield spread rather than an option-adjusted one, changes in the underlying rate can distort it, so the source of the move must be identified.

4. Is the borderline tier being repriced for the risk of fallen-angel downgrades that would force mandate-bound investors to sell? Baa sits one notch above junk, and Ziegel notes many pension funds and insurers cannot hold bonds below investment grade, so pressure here can cascade into forced selling.

5. Are the Baa issuers most exposed to the widening ones whose leverage is high relative to their cash-flow volatility, which Ziegel identifies as the mismatch that leads to downgrades? A spread move matters most for issuers whose capital structure no longer fits their business risk.

6. Is a widening spread raising refinancing costs enough to threaten the second way out for the large, debt-heavy Baa issuers clustered in Real Estate and Utilities? These sectors roll their debt continuously, so a sustained widening pressures even fundamentally sound operators.

7. Is the Baa spread confirming what the broad investment-grade and high-yield spreads are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure may be misleading? Confirmation across the quality ladder strengthens the regime read, while divergence warrants caution.

8. Do banks and insurers hold enough Baa-rated exposure that a widening spread would mark down their portfolios and threaten their capital? Ziegel warns that losses eating through capital are how institutions fail, so the size of the borderline exposure relative to capital is what matters.

9. Is the widening spread reflecting a concentration of stress in one industry within the Baa universe, or is it broad across many sectors? A downgrade wave concentrated in one sector carries different implications than an economy-wide repricing of borderline credit.

10. Is the move in the spread large and sustained enough to mark a genuine change of regime, or is it a short-term fluctuation that history would treat as noise? Only a durable shift relative to the long record warrants a full cyclical-to-defensive rotation, since the value of this series lies in its regime signal rather than its daily wiggles.
