# Delinquency Rate, All Bank Loans

## Part 1: Definition

The Delinquency Rate on All Bank Loans measures the share of loans and leases held by US commercial banks on which the borrower has fallen behind on payments, expressed as a percentage of the total loan book. A loan is counted as delinquent once it is past due, generally thirty days or more, and still accruing or recently placed on nonaccrual status. The FRED series is DRALACBN, published quarterly by the Federal Reserve, and it typically sits near 1.4 percent in a healthy economy (an approximate recent reading), rising toward 5 to 7 percent in a severe downturn as it did after 2008. The rate reveals how many borrowers have begun to miss payments, which is the first visible crack in credit quality before losses are actually realized.

The figure is constructed by dividing the dollar value of loans that are past due by the total dollar value of all loans and leases on bank balance sheets, then aggregating across every reporting bank. It captures the whole loan book at once, business loans, commercial real estate, mortgages, and consumer credit, so it is the broadest single gauge of borrower distress in the banking system. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because a missed payment is the earliest sign that a borrower's operating cash flow has faltered. In Ziegel's framework the first way out of any loan is repayment from the borrower's own cash flow, and a delinquency is the moment that first way out begins to fail. Because banks report delinquencies before they write the loans off, the rate leads the charge-off rate by one to several quarters and marks the down-leg of the credit cycle before losses crystallize into lost capital. A low and stable rate is not proof of safety by itself, because delinquencies can be masked late in a boom by easy refinancing, so the direction and the speed of change matter more than the level alone.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The delinquency rate matters for sector investing because it is the earliest quantitative confirmation that the credit cycle is turning down, and the credit cycle drives which sectors lead. When delinquencies are low and flat, borrowers are servicing their debt from cash flow, banks earn steadily on their loan books, and cyclical sectors that depend on credit-fueled spending can lead. When delinquencies begin to climb, the first way out is failing for a growing share of borrowers, and that pressure lands first and hardest on Financials, because a rising delinquency rate directly foreshadows higher provisions, lower earnings, and eventually charge-offs that eat into bank capital. It lands next on Real Estate, because property is bought largely with borrowed money and rising delinquencies signal both weakening collateral and tightening credit. The metric also carries a late-cycle warning that ties back to Ziegel's account of easy credit extended late in a boom: underwriting standards loosen when times are good, so the loans made at the top of the cycle are the ones that go delinquent first when conditions turn. Because it is a leading, broad, and borrower-level gauge of distress, the delinquency rate is a valuable input for positioning between credit-sensitive cyclicals and defensive sectors before the deterioration is fully priced.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in the delinquency rate concentrated in one loan category such as commercial real estate or consumer credit, or is it broad across the whole loan book? A concentrated rise points to a specific stress that may stay contained, while a broad rise signals a general turn in the credit cycle.

2. Are delinquencies rising because borrowers' operating cash flow is genuinely failing, or because a temporary shock such as a natural disaster or a payment-processing disruption has delayed payments? Only a genuine failure of the first way out signals a durable turn, while a temporary disruption reverses quickly.

3. Is the current low delinquency rate genuine, or is it being masked by easy refinancing that lets weak borrowers roll their debt rather than default? Late in a boom, abundant refinancing can hold delinquencies down even as underlying credit quality erodes, which makes a low reading misleading.

4. How fast is the rate rising, and is the pace consistent with a normal late-cycle drift or with an accelerating deterioration? A slow drift can accompany a mature but healthy expansion, while a sharp acceleration warns that the down-leg of the credit cycle has begun.

5. Do the loans now going delinquent share the loose underwriting standards of the late-boom period, confirming Ziegel's point that easy credit extended late in a cycle defaults first? If the delinquencies trace back to the most recent vintage of loose lending, the deterioration is likely to widen as more of that vintage seasons.

6. Are rising delinquencies being met by adequate bank reserves that cover expected losses, or are they large enough to threaten capital, which is meant to cover only unexpected losses? Reserves absorb expected loss, but a delinquency wave that overwhelms reserves eats into capital and forces banks to retrench.

7. Is the delinquency rate confirmed by the charge-off rate and by market-priced credit spreads, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure is misleading? Confirmation across leading and lagging credit indicators strengthens the signal, while divergence warrants caution before rotating sectors.

8. Is the rise in delinquencies accompanied by rising unemployment, since job losses are a primary cause of household and consumer loan distress? A delinquency rise driven by a weakening labor market is more likely to deepen than one occurring while employment stays strong.

9. Is credit that would otherwise show as delinquent migrating to non-bank lenders such as private credit funds, so that the bank series understates the true level of borrower distress in the economy? When lending shifts outside banks, the bank delinquency series alone can miss part of the picture.

10. Is the change in the delinquency rate large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in credit quality, or is it a one- or two-quarter fluctuation driven by temporary factors? Only a sustained and meaningful rise warrants a defensive sector rotation, since delinquency rates can wobble quarter to quarter for reasons that reverse.
