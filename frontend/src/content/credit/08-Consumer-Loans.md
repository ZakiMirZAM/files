# Consumer Loan Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Consumer Loan Growth measures how fast the stock of consumer loans on US bank balance sheets is expanding or shrinking. The base figure is the dollar amount of loans banks make directly to households, mainly credit cards, auto loans, and other personal installment credit, reported by the Federal Reserve as FRED series CCLACBW027SBOG for all commercial banks. The series is published weekly and stood near 2.0 trillion dollars in mid-2026 (an approximate, illustrative level). It can be viewed as a level (the dollars outstanding) or, more usefully for the credit cycle, as a year-over-year growth rate (the percentage change from twelve months earlier), which recently ran in the low single digits. The metric reveals how willing households are to borrow and how willing banks are to lend to them, which is one of the most direct reads on the strength of household credit demand and the appetite for consumer risk in the banking system.

The figure is constructed by totaling the consumer loans banks hold as assets and comparing that total to the level one year earlier. It belongs on a credit and banking dashboard because consumer lending is where Arnold Ziegel says the credit decision has been most fully industrialized. Consumer loans are small relative to corporate loans, so lenders cannot afford deep individual analysis and instead rely on statistical models that turn income, work history, residency stability, and payment history into a credit score, then approve or decline against that score and the lender's own appetite for risk. Growth in this series therefore reflects both household demand and how loose or tight those model-driven standards have become. Rising growth is not automatically healthy. Ziegel's central warning about consumer credit is overextension: the subprime episode of 2007 and 2008 happened because underwriting became very liberal, borrowers were given loans they could not afford from their own cash flow, and the loans were made on the assumption that collateral values would keep rising. That is one way out lending, where the only source of repayment is the sale or refinancing of the asset, and it fails the moment collateral values reverse. The rate of change and the quality of the credit behind the growth matter far more than the level alone.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Consumer Loan Growth matters for sector investing because household borrowing funds a large share of discretionary spending, so this series is a direct read on the fuel behind Consumer Discretionary and a coincident read on the consumer-lending profits inside Financials. When consumer credit is growing at a steady pace, households can finance cars, appliances, and everyday purchases, which supports Consumer Discretionary and the banks earning on the lending. When growth accelerates while underwriting loosens, it can signal the overextension Ziegel describes, where households take on more debt than their income can service and the loans depend on rising asset values rather than cash flow. When growth stalls or turns negative, it usually means banks are tightening model-driven standards or households are retrenching, which cuts discretionary spending and raises loan losses at consumer-heavy lenders. Because consumer loans are underwritten by score-based models that assume the future looks like the past, this metric carries a specific danger: the models can badly understate risk late in a boom, so a rapid rise in consumer credit alongside falling credit standards is a warning rather than a green light. Read this way, the series helps position between the credit-sensitive consumer economy and defensive sectors across the cycle.

It supports Consumer Discretionary and consumer lenders in the short run, but rapid growth alongside easing underwriting is the overextension Ziegel warns about, the pattern that preceded the subprime failures. Whether the borrowers can service the debt from income, rather than from rising asset values, must be checked closely. | Consumer Discretionary, Financials, Industrials | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Energy, Materials, Information Technology, Communication Services |

Households are financing purchases at a manageable rate, banks earn consistent consumer-lending revenue, and delinquencies stay contained. This is the sustainable middle of the cycle where the score-based models are working as intended. Consumer Discretionary and consumer lenders benefit without the warning signs of excess. | Consumer Discretionary, Financials | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Energy, Materials, Information Technology, Communication Services |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer Discretionary | Discretionary purchases such as cars, appliances, furniture, and much credit-card spending are financed with consumer credit, so growing consumer loans directly fund the sales that drive discretionary earnings. When consumer credit contracts, households cut the financed purchases first, and the sector's revenue falls. This is the sector most directly tied to the metric. | Buy when consumer credit growth is moderate to strong with contained delinquencies. Avoid when growth turns flat or negative, which signals households pulling back. |
| Financials (consumer lenders and card issuers) | Credit cards and consumer installment loans are a core, high-margin earnings stream for many banks and card issuers, so growing consumer credit lifts interest and fee income while contraction brings rising charge-offs. The overextension Ziegel describes hits these lenders directly when model-driven underwriting proves too loose. Earnings move with both the volume and the quality of consumer credit. | Buy when growth is moderate and underwriting is disciplined. Avoid when rapid growth rides on loosening standards, or when growth contracts and charge-offs rise. |
| Industrials (autos and consumer durables supply chains) | A large share of auto and durable-goods demand is financed with consumer credit, so the parts, equipment, and assembly activity feeding those products rise and fall with consumer lending. When consumer credit contracts, big-ticket financed purchases drop and industrial demand weakens. The link runs through financed durable-goods sales. | Buy when consumer credit growth supports strong auto and durables demand. Avoid when it contracts and financed purchases fall. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples demand comes from everyday necessities that households buy regardless of credit conditions, so the sector gains relative appeal when contracting consumer credit signals a household slowdown. Its defensive characteristics dominate when discretionary, credit-driven spending weakens. It becomes a destination when consumer credit slows or contracts. | Buy when consumer credit growth is slowing or contracting. Underperforms when strong consumer credit growth drives discretionary leadership. |
| Health Care | Health care demand is driven by medical need rather than household borrowing, so the sector behaves defensively when consumer credit stress signals broader weakness. Its earnings stability becomes valuable when the credit-driven consumer economy falters. The sector is insulated from the consumer-credit channel. | Buy when consumer credit growth is slowing or contracting. Defensive default in a consumer-led slowdown. |
| Utilities | Utility demand is stable and independent of household borrowing, so the sector holds up defensively when consumer credit signals a slowdown. Its own heavy borrowing means broad credit stress can raise its financing costs, but its cash flows do not depend on the consumer credit cycle. The sector is a refuge when consumer credit contracts. | Buy when consumer credit growth is slowing. Avoid when strong growth drives discretionary leadership. |
| Communication Services | Household spending on subscriptions, devices, and services is supported when consumer credit is flowing and pressured when households retrench, though the sector's recurring-revenue components are more resilient than one-time discretionary purchases. The connection is partial. The sector is moderately sensitive through consumer spending power. | Modest sensitivity. Trim discretionary-facing components when consumer credit contracts. |
| Materials | Consumer durables and housing-related goods that consume materials are often financed, so materials demand follows financed consumer purchases indirectly. The link is weaker than for autos or discretionary retail and runs through the broader consumer economy. The effect is secondary. | Low to modest sensitivity. Follows the broad consumer cycle more than consumer credit directly. |
| Energy | Energy demand is driven by economic activity and prices rather than consumer borrowing, so the connection to consumer credit is faint and runs only through overall consumer strength. Consumer credit growth is a secondary consideration for the sector. | Low direct sensitivity. Follows energy demand and prices more than consumer lending. |
| Information Technology | Large tech companies earn from enterprise and platform revenue more than from financed consumer purchases, so the sector is largely insulated from the consumer-credit channel, though consumer-hardware components feel it and the broad economy matters indirectly. The connection is weak. | Low direct sensitivity. Mostly insulated from the consumer-credit cycle. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside Consumer Loan Growth | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Delinquency Rate, All Bank Loans | Consumer loan growth shows how fast households are borrowing, while the delinquency rate shows whether they are keeping up on the loans already made, so reading them together separates healthy demand from the overextension Ziegel warns about. The two together reveal both the volume and the quality of consumer credit. This joins the quantity of lending to its performance. | When consumer credit growth is moderate AND delinquencies are low, households are borrowing within their means, which supports Consumer Discretionary and consumer lenders. When growth has been rapid AND delinquencies are now rising, the score-based models understated the risk and overextension is surfacing, which confirms a defensive rotation. |
| Personal Savings Rate | Consumer loan growth reflects how much households are borrowing, while the savings rate reflects how much they are holding back from spending, so reading them together shows whether the consumer is leaning on credit or on their own funds. The two together reveal the financial posture of the household sector. This connects borrowing to saving behavior. | When consumer credit growth is strong AND the savings rate is low, households are spending freely on credit, which supports Consumer Discretionary but can signal late-cycle overextension. When credit is contracting AND the savings rate is rising, households are retrenching and paying down debt, which favors Consumer Staples and Health Care. |
| Unemployment Rate | Consumer loans are repaid from household income, so the unemployment rate is the single most important check on whether the cash flow behind consumer credit is secure, exactly the cash-flow-versus-collateral distinction Ziegel stresses. The two together show whether the borrowers can actually service their debt. This ties the loan book to the income that repays it. | When consumer credit growth is healthy AND unemployment is low, incomes support the debt, which sustains discretionary spending. When credit has grown rapidly AND unemployment is rising, the income behind the loans is eroding and defaults will climb, which strongly favors defensives over Consumer Discretionary and consumer lenders. |
| Net % of Banks Tightening Standards (SLOOS) | Consumer loan growth measures the credit that actually flowed, while the SLOOS survey measures banks' stated willingness to lend to households, so reading them together shows whether a slowdown is driven by tighter underwriting or weaker demand. The survey often leads the loan data. This connects lender willingness to realized lending. | When consumer credit growth is steady AND banks are not tightening, the expansion has room to run, supporting Consumer Discretionary. When banks are sharply tightening consumer standards AND growth is still positive, the survey is warning of a coming contraction, giving an early signal to rotate toward defensives. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the growth in consumer credit being driven by genuine household demand from confident, employed borrowers, or by banks loosening the score-based standards that gate approvals? Demand-driven growth reflects a healthy consumer, while supply-driven growth from looser underwriting is the overextension that precedes losses.

2. Are the loans being repaid from household income and cash flow, or do they depend on rising collateral values such as home or car prices? Loans that rely on rising asset values rather than income are one way out lending, which fails when values reverse, exactly as it did in the subprime episode.

3. How well are the statistical credit-score models capturing current risk, given that they assume the future resembles the past? Late in a boom the models can badly understate default risk, so rapid growth built on optimistic scores warrants extra caution.

4. Is loan quality holding up, or are delinquencies and charge-offs rising even as the consumer loan total keeps growing? Rising defaults beneath a still-growing total is an early warning that underwriting was too loose and the cycle is about to turn.

5. Is household leverage rising faster than income, so that debt-service burdens are climbing relative to what borrowers can afford? When debt grows faster than the income that services it, households become fragile to any shock, which is the core of consumer overextension.

6. Which segment is driving the change, credit cards, auto loans, or other installment credit, and does any segment show concentrated stress? A problem concentrated in one segment, such as subprime auto, can signal deteriorating quality that the aggregate number hides.

7. Is consumer borrowing shifting toward non-bank lenders such as fintech and buy-now-pay-later providers, which would make the bank series understate true household credit? When credit migrates outside the banking system, the bank data alone misses part of the household leverage building up.

8. How exposed is the household sector to rising interest rates, since higher rates raise the cost of variable-rate consumer debt and can push stretched borrowers into default? Rising rates can turn a serviceable loan book into a stressed one even without a rise in unemployment.

9. Is consumer credit growth consistent with what delinquency rates, the savings rate, and the SLOOS survey are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one signal is misleading? Confirmation across indicators strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution before repositioning.

10. Is the change in consumer credit growth large and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the household credit cycle, or a short-term fluctuation from seasonal or temporary factors? Only a sustained, meaningful shift warrants rotating between Consumer Discretionary, consumer lenders, and defensives, since consumer credit can move for temporary reasons.
