# Commercial and Industrial Loan Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Commercial and Industrial Loan Growth measures how fast banks are lending to businesses, shown as the year-over-year percentage change in commercial and industrial (C&I) loans at all US commercial banks. C&I loans are the credit banks extend to companies to fund operations, inventory, equipment, and expansion, as opposed to loans for real estate or to households. The FRED series id is BUSLOANS, reported weekly, with a level recently near 2.8 trillion dollars (approximate) and a year-over-year growth rate that swings widely across the cycle, from double-digit growth in booms to sharp contraction in recessions. The growth rate is the view that matters, because the level rises over time in any growing economy, so the rate of change is what signals a turn. The metric reveals the state of business borrowing, which reflects both how much credit banks are willing to supply to companies and how much companies want to invest.

The figure is constructed by totaling the C&I loans banks carry as assets and comparing that total to the level one year earlier. It is the business-lending slice of total bank credit, and it is tracked on its own because business borrowing behaves differently from mortgage and consumer credit and tends to turn earlier in the cycle. C&I loan growth carries both a demand signal and a supply signal at once. On the demand side, companies borrow when they are confident enough to invest in operations and expansion, so growth reflects business appetite for risk. On the supply side, banks lend to businesses only when they judge the borrowers can repay, so growth also reflects bank willingness to extend credit. Arnold Ziegel's framing sits directly on this loan type. Working capital loans that fund inventory and receivables are generally unsecured and depend on the borrower's cash flow, and the credit analyst's job is to judge whether that cash flow can service the debt. When banks tighten their C&I standards, growth falls even when firms still want to borrow, and Ziegel's warning that short-term loans to weak borrowers have a tendency not to get paid means a contraction in C&I lending often precedes rising business defaults. Because business investment turns before consumer spending, a roll-over in C&I loan growth is one of the earlier precursors of a recession.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Commercial and Industrial Loan Growth matters for sector investing because business lending is the channel through which credit funds the capital spending that drives the cyclical economy, and it tends to turn before the broader credit aggregate. When C&I loans are growing, companies are investing in operations, equipment, and expansion, which supports Industrials, Materials, and the Financials that book the loans. When C&I growth rolls over, business investment is pulling back, which is an early signal of a slowdown that favors defensive sectors. The metric matters most for Financials, because C&I lending is a core banking business, and for Industrials and Materials, because their demand is driven by the very capital spending that these loans fund. Ziegel's account gives the metric a specific edge. A company's debt capacity is a function of its free cash flow, and the natural capital structure of a business follows the volatility of that cash flow. When C&I lending contracts, it usually reflects banks tightening standards because they doubt future cash flow, or firms retreating because they see weaker demand, and both point the same way. Because business borrowing reflects investment plans made before the spending actually happens, and because banks pull back on business credit before losses appear, a contraction in C&I loan growth is a leading precursor of a recession and a signal to rotate from cyclical toward defensive sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in C&I loan growth being driven by business demand for investment, reflecting confidence to expand, or by banks changing their willingness to lend to companies, reflecting credit conditions? A demand-driven change signals shifting business confidence, while a supply-driven change signals shifting bank willingness, and the two carry different implications.

2. Is business borrowing funding productive investment such as equipment and expansion, or defensive needs such as covering operating cash deficits and drawing down credit lines to build precautionary cash? Borrowing to invest signals confidence, while borrowing to survive can signal stress even as the loan total rises.

3. Is C&I loan growth leading the broader credit aggregate up or down, since business lending tends to turn first? A divergence between C&I growth and total bank credit can give an early read on the direction of the cycle before the broad total confirms it.

4. Are banks tightening their C&I standards at the same time growth is slowing, which would confirm that the slowdown is supply-driven rather than a temporary dip in demand? When standards tighten alongside falling C&I growth, business credit is being withdrawn and the signal is stronger.

5. Is loan quality holding up, or are business delinquencies beginning to rise even as C&I loans keep growing? Because working-capital loans depend on borrower cash flow, rising delinquencies beneath a growing total can be an early warning that the weaker borrowers funded during the easy period are starting to fail.

6. Is the growth concentrated in strong, cash-generative borrowers or in weaker firms that took on debt against uncertain future cash flow? Ziegel's point that short-term loans to weak borrowers tend not to get paid means the composition of the borrowers matters as much as the volume of loans.

7. Are large companies shifting between bank C&I loans and the corporate bond market, which would make the bank series understate or overstate true business borrowing? When firms move to bonds or commercial paper, C&I loan growth can fall for reasons unrelated to a genuine pullback in business investment.

8. Is business lending shifting toward non-bank lenders such as private credit funds, which would make the bank C&I series miss part of the true level of corporate borrowing? When business credit migrates to the shadow banking system, the bank series alone understates the picture.

9. Does the C&I loan signal agree with what lending standards, credit spreads, and business-delinquency data are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure is misleading? Confirmation across the supply, price, and quality signals strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution.

10. Is the change in C&I loan growth large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in business investment, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by temporary factors such as a wave of refinancing or a one-off drawdown? Only a sustained and meaningful change warrants a sector rotation, since business lending can swing for temporary reasons.
