# Delinquency Rate, Single-Family Mortgages

## Part 1: Definition

The Delinquency Rate on Single-Family Residential Mortgages measures the share of home loans held by US commercial banks on which the household borrower has fallen behind on payments, expressed as a percentage of the total single-family mortgage book. A mortgage is counted as delinquent once it is past due, generally thirty days or more. The FRED series is DRSFRMACBS, published quarterly by the Federal Reserve, and it typically sits near 1.5 to 2 percent in a healthy housing market (an approximate recent reading), but it surged above 11 percent in 2009 and 2010 during the housing crisis. The rate reveals how many households have stopped servicing their home loans, which is the clearest single gauge of stress on household collateral and on the largest debt most families ever carry.

The figure is constructed by dividing the dollar value of past-due single-family mortgages by the total dollar value of such mortgages on bank balance sheets, then aggregating across every reporting bank. It isolates residential housing credit from business and commercial property lending, so it tracks the household and housing cycle directly. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because residential mortgage credit sits at the epicenter of Ziegel's 2008 case study. Ziegel describes how many subprime and even prime mortgages were made with only one way out, the sale or refinancing of the home based on steadily rising market values, while repayment from the borrower's own cash flow was often not a serious consideration. When home prices stopped rising and then fell, that single way out closed, and losses followed because the loans were never underwritten to be repaid from income. The single-family mortgage delinquency rate is therefore the clearest real-time gauge of household collateral stress, and its behavior in the last crisis is the reason it is watched so closely. A low rate reflects rising or stable home values and healthy household cash flow together, so a turn in this series carries an unusually heavy warning.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The single-family mortgage delinquency rate matters for sector investing because housing is the largest asset and the largest debt for most households, so stress here radiates through the whole economy and the credit cycle. When mortgage delinquencies are low, households are servicing their home loans from income and home values are supporting the collateral, which underpins consumer confidence and spending and lets credit-sensitive cyclicals lead. When they begin to rise, the pressure lands first on Real Estate, because falling home values and rising defaults directly impair property, and on Financials, because mortgages are a huge share of the loan book and the mortgage losses of 2008 nearly destroyed the banking system through exactly this channel. It lands next on Consumer Discretionary, because households that are underwater or behind on their mortgage cut discretionary spending sharply. The metric embodies two of Ziegel's central warnings at once. It shows the danger of one-way-out lending, where repayment depends solely on rising collateral rather than borrower cash flow, and it shows concentration risk, because banks that concentrated mortgage exposure relative to their capital were the ones that failed. Because it is the clearest gauge of household collateral stress and because its last major turn triggered a systemic crisis, the single-family mortgage delinquency rate is a decisive input for positioning between housing-exposed cyclicals and defensive sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Are the mortgages going delinquent one-way-out loans whose repayment depended on rising home values rather than borrower cash flow, as in Ziegel's 2008 case study? If repayment never rested on income, a fall in home prices closes the only exit and losses can be severe, so the underwriting basis of the loans matters as much as the delinquency level.

2. Are home values rising, flat, or falling, and how does that trend affect the collateral behind mortgages where the home is the primary source of repayment? Falling home values remove the cushion that lets lenders recover on default, turning delinquencies into realized losses.

3. Is the rise in mortgage delinquencies driven by falling household cash flow, such as job losses, or by rising interest rates that have lifted payments on adjustable loans? A cash-flow failure signals a durable downturn, while a payment shock from resetting rates can ease if rates fall or if borrowers refinance.

4. Are the delinquencies concentrated in a particular region, loan type, or vintage of loose underwriting, or are they broad across the mortgage book? A concentrated rise may stay contained, while a broad rise across a recent loose vintage warns of a widening housing credit problem.

5. Do banks hold mortgage exposure that is large relative to their capital, creating the concentration risk that Ziegel identifies as the usual cause of bank failure? The mortgage losses of 2008 destroyed institutions whose housing exposure overwhelmed their capital, so concentration is what turns a delinquency wave into a solvency crisis.

6. Are rising mortgage delinquencies being met by adequate reserves for expected loss, or are they large enough to threaten the capital that covers only unexpected loss? Reserves absorb a normal level of housing losses, but a delinquency surge beyond what reserves cover eats into capital and forces banks to retrench.

7. Is mortgage credit being extended outside the banking system, through nonbank originators and securitization, so that the bank series understates the true level of housing credit stress as it did before 2008? When origination moves to the shadow banking system, the bank delinquency series alone can miss the real risk.

8. Is the rise in mortgage delinquencies feeding a negative wealth effect that is cutting consumer spending, since housing is most households' largest asset? A housing downturn that erodes household net worth tends to broaden into weaker discretionary spending and a general slowdown.

9. Is the mortgage delinquency rate confirmed by the all-loans delinquency rate, the charge-off rate, and credit spreads, or is housing moving on its own ahead of the rest of the credit system? Housing leading the other credit indicators is the pattern that preceded the 2008 crisis, so a divergence with housing turning first is an especially serious warning.

10. Is the change in the mortgage delinquency rate large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the housing credit cycle, or is it a one- or two-quarter fluctuation driven by a temporary factor? Only a sustained and meaningful rise warrants a defensive rotation, since the series can move quarter to quarter for reasons that reverse.
