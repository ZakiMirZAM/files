# Bank Equity to Total Assets

## Part 1: Definition

Bank Equity to Total Assets measures how much of the banking system's assets are funded by the owners' own money rather than by borrowed money. It is a ratio: total common equity divided by total assets across US commercial banks, reported as a percentage. The FRED series is EQTA, published quarterly, and it has run near 11 to 12 percent in recent quarters (an approximate figure used here for illustration). A reading of roughly 11 percent means that for every 100 dollars of assets a bank holds, only about 11 dollars is the owners' cushion and the other 89 dollars is funded by deposits and other borrowing. That translates into effective leverage of roughly nine times: assets are about nine times equity. The ratio is the rawest read on capital adequacy, because it answers a single question, how thick is the buffer that stands between a bank's losses and its depositors.

The figure is constructed by adding up the equity that all commercial banks carry on their balance sheets and dividing by their combined assets. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because it captures the defining feature of banking, which is that banks operate on far more leverage than ordinary companies. Ziegel explains why this is not reckless in itself. A company can safely support a lot of debt when its cash flow is stable or its assets are liquid, and banks meet both conditions, so their natural capital structure carries heavy leverage. The DuPont logic makes the reason concrete: return on equity is profit margin times asset turnover times leverage, and a bank earns only a razor-thin spread on each dollar of assets, so it must stack many dollars of assets on each dollar of equity to earn an acceptable return for its owners. The thin cushion is only safe if the assets behind it are liquid and high in quality. Ziegel's crisis lesson is the counterpoint: reserves are set aside to cover the expected losses in a loan book, but equity capital exists to absorb the unexpected losses, and when concentrated losses grew larger than the capital of firms like Lehman and Bear Stearns, the buffer was eaten through and the firms failed. So a falling or already thin equity ratio is the raw warning that the buffer may not be large enough for the risk the system is carrying.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Bank Equity to Total Assets matters for sector investing because the thickness of the banking system's capital cushion sets how much lending the system can safely do and how well it can survive a shock, and both drive the credit cycle that leads sector performance. Financials are affected most directly, because equity is the base on which a bank builds its whole balance sheet, so a well-capitalized system can keep lending and paying dividends through stress while a thinly capitalized one must retrench to protect its ratios. Real Estate is affected next, because property is bought largely with borrowed money and a capital-constrained bank pulls back exactly the mortgage and commercial-property credit that the sector depends on. The metric carries Ziegel's core warning about capital and concentration. Late in a boom, banks tend to let leverage stretch and let concentrations build, and a thin equity ratio at that moment means a downturn can wipe out capital quickly, which is precisely the pattern that felled over-leveraged institutions in past crises. A strong, rising capital cushion supports a durable expansion led by Financials and credit-sensitive cyclicals, while a thin or falling cushion argues for rotating toward defensive sectors whose earnings do not depend on the banking system's willingness or ability to lend.

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

|---|---|---|
| Net Charge-Off Rate, All Loans | The equity ratio shows the size of the capital cushion, while the charge-off rate shows the losses that are actually eating into it, so reading them together shows whether the buffer is large enough for the losses arriving. In Ziegel's terms, reserves absorb expected losses and capital absorbs unexpected ones, so charge-offs test the adequacy of the cushion. This combines the size of the buffer with the size of the drain on it. | When the cushion is strong AND charge-offs are low, the buffer comfortably exceeds the losses, which supports Financials and credit-sensitive sectors. | Net Interest Margin, US Banks | The equity ratio shows how thin the cushion is, while the net interest margin shows the spread revenue that must first cover losses and then rebuild capital, so reading them together shows whether a bank can earn its way out of a thin position. A wide margin can restore a thin cushion over time, while a squeezed margin cannot. This combines the level of capital with the ability to regenerate it. | When the cushion is adequate AND the margin is wide, banks can absorb losses and rebuild capital, which supports Financials. When the cushion is thin AND the margin is squeezed, banks cannot earn their way back to safety, which signals lasting pressure on Financials and favors defensives. |
| Net Percent of Banks Tightening C&I Standards | The equity ratio shows how much room a bank has to lend, while the tightening survey shows whether banks are actually choosing to lend, so reading them together shows both the capacity and the willingness to extend credit. This combines the constraint on lending with the behavior it produces. | When the cushion is adequate AND banks are loosening standards, credit is both possible and flowing, which supports Financials, Real Estate, and cyclicals. When the cushion is thinning AND banks are tightening, capital pressure is choking off credit, which confirms a credit crunch and favors defensive sectors. |

This combines an internal solvency read with a market-priced risk read. | When the cushion is strong AND the high-yield spread is narrow, both the banks and the market judge risk to be low, which supports Financials and cyclicals. ## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the level of equity to assets adequate for the actual riskiness of the assets it supports, since a ratio that looks safe against liquid, high-quality assets can be dangerously thin against illiquid or concentrated ones? Ziegel's point is that the same cushion means different things depending on asset quality, so the ratio must be read against the risk it stands behind.

2. Is the equity ratio being lifted by genuine capital building or by shrinking the asset base, since a rising ratio driven by retreating from lending signals a different condition than one driven by retained earnings? A cushion that grows because banks are pulling back is a warning, while one that grows from profits is a sign of health.

3. Are reserves for expected loan losses adequate and separate from the equity that must cover unexpected losses, since a bank that has under-reserved will see unexpected losses reach into capital sooner? The distinction between reserves and capital determines how quickly a loss event erodes the true cushion.

4. Is the banking system carrying dangerous concentrations relative to its capital, since Ziegel identifies concentration as the single most significant issue in credit risk management? A thin cushion is far more dangerous when exposure is concentrated in one industry, geography, or asset class that could go bad together.

5. Is the cushion thinning late in a boom, when easy credit and stretched leverage tend to build, or is it thin because the system is rebuilding after a shock? The same low reading carries opposite meaning depending on where the credit cycle stands.

6. Is the aggregate ratio masking wide differences between strong and weak banks, since a healthy average can hide individual institutions whose cushions are already stretched? The system can look adequately capitalized while the weakest banks are close to the edge.

7. Is the equity on the balance sheet high quality and truly loss-absorbing, or is it inflated by assets that are hard to value and could be marked down, since Ziegel notes that hard-to-value assets can wipe out capital when their value falls? Capital is only a real cushion if the assets behind the equity hold their stated value.

8. Are regulators tightening or loosening capital requirements, since Basel-style rules tie required capital to the risk of the assets and can force the ratio up independent of the banks' own choices? A rising ratio driven by regulation reflects a policy constraint rather than a change in underlying risk appetite.

9. Is the change in the ratio consistent with what loan losses, credit spreads, and lending surveys are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure is misleading? Confirmation across the credit indicators strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution.

10. Is the change in the cushion large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in banking-system safety, or is it a small quarterly fluctuation? The aggregate ratio moves slowly, so only a sustained and meaningful shift warrants a sector rotation.
