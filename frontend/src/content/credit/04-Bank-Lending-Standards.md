# Bank Lending Standards (Senior Loan Officer Survey)

## Part 1: Definition

Bank Lending Standards measures how willing banks are to make loans, reported as the net percentage of banks that are tightening their standards for commercial and industrial (C&I) loans. It comes from the Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS), in which loan officers at a large panel of banks are asked each quarter whether they have tightened, eased, or left unchanged the terms on which they extend credit. The FRED series id is DRTSCILM, reported quarterly, and it is expressed as a diffusion index in percentage points. A recent reading near 15 percent (approximate) means that the share of banks tightening exceeds the share easing by 15 percentage points. The metric reveals the willingness of the banking system to lend, which is the supply side of the credit cycle and one of the earliest signals that conditions are turning.

The figure is constructed by subtracting the percentage of banks that report easing from the percentage that report tightening, so a positive number means more banks are tightening than easing and a negative number means the opposite. A reading near zero means banks are broadly holding standards steady. During calm expansions the index often sits slightly negative or near zero, meaning banks are competing to lend. In a credit crunch it can spike toward 60 to 80 percent, as it did in 2008 and again in early 2020, meaning almost every bank is pulling back at once. The metric is included as a credit indicator because it captures a change in bank willingness to lend before that change shows up in loan volumes or defaults. Arnold Ziegel's account of the 2008 crisis is blunt on this point: very liberal lending standards provided the fuel for the housing bubble, and the losses arrived only later when the underwriting laxity was exposed. Because underwriting decisions are made before loans are booked and long before borrowers default, this survey tends to lead loan growth and delinquencies by several quarters. Watching the change in standards is therefore closer to watching the cause than watching the effect.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Bank Lending Standards matters for sector investing because a change in bank willingness to lend moves the whole credit cycle, and the credit cycle decides which sectors lead. When banks tighten, credit growth can fall even when borrowers still want to borrow, which drains the fuel from spending and investment and favors defensive sectors. When banks ease, credit flows freely, which supports cyclical sectors and above all Financials, which earn on the lending itself. The metric matters most for Financials, because the terms banks set today determine both the volume of loans they will book and the quality of those loans, and lax standards set at the top of a cycle are the seeds of the loan losses that arrive later. It matters heavily for Real Estate, because property is bought largely with borrowed money, so a tightening in standards raises financing costs and starves the sector of buyers. Ziegel's framing carries a specific warning here. Easy credit late in a boom is not a clean buy signal, because the very laxity that supports asset prices in the short run is what funds the assets that later default. A survey that shows banks easing aggressively into a mature expansion is therefore a mixed signal, supportive for now but a marker of building risk, while a sharp swing toward tightening is one of the most reliable early warnings that the cycle is about to turn down.

| Delinquency Rate, C&I Loans | Standards are the leading cause and delinquencies are the lagging effect, so pairing them traces the full arc from the underwriting decision to the eventual loss. When standards are tightening AND unemployment is rising, credit is being withdrawn just as borrowers weaken, which is the classic recession setup and argues for defensive positioning. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in standards driven by banks reassessing the riskiness of borrowers, or by banks managing their own capital and funding constraints? A change driven by borrower risk signals a shift in the credit cycle, while a change driven by bank capital signals stress inside the banking system itself, and the two carry different implications.

2. Are banks tightening the price of credit (spreads and fees) or the availability of credit (collateral and covenant terms), and which is moving first? Tighter terms on availability often bite harder than higher prices, because they cut weaker borrowers off entirely rather than simply charging them more.

3. How lax were standards during the preceding easy period, since Ziegel's lesson is that the laxity of the boom determines the size of the losses in the bust? A tightening cycle that follows a period of very liberal underwriting is more dangerous than one that follows disciplined lending.

4. Is the tightening concentrated in one loan category such as commercial real estate, or is it broad across C&I, consumer, and mortgage lending? Concentrated tightening points to a specific pocket of risk, while broad tightening signals a system-wide retreat closer to a credit crunch.

5. Are banks reporting weaker loan demand at the same time they tighten, or is demand still strong while supply pulls back? When banks tighten into still-strong demand, the supply constraint is binding and credit growth will fall for reasons unrelated to borrower appetite.

6. How many quarters of lead time does the current swing in standards imply before it shows up in loan growth and delinquencies? Because the survey leads volumes and losses by several quarters, the timing of the eventual effect matters for when to rotate between cyclical and defensive sectors.

7. Is the tightening a reaction to a specific shock, such as a banking scare or a policy change, or a gradual reassessment of a maturing cycle? A shock-driven spike can reverse quickly, while a gradual, sustained tightening is more likely to mark a genuine turn in the credit cycle.

8. Are non-bank lenders such as private credit funds filling the gap left by tightening banks, which would blunt the impact of the survey on actual credit availability? When credit migrates to the shadow banking system, the bank survey overstates the true squeeze on borrowers.

9. Does the survey agree with what credit spreads and loan growth are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure is misleading? Confirmation across the supply signal, the price signal, and the volume signal strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution.

10. Is the move in standards large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the credit cycle, or is it a modest quarter-to-quarter fluctuation? Only a large and sustained swing warrants a sector rotation, because a single quarter of tightening can reverse without a broader change in conditions.
