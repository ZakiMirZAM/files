# Commercial Real Estate Loan Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Commercial Real Estate Loan Growth measures how fast the stock of commercial real estate (CRE) loans on US bank balance sheets is expanding or shrinking. The base figure is the dollar amount of real estate loans secured by nonfarm nonresidential property, multifamily buildings, and construction and land development at all commercial banks, reported by the Federal Reserve as FRED series CREACBM027NBOG. The series is published monthly and stood near 3.0 trillion dollars in mid-2026 (an approximate, illustrative level). It can be viewed as a level (the dollars outstanding) or, more usefully for the credit cycle, as a year-over-year growth rate (the percentage change from twelve months earlier), which recently ran in the low single digits. The metric reveals how aggressively banks are funding offices, apartments, warehouses, shopping centers, and new construction, which is one of the clearest windows into where lending risk is building up inside the banking system.

The figure is constructed by totaling the CRE loans banks carry as assets and comparing that total to the level one year earlier. It belongs on a credit and banking dashboard because commercial real estate is where bank lending has repeatedly concentrated to the point of danger. Arnold Ziegel names concentration risk, too much exposure relative to capital to a single asset class, as perhaps the single most significant issue in credit risk management, and he points to CRE directly: too much exposure to commercial real estate loans relative to capital caused a number of US banking failures in the early 1990s. Real estate is a natural place for that concentration to form because the loans are large, the collateral looks tangible, and easy credit late in a boom funds the very buildings whose values are being bid up. Rapid CRE loan growth is therefore not automatically healthy. A fast climb builds the concentration Ziegel warns about, and because many CRE deals lean on the property value rather than reliable tenant cash flow, they carry the one way out weakness that turns a routine loan into a loss when values reverse. The rate of change and the loan quality matter far more than the level alone.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Commercial Real Estate Loan Growth matters for sector investing because it is a direct read on the health of two of the most credit-sensitive parts of the market, Financials and Real Estate, and an early warning on the kind of concentrated lending that has broken banks before. When CRE credit is growing at a steady, sustainable pace, property developers can build and refinance, landlords can carry their buildings, and banks earn on the lending, which supports Real Estate and the regional banks inside Financials that hold the bulk of this exposure. When CRE growth accelerates from an already high base, it signals easy credit late in the cycle, exactly the concentration Ziegel identifies as the cause of the early 1990s failures, so a fast rise is a caution flag rather than a green light. When CRE growth stalls or turns negative, it usually means banks are pulling back because property values, vacancy rates, or refinancing risk have deteriorated, which pressures Real Estate directly and threatens the banks most concentrated in the sector. Because CRE losses tend to eat straight through the thin capital that Ziegel describes banks holding against unexpected loss, this metric is a leading indicator for the regional-bank slice of Financials and a coincident read on Real Estate, and it helps position between credit-exposed cyclicals and defensives across the cycle.

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Real Estate | Commercial property is bought, built, and refinanced almost entirely with borrowed money, so the flow of CRE credit directly sets property values, transaction volume, and the ability of landlords and developers to carry their buildings. When CRE lending grows steadily the sector's assets are financeable and values are supported; when it contracts, refinancing dries up and values fall. This is the sector most directly tied to the metric. | Buy when CRE growth is moderate and steady with sound loan quality. Avoid when growth turns flat or negative, which signals a refinancing squeeze and falling values. |
| Financials (regional and community banks) | CRE loans are a large share of regional and community bank balance sheets, so this lending is a core earnings driver and also their biggest concentration risk. Steady growth adds interest income, but rapid growth from a high base builds the exposure that Ziegel says caused the early 1990s failures, and contraction brings losses that cut through thin capital. Earnings and even solvency at these banks move with the CRE cycle. | Buy when growth is moderate and concentration relative to capital is contained. Avoid when growth is rapid and concentrated late-cycle, or when it contracts and defaults rise. |
| Financials (large diversified banks) | Large banks hold CRE but spread it across many businesses and geographies, following the diversification Ziegel prescribes, so their earnings are less hostage to the CRE cycle than regional banks. They still take losses when CRE sours, but a diversified book absorbs them better. The connection is real but cushioned. | Buy when growth is moderate. Reduce exposure when contraction signals system-wide CRE losses, though diversified banks weather it better than concentrated ones. |
| Industrials | Commercial construction of offices, warehouses, and other structures drives demand for building products, engineering, and equipment, so growing CRE credit funds the projects that feed industrial order books. When CRE lending contracts, construction pipelines shrink and industrial demand weakens. The sector benefits from the building activity that CRE credit finances. | Buy when CRE growth is moderate to strong and construction pipelines are filling. Avoid when growth contracts and projects are shelved. |
| Materials | Cement, steel, glass, and other building materials are consumed by the commercial construction that CRE loans finance, so materials demand follows the CRE building cycle. Growing credit supports construction volumes; a credit crunch halts projects and cuts demand. The link runs through construction activity. | Buy when CRE growth is strong and construction is active. Avoid when it contracts. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Retail landlords, hotels, and shopping-center owners depend on CRE financing, and the sector's property-linked components suffer when CRE credit tightens. The broader connection is weaker than for Real Estate, but retail and leisure property exposure ties part of the sector to CRE conditions. The effect is partial and indirect. | Modest sensitivity through retail and hospitality property. Trim property-heavy names when CRE contracts. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples demand comes from everyday needs and is unrelated to the property credit cycle, so the sector gains relative appeal when contracting CRE credit signals broader banking stress. Its defensive characteristics dominate when CRE-driven activity weakens. It becomes a destination when CRE credit slows or contracts. | Buy when CRE growth is slowing or contracting. Underperforms when healthy CRE growth supports cyclical leadership. |
| Health Care | Health care demand is driven by medical need rather than property lending, so the sector behaves defensively when CRE stress signals wider weakness. Its earnings stability becomes valuable when a CRE downturn threatens the banks. The sector is insulated from the CRE channel. | Buy when CRE growth is slowing or contracting. Defensive default in a CRE-led banking slowdown. |
| Utilities | Utility demand is stable and independent of commercial property lending, so the sector holds up defensively when CRE credit signals a slowdown. Its own heavy borrowing means broad credit stress can raise its financing costs, but its cash flows do not depend on the property cycle. The sector is a refuge when CRE contracts. | Buy when CRE growth is slowing. Avoid when healthy CRE growth drives cyclical leadership. |
| Energy | Energy demand is driven by economic activity and commodity prices rather than commercial property lending, so the connection to CRE credit is faint and runs only through the broader economy. CRE growth is a secondary consideration for the sector. | Low direct sensitivity. Follows energy demand and prices more than CRE lending. |
| Information Technology | Large tech companies fund themselves from cash and capital markets rather than bank property loans, so the sector is largely insulated from the CRE channel, though the broad economic health that CRE conditions reflect matters indirectly. The connection is weak. | Low direct sensitivity. Strong balance sheets reduce reliance on the CRE cycle. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside Commercial Real Estate Loan Growth | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Delinquency Rate, All Bank Loans | CRE loan growth shows how fast the exposure is building, while the delinquency rate shows whether the loans already made are being paid, so reading them together separates a healthy expansion from concentration risk that is turning into losses. The two together reveal both the size and the quality of the CRE book. This joins the quantity of lending to its performance. | When CRE growth is moderate AND delinquencies are low, the property book is expanding soundly, which supports Real Estate and the regional banks. When CRE growth has been rapid AND delinquencies are now rising, the late-cycle concentration Ziegel warns about is souring, which confirms a defensive rotation away from Real Estate and CRE-heavy banks. |
| Net % of Banks Tightening Standards (SLOOS) | CRE growth measures the credit that actually flowed, while the SLOOS tightening survey measures banks' stated willingness to lend, so reading them together shows whether a slowdown in CRE credit is coming from banks pulling back or from weaker borrower demand. The survey often leads the loan data. This connects lender willingness to realized lending. | When CRE growth is steady AND banks are not tightening, the expansion has room to run, supporting Real Estate and Financials. When banks are sharply tightening CRE standards AND growth is still positive, the survey is warning that a CRE contraction is coming, giving an early signal to rotate out of property-exposed sectors before the loan data confirms it. |
| Bank Equity to Total Assets | CRE growth shows how concentrated the exposure is becoming, while bank equity to total assets shows how much capital stands behind it, so reading them together measures the concentration risk exactly as Ziegel frames it, exposure relative to capital. The two together show whether banks can absorb a CRE downturn. This ties the risk buildup to the capital that must cover unexpected loss. | When CRE growth is contained AND bank capital is ample, the system can absorb property losses, which limits the damage to Financials. When CRE growth has been rapid AND bank capital is thin, the concentration is dangerous and a downturn could cut through capital, which argues for avoiding CRE-concentrated banks and favoring defensives. |
| High-Yield Credit Spread | CRE growth reflects the quantity of property lending, while the high-yield spread reflects the market price of credit risk across the economy, so reading them together shows whether CRE stress is isolated or part of a broader repricing of credit. The two together place the property cycle inside the wider credit cycle. This combines a sector-specific quantity with an economy-wide price. | When CRE growth is healthy AND high-yield spreads are narrow, credit is abundant and cheap, which supports Real Estate and cyclicals. When CRE growth is contracting AND high-yield spreads are widening, property stress is coinciding with a broad credit crunch, which strongly favors a defensive rotation. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is CRE loan growth concentrated in a few banks or property types, or spread broadly across the system? Concentration relative to capital is the single most significant issue in credit risk management, so growth piled into a narrow set of exposed banks is far more dangerous than the same growth spread widely.

2. Is the growth funding productive property with real tenant demand, or speculative building and refinancing that leans on rising values alone? Loans repaid only from an assumed rise in property value are one way out lending, which turns into losses the moment values reverse.

3. How much CRE exposure sits against each bank's capital, since it is the ratio of exposure to capital, not the dollar total, that determines whether a downturn is survivable? CRE losses that exceed the capital held against unexpected loss are what caused the early 1990s bank failures Ziegel cites.

4. Is the growth rate rising from an already high base, which signals late-cycle excess, or expanding from a normal level in line with the economy? A rapid climb from a high base is the classic concentration buildup that precedes CRE-driven bank stress.

5. Which property segments are driving the change, office, multifamily, retail, industrial, or construction, and are any of them facing structural rather than cyclical decline? A segment in secular decline, such as office space after a shift to remote work, carries permanent impairment risk that a cyclical read would miss.

6. Are banks tightening or loosening CRE underwriting standards, and how is that changing growth independent of borrower demand? When banks tighten, CRE growth can fall even when demand exists, which signals deteriorating conditions and often leads the loss data.

7. Is refinancing risk building, given that many CRE loans are repaid by refinancing rather than from operating cash flow? If loans cannot be refinanced when they mature because values or rates have moved against the borrower, the lender becomes a long term lender to a project that cannot pay, which is how the one way out weakness surfaces.

8. Is CRE lending shifting from banks toward non-bank and private-credit lenders, which would make the bank series understate the true level of property lending? When credit migrates to the shadow lending system, the bank data alone misses part of the risk building in commercial real estate.

9. Is CRE loan performance consistent with what delinquency rates, credit spreads, and the SLOOS survey are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one signal is misleading? Confirmation across credit indicators strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution before rotating sectors.

10. Is the change in CRE growth large and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in the property credit cycle, or a short-term fluctuation from temporary factors such as a single quarter of maturities? Only a sustained, meaningful shift warrants repositioning between Real Estate, regional banks, and defensives, since CRE flows can move for temporary reasons.
