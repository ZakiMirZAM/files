# Reserves of Depository Institutions

## Part 1: Definition

Reserves of Depository Institutions measures the cash that banks hold on deposit at the Federal Reserve, reported in billions of dollars. It is the pool of ready money that sits at the center of the banking system, the balances banks keep at the central bank to settle payments with one another and to meet the demands of depositors who want their money. The FRED series is TOTRESNS, published monthly and not seasonally adjusted, and it has run near 3,300 billion dollars, or about 3.3 trillion dollars, in recent months (an approximate figure used here for illustration). The level rose sharply when the Federal Reserve expanded its balance sheet and has drained as the Fed has let that balance sheet shrink. The metric reveals how much liquidity is available in the banking system's plumbing, which is the immediate fuel banks draw on when they need cash quickly.

The figure is constructed by adding up the reserve balances that all depository institutions hold at the Federal Reserve. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because liquidity, not just solvency, is what keeps a bank alive from day to day. Ziegel makes this the dominant risk in banking. He states that companies, and banks in particular, go bankrupt for one of three reasons: they run out of cash, they fear they will run out of cash, or their suppliers of liquidity fear they will run out of cash. Reserves are the raw cash that stands against the first of those reasons and reassures against the other two. Ziegel drives the point home with the collapses of Bear Stearns and Lehman, where continued market confidence, trust, and liquidity were critical to survival even for large and highly rated firms, and where the suppliers of short-term funding took their money and ran the moment they doubted the quality of the assets behind the leverage. He also draws the deeper lesson that liquid assets can suddenly become illiquid, which was at the heart of the 2008 crisis, so a system that looks well funded can seize up when the cash in its plumbing runs low. Draining reserves tightens that plumbing, and when reserves fall toward scarcity the cost of overnight funding can spike and confidence can crack, which is why the level of system reserves is the liquidity dimension of the confidence risk that Ziegel places at the center of banking.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Reserves of Depository Institutions matters for sector investing because system liquidity is the plumbing that keeps credit flowing, and when that plumbing tightens the whole credit cycle is at risk of seizing. Financials are affected most, because banks live and die on their access to cash, and abundant reserves let them fund lending and meet withdrawals with ease while draining reserves force them to compete for scarce funding and grow cautious. Real Estate is affected next, because property depends on a steady flow of credit that only a well-funded banking system can provide, and a liquidity squeeze pulls that credit back. The metric captures Ziegel's insight that the most dangerous bank risk is not a slow erosion of earnings but a sudden loss of confidence among the suppliers of liquidity, the moment when they fear a bank will run out of cash and stop lending to it. Abundant reserves make that fear unlikely and support a risk-on environment led by Financials and credit-sensitive cyclicals. Draining reserves toward scarcity raise the odds of a funding scare like the money-market disruptions that have punctuated recent tightening cycles, which argues for rotating toward defensive sectors that do not depend on the smooth functioning of the banking system's plumbing. Because a solvency problem can be survived but a liquidity problem can kill a bank in days, the level and direction of reserves is a fast-moving signal worth watching closely.

This combines the supply of liquidity with the market's reaction to it. | When reserves are ample AND the stress index is below zero, liquidity is abundant and markets are calm, which supports Financials and cyclicals. This combines a liquidity read with a market-priced risk read. | When reserves are ample AND the spread is narrow, both liquidity and risk pricing are benign, which supports Financials and cyclicals. When reserves are draining AND the spread is widening, a liquidity squeeze is turning into repriced credit risk, which strengthens the case for a defensive rotation. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Are reserves draining toward the level where funding markets have shown stress before, since the danger is not the absolute level but the point where the plumbing begins to seize? Past money-market disruptions have shown that stress can appear well before reserves reach zero, so the threshold matters more than the level.

2. Is the fall in reserves a deliberate policy of shrinking the central bank balance sheet or a sign of banks hoarding cash out of caution, since the two carry very different implications? A managed drawdown is orderly, while hoarding reflects the fear among suppliers of liquidity that Ziegel identifies as a warning sign.

3. Are reserves distributed evenly across the banking system or concentrated at a few large banks, since an adequate aggregate can hide smaller banks that are already short of cash? The system can look well funded while the weakest institutions face a squeeze.

4. Is the level of reserves consistent with the demand for cash that the banking system actually needs, given that required and desired reserve levels shift with regulation and bank behavior? The same dollar figure can be comfortable in one regime and tight in another.

5. Is a liquidity squeeze in the plumbing at risk of turning market risk into credit risk, since Ziegel notes that liquid assets can suddenly become illiquid when funding dries up? A funding scare can force banks to hold assets they meant to sell, converting a liquidity problem into a credit problem.

6. Are the suppliers of short-term funding showing signs of pulling back, since Ziegel's most dangerous bank risk is the moment those suppliers fear a bank will run out of cash? Watching the behavior of wholesale funding markets gives an early read on confidence.

7. Is the banking system's liquidity being tested by a solvency worry elsewhere, since a solvency problem can be survived but a liquidity problem can kill a bank in days once confidence cracks? The interaction between a capital scare and a funding scare is what turns problems into failures.

8. Is the drain in reserves being offset by other sources of bank funding, or is it a genuine tightening of overall liquidity? Banks can substitute other funding for reserves up to a point, so the broader funding picture matters.

9. Is the change in reserves consistent with what the stress index, deposit growth, and credit spreads are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure is misleading? Confirmation across the liquidity indicators strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution.

10. Is the change in reserves large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine tightening of system liquidity, or is it a short-term fluctuation driven by seasonal or technical factors? Because the series is not seasonally adjusted, only a sustained and meaningful shift warrants a sector rotation.
