# Delinquency Rate, Commercial and Industrial Loans

## Part 1: Definition

The Delinquency Rate on Commercial and Industrial Loans measures the share of business loans held by US commercial banks on which the corporate borrower has fallen behind on payments, expressed as a percentage of the total C&I loan book. Commercial and industrial loans are the credit banks extend to companies for working capital, inventory, equipment, and expansion, so this series isolates corporate credit stress from the household and property lending captured by the all-loans rate. A loan is counted as delinquent once it is past due, generally thirty days or more. The FRED series is DRBLACBS, published quarterly by the Federal Reserve, and it typically sits near 1 percent in a healthy economy (an approximate recent reading), rising toward 3 to 4 percent in a downturn. The rate reveals how many companies have begun to miss payments, which is the earliest sign that corporate operating cash flow is failing.

The figure is constructed by dividing the dollar value of past-due C&I loans by the total dollar value of C&I loans on bank balance sheets, then aggregating across every reporting bank. It captures the business-lending slice of the loan book specifically, which is why it moves with the corporate credit cycle rather than the consumer or housing cycle. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because a company that misses a loan payment is showing, in Ziegel's terms, that its first way out, repayment from operating cash flow, has begun to fail. Ziegel emphasizes that assessing a company is harder than assessing a consumer because the corporation does not receive a paycheck every week, so the analyst must judge the size and volatility of the company's cash flow. A rising C&I delinquency rate is the aggregate, after-the-fact confirmation that corporate cash flow has turned volatile and insufficient across a broad set of borrowers. Because bank lending to companies competes with the public bond market, the C&I delinquency rate is best read as the realized, balance-sheet counterpart to the market-priced signal in the high-yield spread.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The C&I delinquency rate matters for sector investing because it is a direct read on corporate credit health, and corporate credit stress is one of the clearest markers of where the business cycle stands. When C&I delinquencies are low, companies are servicing their debt from operating cash flow, banks lend freely to business, and cyclical sectors driven by corporate investment can lead. When they begin to rise, corporate cash flow is faltering, and the pressure lands first on Financials, because business lending is a core bank activity and rising delinquencies foreshadow provisions and charge-offs, and on Industrials and Materials, whose demand depends on the very corporate investment that a healthy business-credit environment funds. The metric carries a specific late-cycle warning drawn from Ziegel: late in a boom, banks and investors extend easy credit to highly leveraged companies regardless of their operating risk, and those are the borrowers that go delinquent first when conditions tighten. Reading the C&I rate alongside the high-yield spread is especially powerful because the spread reflects what the market fears about corporate default while the delinquency rate reflects what has actually begun to happen on bank balance sheets. When the two agree, the signal is strong. When they diverge, one is either early or complacent. Because it isolates corporate distress and pairs naturally with market-priced risk, the C&I delinquency rate is a valuable input for timing rotations between cyclical and defensive sectors.

The absence of a rising trend is the reassuring signal. | Financials, Industrials, Materials, Consumer Discretionary | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Energy, Communication Services |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the rise in C&I delinquencies broad across industries, or concentrated in a few sectors such as energy or retail where specific shocks are at work? A broad rise signals a general corporate credit turn, while a concentrated rise may reflect an industry-specific stress that stays contained.

2. Are the companies going delinquent the highly leveraged borrowers that banks and investors financed with easy credit late in the boom, regardless of their operating risk? Ziegel warns that this late-cycle lending to leveraged companies is exactly what fails first, so delinquencies traced to that lending are likely to widen.

3. Is corporate cash flow, the first way out, genuinely deteriorating, or are delinquencies rising because interest rates have risen and squeezed otherwise-healthy borrowers? A cash-flow failure signals a durable credit turn, while a rate-driven squeeze on solvent companies can ease when rates fall.

4. How does the C&I delinquency rate compare with what the high-yield spread is signaling, and does agreement or divergence between them strengthen or weaken the read on corporate credit? When the realized bank measure and the market-priced measure agree, the corporate credit signal is far more reliable.

5. Are banks tightening C&I lending standards in response to rising delinquencies, and is that tightening likely to accelerate the corporate downturn by cutting off refinancing? When banks pull back, companies that relied on rolling their debt can be pushed from delinquency into default.

6. Is business borrowing shifting from banks toward the public bond market or private credit funds, so that the C&I delinquency series understates the true level of corporate distress? When corporate credit migrates outside banks, the C&I series alone can miss part of the deterioration.

7. Are the delinquent loans concentrated in a few banks relative to their capital, creating the concentration risk that Ziegel identifies as the usual cause of bank failure? Corporate losses bunched in a few lenders are far more dangerous than the same losses spread across a diversified banking system.

8. Do the delinquent loans depend on cash flow for repayment, or were they one-way-out structures such as loans made against an expected asset sale or market takeout? Ziegel notes that a loan whose only exit is a public security sale relies on the greater-fool theory, and such loans can produce severe losses when the exit closes.

9. Is the rise in C&I delinquencies accompanied by falling corporate profits and rising layoffs, which would confirm that the corporate stress is feeding through to the labor market and household spending? A corporate credit turn that reaches employment is more likely to broaden into a general downturn favoring defensives.

10. Is the change in the C&I delinquency rate large enough and sustained enough to signal a genuine turn in corporate credit, or is it a one- or two-quarter fluctuation driven by a temporary factor? Only a sustained and meaningful rise warrants a defensive rotation, since the business-lending series can move quarter to quarter for reasons that reverse.
