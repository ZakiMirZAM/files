# Bank Deposit Growth

## Part 1: Definition

Bank Deposit Growth measures how fast the deposits held at US commercial banks are expanding or shrinking. The base figure is the total dollar amount of deposits at all commercial banks, reported by the Federal Reserve as FRED series DPSACBW027SBOG on a seasonally adjusted basis. The series is published weekly and stood near 17.8 trillion dollars in mid-2026 (an approximate, illustrative level). It can be viewed as a level (the dollars on deposit) or, more usefully for the credit cycle, as a year-over-year growth rate (the percentage change from twelve months earlier), which recently ran in the low single digits after briefly turning negative during the deposit outflows of 2023. The metric reveals the size and stability of the funding base that banks lend against, which is the foundation of the entire banking system and one of the clearest reads on confidence in banks.

The figure is constructed by totaling checking, savings, and time deposits across commercial banks and comparing that total to the level one year earlier. It belongs on a credit and banking dashboard because deposits are the stable, low-cost funding that lets banks make loans, and their stability rests entirely on confidence. Arnold Ziegel teaches that companies, and banks above all, fail for one of three reasons: they run out of cash, they fear they will run out of cash, or their suppliers of liquidity fear they will run out of cash. Deposits are a bank's primary supply of liquidity, so a falling deposit base is the mechanism through which that fear becomes fatal. Ziegel points to the demise of Bear Stearns and Northern Rock as cases where continued market confidence, trust, and liquidity were critical to survival, and where the loss of that confidence, not a slow erosion of loan quality, brought the institution down almost overnight. A bank run is exactly the fear-of-running-out-of-cash channel: once depositors and short-term funders lose confidence, they take their money and run for safety, and even a large, well-regarded bank can fail in days. Deposit growth is therefore read as a confidence and liquidity indicator, and the rate of change, especially a sharp decline, matters far more than the level alone.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

Bank Deposit Growth matters for sector investing because deposits are the funding base of the banking system, so this series is a direct read on the health and confidence of Financials and, through the credit banks can extend, an indirect read on every credit-sensitive sector. When deposits are growing steadily, banks have ample low-cost funding to lend, which supports net interest margins across Financials and keeps credit flowing to Real Estate, Consumer Discretionary, and other borrowers. When deposit growth stalls, banks must compete for funding by raising rates or shrink their lending, which compresses margins and slows credit to the wider economy. When deposits fall outright, it signals the loss of confidence that Ziegel identifies as the dominant risk for banks, the fear-of-running-out-of-cash channel that felled Northern Rock and Bear Stearns and reappeared in the 2023 regional-bank failures. Because a deposit run can destroy a bank in days regardless of the quality of its loan book, this metric is the single most important liquidity indicator for Financials, especially the regional banks most dependent on deposit funding. A sharp fall pushes the market toward the largest, most trusted banks and toward defensive sectors, while steady growth supports the credit expansion that favors cyclicals. Read this way, the series helps position around the confidence and liquidity risk that sits at the center of banking.

## Part 3: What it tells you

| Reading | What it means | Sectors Favored | Sectors Not Favored | Sectors Not Affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong growth (above 6% year over year) | Deposits are flowing into banks rapidly, giving them abundant low-cost funding to lend. This appears when confidence is high, when the Fed is easing and money is plentiful, or when households and businesses hold large cash balances. Banks can expand lending cheaply, net interest margins are supported, and credit flows freely to the wider economy. Very rapid growth can also reflect a flight to the safety of deposits during stress, so the driver must be checked. | Financials, Real Estate, Consumer Discretionary, Industrials | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Energy, Materials, Information Technology, Communication Services |
| Moderate growth (2 to 6% year over year) | The deposit base is expanding at a steady pace that reflects normal confidence and a stable funding environment. Banks have reliable, low-cost funding to support lending, margins are steady, and credit flows to borrowers at a manageable rate. This is the healthy middle of the cycle where funding stability underpins the credit expansion. Financials and credit-sensitive sectors benefit without the warning signs of a funding squeeze. | Financials, Real Estate, Industrials | Consumer Staples, Utilities | Health Care, Energy, Materials, Information Technology, Communication Services |
| Slowing toward flat (near zero growth) | Deposit growth is stalling as savers move cash to higher-yielding alternatives or as confidence softens. This often appears when short-term rates rise and money funds or Treasuries out-compete deposits, forcing banks to pay up for funding or curb lending. Net interest margins compress, credit to the wider economy slows, and banks most dependent on deposit funding come under pressure. It can be an early sign of funding strain in the banking system. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Financials, Real Estate | Industrials, Materials, Energy, Information Technology, Communication Services |
| Contracting (negative year over year) | The deposit base is shrinking as depositors and short-term funders pull money out, which is the fear-of-running-out-of-cash channel Ziegel describes. This appears in funding stress and bank runs, when confidence breaks and liquidity flees to safety, as it did at Northern Rock, Bear Stearns, and the 2023 regional-bank failures. Banks forced to sell assets or shrink lending face losses and, in the extreme, failure regardless of loan quality. Credit contracts sharply and the market flees to the largest banks and to defensive sectors. | Consumer Staples, Health Care, Utilities | Financials, Real Estate, Consumer Discretionary, Industrials | Energy, Materials, Information Technology, Communication Services |

## Part 4: Finding the best sectors to invest in

| Sector or sector group | Why this metric directly affects this sector's earnings | The specific reading that signals a buy versus avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Financials (regional and community banks) | Deposits are the primary, low-cost funding base for regional banks, so deposit growth directly sets their funding cost, their lending capacity, and their net interest margin. A falling deposit base is the liquidity failure Ziegel identifies as the dominant bank risk, capable of destroying a bank in days regardless of loan quality, as the 2023 regional failures showed. Earnings and survival move with deposit stability. | Buy when deposit growth is moderate to strong and funding is stable. Avoid when deposits are falling or fleeing to larger banks, which signals a liquidity squeeze. |
| Financials (large diversified banks) | Large, trusted banks fund themselves from deposits plus diversified wholesale sources and often gain deposits in a flight to safety, so they are more resilient than regional banks when confidence wavers. Their margins still depend on deposit costs, but their scale and perceived safety cushion them. They can even benefit from deposit outflows elsewhere. | Buy when deposit growth is steady. In a deposit run, favor the largest banks as safe-haven beneficiaries over vulnerable regionals. |
| Real Estate | Property depends on the credit that banks fund with deposits, so a stable, growing deposit base keeps mortgage and CRE lending flowing, while a deposit squeeze forces banks to curb property lending and raise financing costs. The link runs from bank funding to the availability of property credit. The sector benefits from stable bank funding. | Buy when deposit growth is steady and credit is flowing. Avoid when deposits contract and banks pull back on property lending. |
| Consumer Discretionary | Discretionary spending relies on the consumer credit that banks extend from their deposit base, so stable deposit growth supports the lending that funds household purchases, while a funding squeeze tightens consumer credit. The connection runs through the credit channel rather than directly. The sector benefits from a healthy banking funding base. | Buy when deposit growth supports steady credit. Avoid when a deposit contraction tightens consumer lending. |
| Industrials | Business investment depends on bank credit funded by deposits, so a stable deposit base supports the lending that finances capital spending, while a funding squeeze slows it. The link is indirect, running through the availability of business credit. The sector benefits from stable bank funding. | Buy when deposit growth supports steady business lending. Avoid when a deposit squeeze curbs credit. |
| Consumer Staples | Staples demand comes from everyday needs and is unrelated to bank funding conditions, so the sector gains relative appeal when contracting deposits signal banking stress. Its defensive characteristics dominate when a funding squeeze threatens the banks. It becomes a destination when deposits fall and confidence breaks. | Buy when deposit growth is slowing or contracting. Underperforms when strong deposit-funded credit growth supports cyclical leadership. |
| Health Care | Health care demand is driven by medical need rather than bank funding, so the sector behaves defensively when a deposit run signals banking stress. Its earnings stability becomes valuable when confidence in banks wavers. The sector is insulated from the funding channel. | Buy when deposit growth is slowing or contracting. Defensive default in a banking liquidity crisis. |
| Utilities | Utility demand is stable and independent of bank funding, so the sector holds up defensively when deposit stress signals a slowdown. Its own heavy borrowing means a broad funding squeeze can raise its financing costs, but its cash flows do not depend on bank deposits. The sector is a refuge when deposits contract. | Buy when deposit growth is slowing. Avoid when strong deposit-funded credit growth drives cyclical leadership. |
| Energy | Energy demand is driven by economic activity and prices rather than bank funding, so the connection to deposits is faint and runs only through the broader economy. Deposit growth is a secondary consideration for the sector. | Low direct sensitivity. Follows energy demand and prices more than bank funding. |
| Information Technology | Large tech companies fund themselves from cash and capital markets rather than bank credit, so the sector is largely insulated from the deposit-funding channel, though the broad economic and financial-system health that deposits reflect matters indirectly. The connection is weak. | Low direct sensitivity. Strong balance sheets reduce reliance on bank funding. |

## Part 5: Metrics to analyze alongside it

| Paired metric | Why this metric should be read alongside Bank Deposit Growth | How combining the two metrics sharpens the answer to which sector is the best investment |
|---|---|---|
| Net Interest Margin, US Banks | Deposit growth shows the size and stability of the funding base, while net interest margin shows how profitably banks turn that funding into loans, so reading them together shows whether banks are funded cheaply and lending profitably or being squeezed on both sides. The two together describe the core economics of banking. This joins the funding base to its profitability. | When deposit growth is steady AND net interest margin is healthy, banks are funded cheaply and lending profitably, which strongly supports Financials. When deposits are contracting AND margins are compressing because banks must pay up for funding, the funding squeeze is hitting profitability, which argues for avoiding deposit-dependent regional banks. |
| Bank Equity to Total Assets | Deposit growth shows whether the funding base is stable, while bank equity to total assets shows how much capital stands behind the bank, so reading them together shows whether a bank can withstand a liquidity shock. Ziegel notes reserves cover expected loss while capital covers unexpected loss, and a deposit run is the classic unexpected shock. The two together measure resilience to a funding crisis. | When deposit growth is stable AND capital is ample, the bank is resilient to a shock, which supports Financials. When deposits are falling AND capital is thin, the bank is fragile to a run, which argues for favoring the largest, best-capitalized banks and defensives. |
| M2 Money Supply | Deposits are the largest component of the money supply, so deposit growth and M2 growth move closely together, and reading them together shows whether the banking funding base is expanding alongside the broader money stock or diverging from it. The two together describe the flow of liquidity through the system. This connects the bank funding base to the wider money stock. | When deposit growth is steady AND M2 is expanding, liquidity is ample and credit can flow, which supports cyclical sectors. When deposits are contracting AND M2 is flat or falling, liquidity is draining from the system, which confirms a tightening environment and favors defensives. |
| High-Yield Credit Spread | Deposit growth is a read on funding-side confidence in banks, while the high-yield spread is a read on market-wide credit fear, so reading them together shows whether stress is confined to bank funding or spreading across all credit. The two together place the banking system inside the wider credit cycle. This combines a funding-confidence signal with an economy-wide price of risk. | When deposit growth is stable AND high-yield spreads are narrow, both bank funding and market credit are calm, which supports Financials and cyclicals. When deposits are contracting AND high-yield spreads are widening, a banking funding squeeze is coinciding with broad credit fear, which strongly favors a defensive rotation and the safest banks. |

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Is the change in deposits driven by confidence in banks, or by savers chasing higher yields in money funds and Treasuries? A yield-driven outflow is a margin problem, while a confidence-driven outflow is the fear-of-running-out-of-cash channel that can destroy a bank quickly.

2. Are the deposit outflows spread broadly across the system, or concentrated in a few vulnerable banks while the largest banks gain? A flight of deposits from regionals to the largest banks signals a loss of confidence in specific institutions, which is exactly how Northern Rock and the 2023 regional failures unfolded.

3. How stable are the deposits themselves, given that uninsured, concentrated, and rate-sensitive deposits can flee far faster than small insured balances? A bank funded by flighty deposits is far more exposed to a run than one funded by sticky, insured retail balances, even at the same growth rate.

4. Is the deposit base falling fast enough to force banks to sell assets at a loss to meet withdrawals, turning a liquidity problem into a solvency problem? When a bank must dump assets into a weak market to raise cash, the fear of running out of cash becomes a self-fulfilling failure, as it did at Bear Stearns.

5. Do banks have enough capital and liquid assets to absorb a deposit shock, since reserves cover expected loss but capital and liquidity cover the unexpected run? A thinly capitalized, illiquid bank can fail on a funding shock regardless of the quality of its loan book.

6. Is the deposit change consistent with what the money supply and reserves are showing, or is there a divergence that suggests one measure is misleading? Confirmation across liquidity indicators strengthens the read, while divergence warrants caution before repositioning.

7. Are rising interest rates pulling deposits out of banks and into higher-yielding alternatives, and how much is that compressing net interest margins? Rate-driven disintermediation can squeeze bank profitability even without a crisis of confidence, which affects interpretation.

8. Is the deposit outflow being replaced by more expensive and less stable wholesale funding, which raises costs and increases fragility? A bank that plugs a deposit gap with hot wholesale money is more vulnerable to the next shock, so the composition of funding matters as much as the total.

9. Is the loss of deposits at any institution a symptom of a deeper problem such as loan losses or a broken business model, or purely a funding event? Ziegel notes that short-term funders are always vulnerable to a borrower's long-term prospects, so a run often signals underlying weakness that the deposit number alone does not show.

10. Is the change in deposit growth large and sustained enough to signal a genuine shift in banking-system confidence, or a short-term fluctuation from seasonal or temporary factors? Only a sustained, meaningful move warrants rotating around Financials and toward defensives, since deposit flows can swing for temporary reasons.
