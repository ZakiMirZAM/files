# Net Charge-Off Rate, All Loans

## Part 1: Definition

The Net Charge-Off Rate on All Loans measures the value of loans that US commercial banks have written off as uncollectible during a period, net of any amounts later recovered, expressed as a percentage of the total loan book on an annualized basis. A charge-off is the accounting act of removing a loan from the balance sheet once the bank concludes it will not be repaid, so this is the point at which a credit problem stops being a warning and becomes a realized loss. The FRED series is CORALACBN, published quarterly by the Federal Reserve, and it typically sits near 0.4 to 0.6 percent in a healthy economy (an approximate recent reading), rising toward 2.5 to 3 percent in a severe recession as it did after 2008. The rate reveals the actual losses banks are absorbing, which in credit terms is the empirical loss given default across the whole loan portfolio.

The figure is constructed by taking gross charge-offs for the period, subtracting recoveries on loans previously written off, and dividing the net figure by average loans outstanding, then annualizing and aggregating across every reporting bank. Because it counts only losses that have actually been recognized, it lags the delinquency rate: a loan first goes past due, then deteriorates further, and only later is charged off. It is included as a credit and banking indicator because it is the cleanest measure of realized credit losses in the banking system, and Ziegel's framework draws a sharp line that this metric sits on. Reserves are built to cover expected loss, and the charge-off rate is the stream of losses those reserves are meant to absorb. Capital, by contrast, exists to cover unexpected loss, so a charge-off spike that runs beyond what reserves were set aside to cover eats directly into bank capital. This is the Basel distinction between expected and unexpected loss made visible in a single series, and it is why a sudden surge in charge-offs is one of the most dangerous signals in banking.

## Part 2: Why this metric matters in finding the best sectors

The net charge-off rate matters for sector investing because it is the definitive measure of how much credit damage has actually been done, and the depth of realized losses drives which sectors can lead and which are impaired. When charge-offs are low, banks are keeping losses within the buffer their reserves already cover, earnings are protected, and credit-sensitive cyclicals can lead. When charge-offs spike, losses can outrun reserves and begin to consume capital, which forces banks to raise equity, cut lending, and rebuild buffers, and this lands first and hardest on Financials, whose earnings and capital are directly reduced by every dollar charged off. It lands next on Real Estate, because property lending is a large share of the loan book and property losses have historically driven the worst charge-off waves, including the commercial real estate losses that felled US banks in the early 1990s and the mortgage losses of 2008. The metric also captures Ziegel's insight about concentration risk: banks fail not from ordinary losses but when losses concentrated in one asset class run through their capital. A charge-off surge is the moment that concentration risk becomes solvency risk. Because it confirms the severity of a downturn and identifies which lenders are most impaired, the charge-off rate is a decisive input for positioning between damaged financial and cyclical sectors and resilient defensive sectors.

## Ten Precise Qualitative Questions

1. Are rising charge-offs still within the reserves banks set aside for expected loss, or are they large enough to run through into capital, the buffer meant only for unexpected loss? This distinction, central to Ziegel's Basel framework, separates a manageable earnings hit from a genuine solvency threat.

2. Are the charge-offs concentrated in one loan category or one region, and does that concentration threaten specific banks disproportionately? Ziegel stresses that banks fail from concentration risk, so losses bunched in one asset class relative to a bank's capital are far more dangerous than the same losses spread across a diversified book.

3. Is the charge-off rate rising because delinquent loans are finally being written off, or because banks are proactively cleaning their books ahead of an expected downturn? Write-offs of already-known bad loans confirm past deterioration, while pre-emptive charge-offs can signal that banks expect conditions to worsen.

4. How does the current charge-off rate compare with the reserves banks have already built, and are provisions rising fast enough to stay ahead of losses? A charge-off rate that outpaces reserve building forces banks to provision aggressively, which cuts earnings even before capital is touched.

5. Is the rise in charge-offs tracing back to loans underwritten with loose standards late in the prior boom, confirming that easy credit extended at the top of the cycle produces the losses at the bottom? If the losses come from the most recent loose vintage, more losses are likely to follow as that vintage seasons.

6. Are recoveries on previously charged-off loans holding up, or are net charge-offs rising faster than gross because collateral values have collapsed? Weak recoveries mean loss given default is high, which is the sign of a severe cycle where even secured lending is impaired.

7. Is the charge-off rate consistent with what the delinquency rate and credit spreads signaled earlier, or has the realized loss come in worse than the leading indicators implied? Losses that exceed what delinquencies foreshadowed suggest the deterioration is deeper and more dangerous than the early warnings priced in.

8. Are the loans being charged off ones that had only one way out, such as collateral-dependent lending where repayment never rested on borrower cash flow? Ziegel's point that one-way-out loans are the downfall of bankers means charge-offs concentrated in such loans can be severe because there was never a second source of repayment.

9. Is the charge-off cycle broad across the banking system or concentrated in a few weak lenders, and does that distinction change which financial names are investable? A system-wide loss cycle impairs the whole sector, while losses concentrated in a few institutions can leave well-capitalized banks as relative winners.

10. Has the charge-off rate peaked, and does a peak signal the point of maximum pessimism from which impaired financial valuations can recover? Because charge-offs lag the cycle, their peak often coincides with the bottom for bank earnings, so the turn can mark an opportunity rather than a warning.
